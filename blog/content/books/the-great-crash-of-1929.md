## Introduction: The View from the Nineties

Galbraith argues that speculative bubbles recur throughout history following a self-reinforcing process in which rising prices attract buyers who drive prices higher until an abrupt collapse, and warns that the mid-1990s exhibit the same vacuous optimism. The book's persistence in print, he claims, owes less to its merit than to recurring speculative episodes that keep bringing readers back to the great modern case of boom and collapse.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-001"></span>**Speculative bubbles follow a recurrent self-reinforcing process: rising prices attract buyers whose purchases push prices higher, justifying expectations by the very action that sends prices up, until an end comes and the descent is always more sudden than the rise.**
    - "a balloon that has been punctured does not deflate in an orderly way" —John Kenneth Galbraith
    - The pattern has recurred since Dutch tulip speculation in 1637, John Law's Louisiana scheme of 1720, and the South Sea Bubble in Britain.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-002"></span>**American history shows a speculative splurge every twenty or thirty years, from unbacked colonial currency issues and Continental notes to the 1830s canal and bank-note mania, illustrating that quick effortless enrichment is a recurrent national impulse.**
    - A 1850s New England bank closed with $500,000 in notes outstanding and assets of only $86.48 to cover them.
    - Baring Brothers had to be rescued by the Bank of England over Argentine loans, and in the 1990s Barings disappeared entirely after the operations of a minor minion in Singapore.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-003"></span>**Reassuring words from Washington that "the economic situation is fundamentally sound" should signal to all who hear them that something is wrong.**
    - A modern downturn would be less painful than 1929 because of deposit insurance, unemployment compensation, welfare, and Social Security, but a recession would still be normal.

## Vision and Boundless Hope and Optimism

The prosperity Coolidge praised was genuine, but the twenties also displayed an inordinate desire to get rich quickly with minimal effort, first manifested in the Florida land boom and then transferred to a stock market boom whose defining feature was the explosive growth of margin trading.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-004"></span>**The Florida real estate boom of the mid-1920s was a classic speculative bubble built on a real element of substance (Florida's climate) inflated into make-believe by people who wanted an excuse to believe, and it collapsed when the supply of new buyers failed and two 1926 hurricanes intervened.**
    - Land was sold for a 10 percent down payment via 'binders'—the right to buy—which multiplied tenfold the acreage from which a speculator could harvest price increases while shedding the burdens of ownership.
    - Miami bank clearings fell from $1,066,528,000 in 1925 to $143,364,000 by 1928.
    - Charles Ponzi sold subdivision lots 'near Jacksonville' that were sixty-five miles west of the city, twenty-three lots to the acre.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-005"></span>**The stock market boom that began in 1924 changed character in early 1928, when the mass escape into make-believe started in earnest and the market began rising by great vaulting leaps rather than steady steps, celebrating the operations of big professional traders assumed to be putting stocks up.**
    - John J. Raskob's 'temperate bit of optimism' on March 23, 1928 about General Motors sent the market into a boiling fury; contemporaries like Durant, the Fisher brothers, and Arthur Cutten were credited with putting their strength behind the market.
    - "the Coolidge market had gone forward like the phalanxes of Cyrus, parasang upon parasang and again parasang upon parasang" —Charles Amos Dice
    - Over 1928 the Times industrial average gained 86 points (245 to 331); Radio went from 85 to 420 without ever paying a dividend.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-006"></span>**The accepted doctrine that the Federal Reserve's 1927 easy-money policy—undertaken to help Britain after its 1925 return to the gold standard—caused the speculation and collapse is attractive because it exonerates Americans, but it wrongly assumes people always speculate whenever money is cheap.**
    - Norman, Schacht, and Rist urged easy money in 1927; the New York Fed cut its rediscount rate from 4 to 3.5 percent and bought government securities, leaving banks and individuals with funds that flowed into common stocks.
    - "the greatest and boldest operation ever undertaken by the Federal Reserve System, and…[it] resulted in one of the most costly errors committed by it or any other banking system in the last 75 years!" —Adolph C. Miller
    - Galbraith rejects this explanation: credit has often been cheaper than in 1927-29 with negligible speculation, so the theory is 'a tribute only to a recurrent preference, in economic matters, for formidable nonsense.'
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-007"></span>**Margin trading was the ingenious machinery by which Wall Street let speculators concentrate on capital gains while shedding the burdens of ownership, and the explosive growth of brokers' loans became the best index of the rising volume of speculation.**
    - The buyer got full title but left securities with his broker as collateral for the loan; yields of 1-2 percent were far less than interest of 8-12 percent, so speculators paid to divest themselves of everything but the chance for a capital gain.
    - Brokers' loans rose from about $1-1.5 billion in the early twenties to $3.48 billion at end of 1927 and were well along to six billion by the end of 1928.
    - Call loans paid up to 12 percent with complete safety, drawing a 'great river of gold' from Montreal, London, Shanghai, and Hong Kong; New York banks could borrow from the Fed at 5 percent and re-lend at 12.

## Something Should Be Done?

The authorities who had nominal responsibility for the boom faced an intractable dilemma—deliberately deflating the bubble would trigger an immediate collapse for which they would certainly be blamed, while inaction promised a worse disaster later—and their feeble efforts at 'moral suasion' were decisively defeated by Charles E. Mitchell in March 1929, after which the Federal Reserve retired from the field.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-008"></span>**Those responsible faced a choice between an immediate engineered collapse for which they would certainly be blamed and a worse eventual disaster; the eventual disaster had the advantage of allowing a few more days of life, so evasion prevailed.**
    - A bubble can easily be punctured, but incising it so it subsides gradually is a task of great delicacy; the real choice was between immediate deliberate collapse and more serious later disaster.
    - For nearly a decade the Federal Reserve authorities had denied responsibility for the deflation of 1920-21, showing who would be blamed for deliberate deflation.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-009"></span>**The Federal Reserve Board of 1929 was a body of startling incompetence and the responsibility was diffused among a president who neither knew nor cared, a Treasury Secretary who was a passionate advocate of inaction, and reassurance from high-placed people who were themselves speculating heavily.**
    - Chairman Daniel Crissinger had been trained by serving as General Counsel of the Marion Steam Shovel Company; Hoover described most Board members as 'mediocrities.'
    - Charles E. Mitchell, Chairman of National City Bank, became a class A director of the New York Fed on January 1, 1929—and the end of the boom would mean the end of Mitchell.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-010"></span>**The classic instruments of control were largely useless in 1929—the Fed had only $228 million in government securities left to sell and a rediscount rate increase would have distressed everyone but the speculator—but the Fed was helpless only because it wanted to be, since a robust denunciation of speculation by high authority would almost certainly have broken the spell.**
    - A speculator paying 10 percent to carry Radio through 1928 made 500 percent on the appreciation, so higher interest rates would not have deterred him.
    - Words were the most unpredictable weapon; their effect might be sudden and terrible and could be attributed with precision to whoever uttered them, so silence seemed golden.
    - The February 1929 warnings were so mild—stating only that further growth of speculation would be viewed with alarm—that they nonetheless caused a sharp setback, showing how effective moral suasion actually was.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-011"></span>**When Federal Reserve silence during daily March 1929 meetings triggered panic selling and call money reached 20 percent, Charles E. Mitchell single-handedly ended the crisis by pledging National City Bank would lend as necessary to prevent liquidation, issuing the Wall Street counterpart of 'I am the law in Jersey City.'**
    - "We feel that we have an obligation which is paramount to any Federal Reserve warning, or anything else, to avert any dangerous crisis in the money market." —Charles E. Mitchell
    - On March 26, 1929, 8,246,740 shares changed hands, far above any previous record, and the rate on call money reached 20 percent.
    - National City announced it would put $25 million into the call market—$5 million at 16 percent and $5 million more for each additional percentage point—regularizing its commitment to the boom.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-012"></span>**After Mitchell's defeat of the Board, the Federal Reserve retired from the field entirely, deciding not to be responsible for the inevitable collapse, and from the end of March the market had nothing further to fear from authority.**
    - "while the hysteria might be somewhat restrained, it would have to run its course" —Roy A. Young
    - Joseph Stagg Lawrence of Princeton defended Wall Street as an 'innocent community' and attacked the Fed's regulatory concern as motivated by bias against 'the wealthy, cultured, and conservative settlements on the seacoast.'

## In Goldman, Sachs We Trust

The investment trust was the most notable piece of speculative architecture of the late twenties, satisfying the demand for common stocks by divorcing the volume of corporate securities from the volume of corporate assets, and through leverage it concentrated gains—and later losses—with geometric ruthlessness, exemplified by Goldman, Sachs's frantic promotion of Shenandoah and Blue Ridge.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-013"></span>**The investment trust brought about an almost complete divorce of the volume of corporate securities outstanding from the volume of corporate assets in existence, making it the perfect answer to fears of a shortage of common stocks.**
    - Trusts sold about $400 million of securities in 1927 and an estimated $3 billion in 1929—at least a third of all new capital issues that year; total trust assets grew roughly elevenfold since the start of 1927 to exceed $8 billion.
    - The New York Stock Exchange required trusts to disclose their holdings, so most listed instead on the Curb or out-of-town exchanges, keeping their portfolios secret—likened to the South Sea promotion 'For an Undertaking which shall in due time be revealed.'
    - Sponsors received allotments or warrants at the offering price and resold at a premium; J.P. Morgan offered United Corporation stock to friends at $75 that reached 99 within days.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-014"></span>**John J. Raskob's plan 'Everybody Ought to be Rich' proposed a leveraged investment trust letting a poor man with $200 buy $500 of stock financed by a subsidiary, reflecting the era's boundless faith in the inevitability of rising stock values.**
    - "Now all the man with $200 to $500 to invest can do today is to buy Liberty bonds…" —John J. Raskob
    - One paper called it 'A practical Utopia' and another 'The greatest vision of Wall Street's greatest mind.'
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-015"></span>**The market value of a trust's securities regularly exceeded—sometimes doubled—the value of the securities it owned, the premium representing the community's admiration for professional financial genius, which trusts advertised by hiring famous professor-economists.**
    - American Founders Group had Professor Edwin W. Kemmerer as director; a Michigan trust employed Irving Fisher of Yale, Joseph S. Davis of Stanford, and Edmund E. Day.
    - United Founders later suffered a net contraction of $301,385,504 by end of 1935, its stock dropping from over $75 in 1929 to under 75 cents.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-016"></span>**Leverage worked like crack-the-whip: issuing bonds and preferred stock to buy common stocks meant a 50 percent rise in portfolio value produced a 150 percent rise in the trust's common stock, and stacking trusts upon trusts multiplied gains 700-800 percent—but geometric series are equally dramatic in reverse.**
    - In a $150 million trust financed a third each by bonds, preferred, and common, a 50 percent portfolio gain raised common-stock asset value from $50 million to $125 million, a 150 percent increase.
    - The American Founders Group grew from a friend's $500 contribution in 1922 to thirteen companies with over a billion dollars in market value, $320 million represented by 'fiscal incest'—inter-company holdings.
    - The Founders portfolio later held 5000 shares of Kreuger and Toll, banana-oil soap stock, and Yugoslav bonds; as values collapsed leverage ran in reverse.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-017"></span>**Goldman, Sachs's promotions of the Trading Corporation, Shenandoah, and Blue Ridge in 1929 stood as the pinnacle of new-era finance—a 'gargantuan insanity' in which a trust bought its own stock to boom its value and sponsored a chain of trusts applying leverage with a vengeance.**
    - The Trading Corporation bought 560,724 shares of its own stock for $57,021,936; Shenandoah (July 26) was oversubscribed sevenfold; Blue Ridge (August 20) was sponsored by Shenandoah just twenty-five days its senior.
    - John Foster Dulles sat on the boards of Shenandoah and Blue Ridge, his lack of discrimination attributed to youthful optimism.
    - "Approximately 1⅓." —Walter Sachs

## The Twilight of Illusion

Through the summer of 1929 the market rose to unprecedented heights while sound and responsible spokesmen decried the prophets of doom rather than the mounting brokers' loans, and though the stock market dominated the culture, actual participation was far smaller than legend holds; the great bull market ended by common consent on September 3.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-018"></span>**As speculation soared over the summer, responsible spokesmen decried not the increase in brokers' loans but those who attached significance to the trend, and pessimism acquired the connotation of sabotaging American prosperity.**
    - Brokers' loans increased about $400 million a month over the summer, exceeding seven billion by summer's end, more than half supplied by corporations and individuals attracted by 7-12 percent call rates.
    - "Even in general newspapers some accurate knowledge is required for discussing most things. Why is it that any ignoramus can talk about Wall Street?" —The Wall Street Journal
    - A Boston firm warned that America had no place for 'destructionists.'
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-019"></span>**The official optimists—especially Ivy League economists—compiled an unfortunate record, with Irving Fisher declaring stocks had reached 'a permanently high plateau' and the Harvard Economic Society repeatedly forecasting no serious depression until it was itself liquidated.**
    - "Stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau." —Irving Fisher
    - "a severe depression like that of 1920–21 is outside the range of probability. We are not facing protracted liquidation." —Harvard Economic Society
    - Paul M. Warburg warned in March 1929 that unrestrained speculation would end in a disastrous collapse and general depression; he was dismissed as obsolete and accused of 'sandbagging American prosperity.'
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-020"></span>**By 1929 numerous journalists resisted flattery by demanding cash for favorable market comment, while the best financial press—Poor's, Standard Statistics, the Commercial and Financial Chronicle, and above all the New York Times under Alexander Dana Noyes—maintained sober skepticism.**
    - A Daily News columnist signing himself 'The Trader' received about $19,000 from operator John J. Levenson; radio commentator William J. McMahon got $250 a week from David M. Lion to boom pool stocks.
    - Those who resisted reassurance were repeatedly, if less permanently, discredited when premature reports of a reckoning proved wrong.
* <span class="pull-quote">Don't part with your illusions; when they are gone you may still exist, but you have ceased to live.</span>
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-021"></span>**The stock market dominated the culture in 1929, with much discourse proceeding from a failure to know what wasn't known, yet the legend that 'everyone was in the market' is far from true—the number of active speculators was probably much less than a million out of 120 million Americans.**
    - Twenty-nine exchanges reported 1,548,707 customer accounts, but only about 600,000 were margin accounts; between end of 1928 and end of July 1929 margin accounts increased by only slightly more than fifty thousand.
    - Frederick Lewis Allen described the chauffeur holding Bethlehem Steel on margin, the window-cleaner eyeing Simmons, and a broker's valet who made nearly a quarter million.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-022"></span>**Pool and syndicate manipulation was rampant, with over a hundred issues subject to manipulative operations in 1929, and the public sought not to avoid being shorn but to get inside information on these operations—illustrated by the seagoing brokerage boardrooms installed on ocean liners in August.**
    - A pool manager took a position, generated active trading to create the impression something big was afoot, tipsheets stimulated public buying, then he sold out and split the profits.
    - On August 17 the Leviathan and Île de France sailed fully equipped for speculation; Irving Berlin sold 1000 shares of Paramount-Famous-Lasky at 72.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-023"></span>**The great bull market ended by common consent on September 3, 1929, after which never again did the market manifest its old confidence, and the 'Babson Break' of September 5—triggered by Roger Babson's warning that 'a crash is coming, and it may be terrific'—was promptly denounced by Wall Street.**
    - "Sooner or later a crash is coming, and it may be terrific." —Roger Babson
    - Irving Fisher countered that there might be a recession in stock prices 'but not anything in the nature of a crash'; Barron's called Babson the 'sage of Wellesley' with 'notorious inaccuracy.'
    - On September 3 the peak levels included U.S. Steel at 262, General Electric at 396, and RCA at 505, still never having paid a dividend.

## The Crash

Galbraith argues the crash did not come because the market foresaw a depression—no depression could be foreseen—but because any serious shock to confidence can collapse a speculative boom; the panic climaxed on Black Thursday October 24, was briefly halted by bankers' 'organized support,' and then resumed catastrophically.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-024"></span>**The crash was not caused by sudden awareness of a coming depression, since no serious depression could be foreseen; rather it is in the nature of a speculative boom that almost anything can collapse it, as any shock to confidence triggers selling by those hoping to get out before the final collapse.**
    - Galbraith rejects the view that the market merely mirrored an already-weakening economy; until September or October the decline was modest and might have reversed as in 1927 or 1949.
    - The collapse of Clarence Hatry's forged-certificate empire in London on September 20 and the Massachusetts refusal on October 11 to let Boston Edison split its stock are cited in the lore, but the equilibrium may simply have been shattered by a spontaneous decision to get out.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-025"></span>**Black Thursday, October 24, saw 12,894,650 shares change hands in a wild, mad scramble to sell, with the panic a phenomenon of the morning hours, showing that the reliable presence of a buyer for every seller is not inevitable.**
    - A crowd gathered outside the Exchange; a workman atop a building was assumed to be a would-be suicide; rumors held that eleven speculators had killed themselves and the Chicago and Buffalo exchanges had closed.
    - Winston Churchill, who returned Britain to the overvalued gold standard, happened to be visiting the gallery viewing his 'awful handiwork'; there is no record of anyone reproaching him.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-026"></span>**At noon on Black Thursday the nation's most powerful bankers pooled resources to provide 'organized support,' and Richard Whitney's theatrical bid of 205 for 10,000 shares of Steel checked the chain reaction of stop-loss orders, producing an electric recovery.**
    - "There has been a little distress selling on the Stock Exchange." —Thomas W. Lamont
    - Bankers assembled at 23 Wall Street: Mitchell of National City, Wiggin of Chase, Potter of Guaranty Trust, Prosser of Bankers Trust, and host Lamont of Morgan's, re-enacting the elder Morgan's 1907 rescue.
    - Falling prices kept crossing stop-loss orders that tripped more securities into the market; the bankers' buying checked this literal chain reaction decisively.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-027"></span>**In the two days following Black Thursday a vast chorus of business and political leaders proclaimed the fundamentals sound, and Sunday sermons suggested divine retribution—yet on Monday the real disaster began.**
    - "the fundamental business of the country, that is production and distribution of commodities, is on a sound and prosperous basis." —Herbert Hoover
    - Eugene Stevens, Walter Teagle, Charles M. Schwab, and Samuel Vauclain all invoked 'fundamentals'; a single dissonant note came from Governor Franklin D. Roosevelt criticizing the 'fever of speculation.'

## Things Become More Serious

The singular feature of 1929 was that the worst kept worsening; Black Tuesday October 29 was the most devastating day in market history, the bankers' organized support collapsed amid rumors they were actually selling, and the investment trusts revealed themselves as a profound source of weakness through reverse leverage, with managements committing 'fiscal self-immolation' by buying their own worthless stock.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-028"></span>**Unlike earlier panics that were over once they happened, the crash of 1929 was ingeniously designed to maximize suffering as the worst continued to worsen, extracting money through successive margin calls and ruining even those who bought bargains after October.**
    - Precisely the same 12,894,650 shares sold on October 24 were also bought; smart-money bargain-hunters then saw values drop to a third or fourth of purchase price over the next twenty-four months.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-029"></span>**By October 28 the bankers had found themselves overcommitted on a falling market, and their chilling new formula was to allow prices to fall in an orderly manner rather than support them, ending the era of 'organized support' as an impersonal market reasserted itself beyond any person's control.**
    - On Black Tuesday October 29, 16,410,030 shares were traded—more than three times a once-fabulous day; a messenger boy reportedly bought White Sewing Machine at a dollar a share amid the vacuum of buyers.
    - Albert H. Wiggin of the Chase was personally short by some millions while publicly pledging cooperative support, an interesting element of ambivalence.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-030"></span>**Few men ever lost position so rapidly as the New York bankers between October 24 and 29, because people come readily to terms with power but are very hard on those who, having had it, lose it—joining anger at past arrogance with contempt for present weakness.**
    - When corporations and out-of-town banks recalled over two billions from Wall Street, the New York banks boldly stepped in and increased their loans by about a billion, preventing a money panic—but received no credit.
    - "a good banker in a healthy capitalist society should probably be much disliked. People do not wish to trust their money to a hail-fellow-well-met but to a misanthrope who can say no." —John Kenneth Galbraith
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-031"></span>**The investment trusts, once considered a buttress of the high plateau, proved a profound source of weakness as reverse leverage removed all value from their common stock with geometric ruthlessness, and their managements committed 'fiscal self-immolation' by using cash to buy their own worthless stock.**
    - A trust with a $10 million portfolio split half in common, half in bonds/preferred, would see the common become worthless once the portfolio fell by half, since the fixed claims absorbed all remaining value.
    - The fall in Blue Ridge hit Shenandoah, whose collapse was even more horrible for the Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation, because the trusts had invested heavily in each other; the great trust boom ended in a unique manifestation of Gresham's Law as bad stocks drove out good.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-032"></span>**The market's course through mid-November was climax and anticlimax ad infinitum, with November 11-13 the dreariest days—organized support having failed and even organized reassurance abandoned—and troubling fundamentals now turning sour in the commodity markets.**
    - "Probably none of the present generation will be able to speak again … of a 'healthy reaction.'" —financial editor of the New York Times
    - Two men jumped hand-in-hand from a high window in the Ritz because they had a joint account; hotel clerks were said to ask guests whether they wished a room for sleeping or jumping.

## Aftermath I

Galbraith demolishes the myth of a suicide wave (the rate actually rose more in later depression years) and argues the crash's most significant effect was on embezzlement—advancing the rate of discovery of the 'bezzle'—while Hoover's response consisted of a negligible tax cut and the ritual of the 'no-business meeting,' a device for simulating action when action is impossible.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-033"></span>**The suicide wave that supposedly followed the crash is a myth; the suicide rate had been rising gradually before 1929 and rose far more sharply in 1930-32, while October and November 1929 had comparatively low numbers—the public simply assigned the crash as an automatic motive.**
    - US suicides per 100,000 rose from 14.0 in 1929 to 17.4 in 1932; October 1929 recorded 1331 suicides and November 1344, fewer than in three other months that year.
    - The death of J.J. Riordan and the withholding of the news until the County Trust bank had closed for the weekend, at Al Smith's decision, illustrates the era's nervousness.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-034"></span>**The crash's effect on embezzlement was more significant than on suicide: embezzlement has a time parameter, so at any moment there exists an inventory of undiscovered embezzlement—the 'bezzle'—that grows in good times when trust is high and shrinks in depression when the crash advances the rate of discovery.**
    - During the boom, universal trust and the new need for margin funds swelled the bezzle; the crash turned trust into suspicion, triggered audits, and made irredeemable the position of the employee who had embezzled to play the market.
    - The looting of the Union Industrial Bank of Flint, Michigan totaled $3,592,000; about a dozen officers cooperated, sending depositor funds to the New York call market while records showed they remained on deposit.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-035"></span>**Hoover's tax cut of one percentage point was dramatic in rate but negligible in effect because the taxes being cut were already insignificant for most people, reducing a $4000 family's burden from $5.63 to $1.88.**
    - A married man with $10,000 saw his tax cut in half from $120 to $65; the step was well received as boosting purchasing power despite its triviality.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-036"></span>**Hoover's series of White House conferences exemplified the 'no-business meeting'—a rite called not to transact business but to create the impression that business is being done, deriving significance from a solemn sense of assembled power and serving as a practical expression of laissez faire that gave the sense of impressive action while producing none.**
    - "Some device for simulating action, when action is impossible, is indispensable in a sound and functioning democracy." —John Kenneth Galbraith
    - After the November 21 industrial leaders' meeting, expressions of confidence were so robust that Julius Rosenwald said he feared a labor shortage; the Boston Globe praised Hoover's 'dynamics of mastery.'
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-037"></span>**The market's decline resumed after a brief January-March 1930 recovery and continued through June 1932, making the crash's worst levels seem memorable by contrast, while Hoover forgot his own wise rule that words are unimportant in economic disturbance and repeatedly forecast imminent recovery.**
    - The Times industrials fell from 224 on November 13, 1929 to 58 on July 8, 1932; U.S. Steel reached a low of 22, down from 262; Blue Ridge was 63 cents and Shenandoah 50 cents.
    - "Every time an Administration official gives out an optimistic statement about business conditions, the market immediately drops." —Simeon D. Fess

## Aftermath II

The crash worked its worst havoc on reputations, discrediting the scholarly forecasters and exposing the market operations of the heads of the Chase and National City banks, while the search for a 'symbolic evil' in Wall Street—a wrongdoer whose sins would stigmatize a whole class—found its perfect object in Richard Whitney, whose fall confirmed the New Deal victory on regulation.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-038"></span>**The scholarly forecasters were held accountable in a way the 'fundamentally sound' ritualists were not, with Joseph Stagg Lawrence disappearing from Princeton and the Harvard Economic Society compiling a two-year record of relentlessly wrong optimism until it was dissolved.**
    - The Society forecast recovery repeatedly through 1930-31; on August 30, 1930 it said 'the present depression has about spent its force,' each forecast wildly optimistic.
    - "It was the psychology of panic. It was mob psychology, and it was not, primarily, that the price level of the market was unsoundly high" —Irving Fisher
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-039"></span>**Albert H. Wiggin of the Chase conducted astonishing operations in his own bank's stock financed by the bank itself, including selling Chase short through the Shermar Corporation just before the crash for a profit of over $4 million while heading the institution.**
    - Shermar sold 42,506 shares of Chase short between September 23 and November 4, 1929, then Murlyn Corporation bought shares with a $6,588,430 loan from the Chase itself to cover the short, netting $4,008,538.
    - On retirement Wiggin was voted a life salary of $100,000, later revealed to have been his own impulse; he ultimately renounced the compensation.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-040"></span>**Charles E. Mitchell of the National City was arrested for tax evasion after selling National City stock to his wife at 212 for a $2.87 million tax loss—a device far more common then than now—having borrowed twelve million from Morgan's in a failed attempt to support the stock during a merger.**
    - "I sold this stock, frankly, for tax purposes." —Charles E. Mitchell
    - Mitchell's cut from the bank's management fund was $1,316,634.14 for 1928 and $1,108,000 for the first half of 1929; he was acquitted by a jury but lost a $1,100,000 civil judgment.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-041"></span>**Our political tradition cherishes the 'generalized symbol of evil'—a wrongdoer whose sins are attributed to a whole class—and Richard Whitney, president of the Stock Exchange, arch-conservative Morgan associate, and its uncompromising public defender, became the ideal such figure when arraigned for grand larceny in 1938.**
    - Whitney was one of the most disastrous businessmen in modern history, running losing ventures in mineral colloids, peat humus, and applejack distilling; he emerged as 'the Ponzi of financial self-deception' by buying 137,672 of 148,750 Distilled Liquors shares to support a collapsing stock.
    - To fund his final desperation he pledged securities belonging to others, including the Stock Exchange gratuity fund; Bernard E. Smith refused him a $250,000 loan, saying 'I frankly didn't like him.'
    - Galbraith draws a parallel to Alger Hiss: each served the enemies of his class, yet neither man's guilt proved his associates were doing the same—crime is the act of an individual, not the predisposition of a class.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-042"></span>**The Whitney affair confirmed the New Deal victory on regulation, as the surrender of the Exchange to the SEC ended the cold war over regulation; the Securities Acts of 1933 and 1934 outlawed the era's abuses and established the principle of public regulation, though Wall Street's good fortune was that the reckoning came late, after the New Deal assault on business was waning.**
    - The 1934 Act empowered the Federal Reserve to set margin requirements up to 100 percent, outlawed pools and wash sales, divorced commercial banks from securities affiliates, and created the SEC.
    - Regulatory bodies have a life cycle: vigorous in youth, they mellow and in old age become an arm of the industry they regulate or senile.

## Cause and Consequence

Galbraith argues the crash was implicit in the preceding speculation and cannot be explained by easy credit, but the far more important question—why the downturn became a decade-long depression—is answered by five fundamental weaknesses in the economy plus a poor state of economic intelligence that forced both parties to reject all corrective action, making the depression a triumph of dogma over thought.

* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-043"></span>**Speculation requires not easy credit but a pervasive mood of confidence, optimism, trust in others' benevolence, and plentiful savings; a speculative outbreak then has an immunizing effect that wears off with time, so recurrence becomes possible—nothing could have induced Americans to speculate in 1935, but by 1955 the chances are much better.**
    - The long-accepted explanation that easy credit impelled borrowing to buy stocks on margin is 'obviously nonsense'—much 1928-29 speculation occurred on money borrowed at astringent rates; money was tight by ordinary tests.
    - "a crowd of projectors, ingenious and absurd, honest and knavish, employed themselves in devising new schemes for the employment of redundant capital" —Macaulay
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-044"></span>**The notion that a depression was inevitable by some biblical law of compensation, or governed by an inevitable business-cycle rhythm, cannot be supported: in 1929 the labor force was not tired, the capital plant was not depleted, raw materials were ample, and people's wants were far from sated—no refreshing pause was necessary.**
    - Depletion of capital plant actually occurred during the ensuing years of idleness when new investment was curtailed, not before.
    - In 1954 an economist on Eisenhower's staff explained a recession by saying the economy was enjoying a well-merited rest, a notion Galbraith rejects.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-045"></span>**The economy was fundamentally unsound in five specific ways: bad income distribution, bad corporate structure, bad banking structure, a dubious foreign balance, and a poor state of economic intelligence—each of which the crash was especially effective at exploiting.**
    - The top 5 percent of the population received about a third of all personal income, so the economy depended on high-bracket investment and luxury spending, both especially susceptible to the crash.
    - Holding-company and investment-trust pyramids threatened devastation by reverse leverage, tempting operating companies to curtail plant investment to keep paying dividends; the many independent banks meant one failure led to others with a domino effect.
    - As a creditor nation with high tariffs, the US had to balance its export surplus through gold, foreign loans, or default; the crash ended the foreign lending, and Hoover's tariff increase eliminated the import route, forcing defaults and a fall in exports that hit farmers hard.
    - J. and W. Seligman and the National City Company paid Juan Leguia $450,000 for not blocking a Peruvian loan; a National City appraisal called Peru 'Bad debt record, adverse moral and political risk' yet still floated its loans.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-046"></span>**The poor state of economic intelligence meant reputable advice was almost uniquely perverse: after a negligible tax cut, policy was entirely on the side of making things worse, with both parties demanding a balanced budget and fearing inflation and going off gold during the most violent deflation in the nation's history—a triumph of dogma over thought.**
    - "It would steady the country greatly if there could be prompt assurance that there will be no tampering or inflation of the currency; that the budget will be unquestionably balanced" —Herbert Hoover
    - The Democratic 1932 platform called for a balanced budget and a 25 percent cut in government cost; Roosevelt in Brooklyn pledged 'a sound currency to be preserved at all hazards.'
    - The rejection of both fiscal and monetary policy amounted to a rejection of all affirmative government economic policy, forcing leaders of both parties to disavow every available step to check deflation.
* <span id="the-great-crash-of-1929-047"></span>**Had the economy been fundamentally sound the crash's effect might have been small, but business in 1929 was exceedingly fragile and vulnerable to the blow it received; while later reforms—better income distribution, dismantled holding-company pyramids, federal deposit insurance, and modest economic knowledge—have strengthened the weak points, another speculative boom and collapse remains possible because government will always find reasons not to use its preventive controls.**
    - "when a greenhouse succumbs to a hailstorm something more than a purely passive role is normally attributed to the storm" —John Kenneth Galbraith
    - By 1948 the top 5 percent's share of personal income had dropped from nearly a third to less than a fifth; deposit insurance dissolved the fear that transmitted banking weakness.
    - Action to break up a boom must be weighed against causing unemployment at a politically inopportune moment; booms are not stopped until after they start, and then the action looks like a decision for immediate against ultimate death, identifying the executioner.
    - "liquidate labor, liquidate stocks, liquidate the farmers, liquidate real estate" —Andrew Mellon
