## Kristjan Kullamägi

Kullamägi’s path from repeated blow-ups to a $100 million-plus peak shows that simple, well-researched setups can compound spectacularly when paired with strict risk controls, but the same trader can suffer massive drawdowns when he violates his own market-environment rules. His story turns trading success into a problem of fit, patience, situational awareness, and execution discipline rather than complexity.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--001"></span>**Kristjan Kullamägi’s early failures came from trading without a defined method or position-sizing discipline, and each blow-up became useful only because he studied what had gone wrong before trying again.**
    - Kullamägi left biomedical laboratory science shortly before graduation after a security-guard job at the Stockholm Nasdaq building sparked his obsession with markets.
    - His first account of about $5,000 lost 90% in two months because he went all-in on individual day trades without risk controls.
    - He returned repeatedly to security work, saved new stakes, and treated the lengthening time before each blow-up as evidence that he was improving.
    - The turning point was not a single revelation but a cluster of habits: quicker losses, higher-probability trades, profit-taking, smaller size, and lower commissions through a US broker.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--002"></span>**Kullamägi’s core methodology rests on three durable setups: episodic pivots, momentum-burst breakouts, and parabolic shorts, each with explicit entry and exit conditions.**
    - Episodic pivots require major news, a sharp gap higher, and a volume explosion that forces the market to reassess the stock.
    - Momentum-burst breakouts target leading stocks that consolidate for one to three weeks and then break out, with profits often concentrated in the next three to five days.
    - Parabolic shorts focus on small caps up several hundred percent over multiple days, but only after visible weakness such as a break of the opening range or a 5-minute candle low.
    - He credits Pradeep Bonde and Stockbee for key concepts and insists he did not invent his setups, but verified them through thousands of charts.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--003"></span>**Kullamägi’s edge depends on trading volatile stocks that can move far, while controlling risk through stops, average daily true range filters, and small percentage risk per trade.**
    - For long trades, his initial stop is usually the low of the entry day; for shorts, it is the high of the day.
    - If the distance to the stop is greater than the average daily true range, he skips the trade because the risk/reward is distorted.
    - He usually risks 0.5% or less of account equity and rarely more than 1% on a single trade, despite having achieved triple-digit annual gains in some years.
    - His win rate is only about 25% to 30%, but the average win is large enough relative to the average loss to make the method profitable.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--004"></span>**The shift from day trading to swing trading improved Kullamägi’s results because larger moves require time, and longer holding periods reduced the need to constantly find new trades.**
    - He began as a 1-minute-bar day trader buying intraday breakouts and shorting parabolic small caps.
    - Swing trading let him work less intensely while holding stocks through the part of the move where major profits develop.
    - He initially took profits after three to five days, then learned to hold partial positions using 10-day or 20-day moving-average trailing stops.
    - The tradeoff is that moving-average exits do not sell near the top, which can produce large drawdowns when the whole market turns down at once.
* <span class="pull-quote">If you don’t have rules or conditions that clearly define when to enter and exit trades, you don’t have a methodology.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--005"></span>**Kullamägi’s long-side setups work only when the broad market is favorable, so market regime filters are part of the strategy rather than optional context.**
    - He uses a simple 10-day and 20-day moving-average rule: when both rise, the market is favorable; when the 10-day crosses below the 20-day, caution is warranted.
    - He notes that four out of five stocks move with the market, making breakout and episodic-pivot trades dangerous in downtrends.
    - He acknowledges breadth-based models used by Bonde and other traders, but personally focuses on moving averages and occasional breadth extremes.
    - Parabolic shorts are less dependent on the general market because the setup is driven by stock-specific excess.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--006"></span>**The 2022 collapse in Kullamägi’s equity came not from a single catastrophic trade but from violating his own rules after a euphoric 2020-2021 run detached him from risk reality.**
    - He started 2020 with about $3.5 million and ended the year near $36 million, then briefly exceeded $100 million in late 2021.
    - At his peak, he held roughly $150 million of long exposure and expected to reach $200 million within months.
    - When the market turned down, he ignored his moving-average trend filters and continued buying breakouts and episodic pivots in a downtrend.
    - From the $105 million peak in November 2021 to mid-2022, he lost about $60 million, much of it through many small to mid-size losses rather than one giant loss.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--007"></span>**Kullamägi’s best years were fueled by thematic volatility, especially Covid-related and quantum-computing moves that created both long episodic pivots and later parabolic shorts.**
    - In 2020, mask manufacturers such as APT and LAKE and vaccine-related stocks produced major episodic-pivot gains followed by short opportunities when they collapsed.
    - Tesla was also a major long-side winner in 2020.
    - In December 2024, quantum-computing names such as LAES provided a sector-based episodic pivot and then a parabolic short after the stock rose from about $2 to $11.
    - He missed a huge LAES long after exiting early despite the stop not being hit, reinforcing the lesson that discretionary exits can sabotage a valid setup.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--008"></span>**After becoming wealthy, Kullamägi’s reduced focus showed that trading performance depends on sustained attention, even when the method is already known.**
    - After buying a yacht in late 2021, he spent more time traveling and trading from hotels or his boat, which weakened his focus.
    - He stopped reviewing his Evernote database of screenshots of elite setups, reducing his conviction when opportunities appeared.
    - He passed on SMCI in January 2024 despite recognizing a five-star episodic pivot on preliminary earnings and massive volume.
    - His phrase for the transition is that he used to live to trade and now trades to live.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--009"></span>**Kullamägi’s advice centers on research-backed conviction, personality fit, and ruthless loss cutting rather than copying another trader’s setup mechanically.**
    - He says traders must verify setups over hundreds or thousands of historical examples before they can hold conviction through drawdowns.
    - He warns that many traders have setups but lack situational awareness about when those setups work.
    - He identifies common mistakes as ignoring intended stops, oversizing, and trading methods that do not fit temperament.
    - "All I have done is make what I have learned from others work for me." —Kristjan Kullamägi

## Lance Breitstein

Breitstein’s career shows how obsessive process improvement, mentorship, and extremely precise short-term execution can produce extraordinary day-trading returns, while later scaling required him to lengthen timeframes and rethink the costs of trading to health and life. His chapter frames trading as a demanding game where the right question is not only how to win, but whether one is playing the right game.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--010"></span>**Breitstein chose a $26,000 prop-trading job at Trillium over a $100,000-plus bank offer because he valued the long-term expected value of learning from proven traders over immediate salary.**
    - Family financial stress during the Great Recession made financial security a powerful motivator.
    - He researched Trillium through Wall Street Oasis and focused on whether real traders there were making money consistently.
    - He accepted a Princeton satellite-office role because it placed him near the firm’s top trader, even though it meant giving up the social appeal of New York City.
    - His low salary reinforced urgency and kept him working late rather than spending money on entertainment.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--011"></span>**Breitstein’s day-trading edge came from combining speed with contextual judgment, allowing him to act on news and order-flow situations that algorithms or slower traders misread.**
    - He describes intraday trading as a complex video game against the world, a frame shaped by his childhood video-game background.
    - He optimized hotkeys, screen layout, ticker entry, and limit-order configurations to shave fractions of a second from execution.
    - He argues that algos can react to simple headlines, but skilled humans still add value when headlines require context, nuance, or sentiment interpretation.
    - He used limit-order hotkeys such as buying a preset number of shares up to a specified percentage through the offer, rather than using market orders.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--012"></span>**His mentor’s nuanced technical-analysis framework taught Breitstein that simple chart patterns rarely suffice; edge emerges when multiple details align across price, volume, volatility, news, and timeframes.**
    - The top trader at Trillium kept annotated notebooks of multi-day patterns, including closes relative to range, volume, Bollinger bands, and moving-average angles.
    - Breitstein argues academia often misses technical-analysis edge because it tests overly simple patterns without enough context.
    - A breakout becomes more meaningful when it occurs on a catalyst, across multiple timeframes, with high short interest or reflexive buying pressure.
    - The November 5, 2024 Bitcoin breakout after a pro-crypto US presidential election outcome is presented as a high-quality alignment of news and chart structure.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--013"></span>**Breitstein overcame 11 consecutive losing months by treating performance as a lagging indicator of inputs and using video review to accelerate pattern recognition.**
    - He recorded his screens and spent nearly every Sunday for 10 years reviewing key trading clips, sometimes at half speed and sometimes practicing at 1.5x or 2x speed.
    - He was still only slightly above breakeven after about 18 months and began interviewing elsewhere, but senior traders saw his process improving.
    - He used bad days as fuel for review rather than as a reason to leave the office early.
    - His first major confidence breakthrough came from shorting Tesla in 2013 on an exhaustion-gap pattern, making $10,000 while his mentor made far more on the same idea.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--014"></span>**Breitstein’s primary trade types exploit extreme short-term dislocations: exhaustion gaps, capitulation panics, bouncy-ball breakdowns, inverse bouncy-ball breakouts, and breaking-news moves.**
    - An exhaustion gap is a multi-day euphoric move on rising volume that culminates in a final gap, after which buyers or shorts capitulate and reversal odds rise.
    - His Nikkei futures buy on August 5, 2024 came after panic selling linked to yen short covering created a move larger than the Covid panic, which he viewed as excessive.
    - The Nikola bouncy-ball short used a pattern of lower highs above support followed by a break of support.
    - The SMCI auditor-resignation headline in August 2024 and Oracle’s January 21, 2025 Stargate AI announcement illustrate fast news trades.
* <span class="pull-quote">Bad trades don’t kill people; bad sizing kills people.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--015"></span>**Risk control in Breitstein’s trading is built around predetermined stops, much smaller size when stops are absent, and sizing that reflects expected value rather than personal goals.**
    - More than 90% of his trades have a predetermined exit; intraday stops often use the previous 2-minute bar, while multi-day trades may use the prior day’s low.
    - For exceptional trades such as the August 2024 Nikkei panic, he accepted risk around 5% of account equity pre-tax, but typical trades were far smaller.
    - His worst Trillium loss, the November 2021 Avis trade, came from sizing too aggressively because he was trying to reach a $20 million annual profit target.
    - "The market demands you size your trades objectively based upon expected value." —Lance Breitstein
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--016"></span>**Leaving Trillium forced Breitstein to give up the shortest-timeframe edge, but adapting the same chart concepts to multi-day trades made him more profitable with far less activity.**
    - At Trillium, about 99% of his trades were intraday, and his buying power peaked near $12 million.
    - After leaving, he no longer had the technology or routine for the fastest headline and order-flow trades.
    - He applied intraday timing to larger swing trades, held portions with daily stops, and became roughly 1% as active while making more money.
    - He frames the lesson as asking whether one is playing the right game, since short-term edges may be highest probability but least scalable.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--017"></span>**Breitstein uses option selling as a conditional-entry tool after volatility spikes, especially by selling puts at prices where he would want to own the stock.**
    - He avoids selling volatility before the extreme move; he prefers selling options after panic or euphoria has inflated premium.
    - Selling a put at a desired buy price can produce premium if the stock does not fall, or a lower effective purchase price if assigned.
    - He is much more cautious selling calls because upside risk is theoretically unlimited.
    - When a short call is exercised during an upside panic, he may sell puts against the short stock to gain cushion from elevated volatility.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--018"></span>**Despite immense financial success, Breitstein increasingly views active trading as a health and identity problem because monetary optimization conflicts with sleep, presence, philanthropy, and longevity.**
    - A sleep study found his results among the worst the professionals had seen outside active military contexts.
    - During the August 2024 Nikkei trade, he stayed up for two days after running a half marathon, then flew to Los Angeles to film his trading course.
    - He created a high-production trading course at theonelanceb.com, with net profits directed to charity, as a way to pass on knowledge and model giving back.
    - He cites Bill Gates, Jeff Bezos, John Arnold, Sam Altman, and Bryan Johnson as examples of people who moved beyond pure money maximization into larger chapters.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--019"></span>**Breitstein’s advice to aspiring traders emphasizes coachability, work ethic, process identity, absence of limiting beliefs, and evidence of improvement more than early P&L.**
    - He says the key question for a struggling second-year trader is whether experienced traders see the thought process improving, because P&L lags inputs.
    - As a Chicago-office manager, he saw roughly one in three or four hires succeed, with failures often lacking work ethic, a strong why, or coachability.
    - He draws on James Clear’s identity-based habit framework: defining himself as a hard worker made staying late and reviewing mistakes natural.
    - "One of the main reasons so many traders fail is that they have limiting beliefs." —Lance Breitstein

## Simon Russo

Russo’s chapter shows both the power and danger of extraordinary trading talent: his research-heavy contrarian methods compounded $40,000 into over $500 million, yet nearly all of his worst losses came from trades outside his own selection and risk rules. The core lesson is that even elite edge cannot substitute for systematic risk controls, patience, and emotional detachment from being right.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--020"></span>**Russo’s path from music to trading was driven by the same obsession with mastery, and his decision to leave a prestigious music school reflected a belief that trading better matched his passion, temperament, and need for independence.**
    - He studied at the USC Thornton School of Music after years of intense musical training shaped by a humble, craft-obsessed teacher.
    - A high-school trading contest introduced him to mean reversion by shorting top gainers, and he won by so much that the teacher suspected cheating.
    - He wrote his parents that trading, like music, required self-discovery, mastery, screen time, and finding one’s own path rather than following a textbook.
    - "It has never been about the money for me; it is about the path of self-discovery and mastery necessary to become proficient." —Simon Russo
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--021"></span>**Russo’s early account blow-ups all had the same structure: short positions in volatile stocks, oversized exposure, no reliable exit plan, and a refusal to accept discomfort until the broker forced liquidation.**
    - His first $3,000 account was lost trying to front-run penny-stock chat-room pump alerts and then holding too long out of greed.
    - In 2014, two sketchy-company shorts with no stop or exit plan erased most of a $50,000 account funded by his father.
    - In 2015, he ran $50,000 to $550,000 shorting biotechs, then lost it all on a scalp intended to make only $2,000 after adding to a loser and holding overnight.
    - The AQXP loss in August 2015 drove the account negative before he briefly recovered the deficit by buying the same stock, an outcome he admits was lucky.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--022"></span>**Russo became consistently profitable only after separating trade selection from execution and refusing to short merely because a stock was up a lot.**
    - His selection process asks why a stock is up, whether the catalyst is real or superficial, and whether SEC filings show financial weakness or likely dilution.
    - He looks for low cash, high burn rates, convertible debt, warrants, or identifiable holders likely to sell into a rally.
    - A stock up on true positive news, such as a move from losses to profits or legitimate biotech trial success, is avoided even if it looks extended.
    - The blow-up trades failed not only on execution but at the selection stage because they did not meet his criteria.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--023"></span>**The 2016 MGT short was Russo’s breakthrough because it proved that a volatile parabolic short could be traded safely when sizing, stops, and pyramiding were integrated.**
    - Before the MGT trade, his trading partner was up far more despite similar research, revealing that Russo still took unnecessary trades outside the best setups.
    - Russo first tried the short the day before the top and took an acceptable loss, preserving capital for the real opportunity.
    - On the decisive day, he shorted as momentum faded, added as the stock broke down, and moved the stop lower as the trade worked.
    - He described the trade as one he would put on the fridge because selection, risk, and execution all aligned.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--024"></span>**Russo’s later adaptations increased scalability: he added broken-short longs, moved from small caps to liquid mid- and large-cap momentum stocks, and used pyramiding to expand winners without increasing initial risk.**
    - A broken short is a stock that begins as a short candidate but refuses to break, traps shorts, consolidates, and breaks higher instead.
    - VRPX in August 2021 illustrates a broken-short long where clean capital structure, Covid-related sensitivity, and price action supported the buy.
    - He shifted from small caps to names like Tesla, GameStop, AMC, and DWAC because his account size made small-cap capacity too limiting.
    - His pyramiding method adds only after the market allows him to lower the stop, keeping total risk roughly constant while increasing profit potential.
* <span class="pull-quote">Relying on willpower and discipline is not noble; it’s stupid.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--025"></span>**Russo’s strongest edge combines selectivity, calm temperament, deep research, and a minimalist willingness to wait for only obvious A+ trades.**
    - Since 2018, he reports a 71% win rate, though he notes it is somewhat overstated by trades where he stayed in rather than stopping and reentering.
    - He attributes success partly to remaining calm under stress and being able to focus intensely for long periods.
    - He enjoys comprehensive research, including SEC filings, case studies, and hundreds of examples, rather than treating it as drudgery.
    - "Why would I do 10 things when I can just do one thing well?" —Simon Russo
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--026"></span>**Russo’s two giant losses in SPY and Carvana show that extraordinary historical success does not eliminate the danger of strategy drift, overconfidence, and ignoring stops.**
    - After being up roughly $50 million in 2023, he shorted SPY as a range mean-reversion idea despite it not being part of his normal strategy and lost about $50 million.
    - He reentered a Carvana short after previously escaping at breakeven, even though the setup no longer met his conditions and he had no risk budget.
    - Carvana’s bullish earnings gap added roughly $30 million to the loss within minutes, and he liquidated around a $50 million realized loss.
    - The liquidation felt liberating because continued stubbornness could have wiped out the account as the stock later rose dramatically.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--027"></span>**Russo’s postscript drawdown forced him to replace mental stops with redundant mechanical risk controls because willpower proved unreliable under stress.**
    - A sub-A+ mean-reversion short began with about 0.5% intended risk but escalated into a $120 million peak drawdown after he re-shorted, doubled, and kept adding to a loser.
    - He eventually covered for a $15 million loss and then recovered to new equity highs through later A+ trades, but recognized luck had played a dangerous role.
    - His new system assigns every trade a 0.5% to 2.0% risk tier and automatically liquidates 50% of the position at 1.2x tier loss and the remainder at 1.5x.
    - A panic override liquidates the entire position and blocks new trades in the symbol if a gap blows past 3x tier risk.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--028"></span>**Russo’s partial withdrawal from trading shows how mastery can lose meaning once money is no longer motivating, and how non-attachment can paradoxically improve selectivity.**
    - In 2022, after a bitter breakup with a trading partner and a period of enormous profits, Russo realized he no longer enjoyed staring at screens all day.
    - He reduced trading to about 10% of his time, hired an analyst as his eyes and ears, and focused more on philanthropy, education, climate, environment, and business projects.
    - He says the best recent performance came partly from favorable market conditions, larger account size, and waiting for obvious trades while living the rest of his life.
    - He cites The Billionaire Who Wasn’t as influential in thinking about anonymous giving and greater uses of wealth.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--029"></span>**Russo’s advice rejects imitation and motivational simplification: traders need a method suited to their traits, a reactive mindset, and brutal honesty about whether they have an edge.**
    - He says traders should act like surfers: wait for the right wave rather than forcing predictions onto the market.
    - He created a composite “Robo-trader” role model by taking traits from admired traders rather than copying any full methodology.
    - He argues that most people will not succeed in trading no matter how hard they work because talent, biology, emotional makeup, and environment matter.
    - "The smallest mistakes can have large consequences." —Simon Russo

## Lukas Fröhlich

Fröhlich’s chapter traces a rare metamorphosis from leveraged teenage blow-ups to small-cap shorting, euphoric mid-cap longs, and finally scalable value-oriented thematic investing. The causal thread is that he repeatedly matched strategy to market regime and account size while preserving the same core habits: research, asymmetry, risk limits, and aggressive pyramiding when the thesis was being confirmed.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--030"></span>**Lukas Fröhlich’s teenage trading began as an escape from school and language difficulty, but repeated leveraged blow-ups taught him that survival rules had to precede returns.**
    - He grew up between France and Germany, struggled after switching into a German-speaking school, and saw money as a route to freedom.
    - At 13, he traded an account opened in his father’s name, starting with about $500 saved from gifts.
    - He traded currencies and CFDs with extreme leverage, including a 100-to-1 DAX short that produced a negative account balance.
    - He broke the blow-up cycle by setting maximum potential loss per stock and maximum daily portfolio loss.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--031"></span>**Fröhlich’s first durable edge came from small-cap shorting, where he combined SEC-filed fundamental weakness with intraday statistical patterns rather than shorting every gap-up indiscriminately.**
    - He learned from free videos by traders such as Tim Grittani and later collaborated with Miah Lee on research and backtesting.
    - He targeted companies with impending working-capital problems that were likely to raise capital through dilutive secondaries.
    - He studied underwriters, warrants, convertibles, and past price behavior after gap-ups as differentiating factors.
    - His technical work compared intraday patterns across samples, asking how stocks behaved after 50%, 100%, or 300% gap-ups and after early sideways action.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--032"></span>**The SONN trade on April 14, 2020 illustrates Fröhlich’s small-cap short process: identify a gap-up with weak fundamentals, infer likely supply from convertibles and warrants, then trade around the position with tight risk.**
    - SONN appeared on his scanner after a huge pre-market gap on massive volume.
    - In about 20 minutes, he reviewed financials, capital structure, warrants, and convertibles and inferred likely selling pressure from holders converting into shares.
    - His first short attempt was stopped out for a small loss, while the second near the day’s highs and a later bounce short caught the real decline.
    - He usually exited same day because borrow costs and overnight gap risk were high in small-float shorts.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--033"></span>**Fröhlich’s small-cap risk controls were essential because halted short squeezes can produce open-ended losses, so he used small initial entries, mental stops, and a three-try rule.**
    - He entered with about half intended size and added only after the trade worked, using pyramiding to increase exposure into weakness.
    - Stops could be new daily highs, percentage thresholds, standard-deviation moves above the opening price, or time stops tied to expected intraday patterns.
    - If he failed on three short attempts in one day, he stopped trying that stock for the session.
    - He had been stuck short in halted stocks multiple times and exited as soon as they reopened.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--034"></span>**Fröhlich switched to mid-cap breakout longs in 2020 because bull-market liquidity, retail stimulus, and account growth made scalable euphoric longs more attractive than small-cap shorts.**
    - He focused on $1 billion to $5 billion market-cap stocks with smaller floats and strong social-media themes, especially electric vehicles in 2020.
    - He wanted a catalyst that moved the stock 40% to 100% on increased volume, followed by consolidation in the upper half of the range.
    - He sometimes entered before the breakout in bullish patterns or near the 20-day EMA because liquidity could vanish once the breakout began.
    - He viewed this strategy as effective only in the 7th to 9th inning of a bull market, during the euphoria phase.
* <span class="pull-quote">As soon as something becomes too easy or a consensus trade, the game changes.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--035"></span>**Fröhlich’s mid-cap breakout method depended on asymmetric outcomes: many small losses or scratches, offset by rare home runs amplified through disciplined pyramiding.**
    - He typically entered about two-thirds of intended size initially, then added on breakouts, pullbacks to the 20-day EMA, or new consolidations.
    - Each addition had to qualify as an independently attractive setup, not merely an add because the position was profitable.
    - He sized additions so that if the stop was hit, the loss on the added position would roughly offset existing open profits, turning the trade into a scratch.
    - If a pyramid addition failed quickly, he treated the whole trade as invalid and exited.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--036"></span>**The GRWG trade in late 2020 and early 2021 exemplifies Fröhlich’s bull-market long setup: catalyst, consolidation, EMA convergence, secondary catalyst, pyramid, and hysteria exit.**
    - GrowGeneration’s August 2020 earnings triggered a 100% plus rally and placed it on his radar amid cannabis-sector social-media attention.
    - After months of consolidation, a pullback converged with the 9-day and 20-day EMAs, motivating the initial position.
    - He added on another positive earnings catalyst and later on a pullback to the 20-day EMA.
    - Exit signals included parabolic third waves, wide upside excursions from moving averages, many consecutive up days, volume expansion, and ADTR increases.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--037"></span>**Fröhlich stopped trading mid-cap breakouts when the bull market stalled because applying a strategy outside its proper environment can turn a high-return method into an account-threatening disaster.**
    - He traded the strategy from spring 2020 to mid-2021, then stepped away as the market environment changed.
    - He argues most traders fail with this kind of method because they keep using it for years when conditions are no longer suitable.
    - The strategy was disastrous in the next couple of years for traders who did not stop.
    - He emphasizes patience to avoid a strategy for two or three years if necessary.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--038"></span>**Fröhlich’s current long-term investing approach blends contrarian valuation with trend-following timing, seeking hated but strong companies with catalysts the market is not pricing.**
    - He compares his approach to Stan Druckenmiller and George Soros: a mix of investing and trading, contrarianism and trend following.
    - BABA in 2024 fit the template: record-low Chinese equity valuations, low P/E, earnings growth, cloud potential, buybacks, and likely government stimulus.
    - Centene in summer 2025 represented a healthcare panic where he expected politicians to protect ACA subsidies and reverse or soften Medicare cuts.
    - He prefers initiating or sizing up when a stock reclaims its 200-day moving average, breaks out of a range, or retests the 200-day from above.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--039"></span>**Fröhlich sizes high-conviction investment themes aggressively but uses losses, 200-day moving averages, and market-cycle awareness to avoid repeating the 2022 mistake of buying too early and too large.**
    - He may risk roughly 5% to 10% on a major investment theme, arguing experienced traders often undersize relative to half-Kelly logic.
    - The chapter includes a Kelly criterion explanation showing why overbetting is more damaging than comparable underbetting when probabilities are uncertain.
    - His worst mistake was a roughly 40% drawdown in 2022 from buying cheap stocks too early, taking large positions, and adding to losers.
    - He learned not to trade his entire net worth, to wait for bottoms to form, and to analyze what could go wrong before the worst case materializes.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--040"></span>**Fröhlich’s broader trading philosophy is that volatility and inefficiency create opportunity, but traders must keep adapting to where inefficiency exists now rather than copying old legends mechanically.**
    - If starting today, he would probably focus on crypto because he sees it as similar to small caps 15 years earlier: inefficient, volatile, and liquid enough.
    - He says studying the past has value, but many traders copy strategies from more inefficient eras without noticing changed competition.
    - He still occasionally takes intuitive mean-reversion trades, such as triple-leveraged semiconductor ETFs or short gold after 10 consecutive up weeks in October 2025.
    - "One of my fundamental market beliefs is that volatility creates opportunity." —Lukas Fröhlich

## Phil Goedeker

Goedeker’s career shows that two supposedly dangerous strategies, short selling and option premium selling, can be survivable when paired with reflexive loss cutting, selective sizing, and constant capital preservation. His success comes less from predicting markets broadly than from recognizing A+ dislocations, sizing them heavily, and turning everything else into harmless entertainment-sized activity.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--041"></span>**Phil Goedeker entered markets for money but stayed because he loved the game, and his impatience led him away from long trades toward short setups that worked or failed quickly.**
    - At 15, seeing wealthy lifestyles at Lake of the Ozarks made him want a path to wealth faster than a conventional career.
    - He read stock-market books at the library and business pages in the St. Louis Post-Dispatch during the late-1990s bull market.
    - His first two $3,000 college trading stakes were lost buying uptrending stocks and selling quickly when they failed to move immediately.
    - He later recognized that his short-term horizon made fundamental analysis almost irrelevant to his trading.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--042"></span>**Goedeker’s breakthrough came when he discovered shorting overextended small caps, turning $5,000 into $1 million in nine months by going all-in while cutting losing entries almost immediately.**
    - His first major short was Host America after a misleading Walmart-related press release drove the stock from around $3 to $15 or $16.
    - Trading was halted for weeks, and the stock reopened near $5 after the company corrected its claims, nearly tripling his account overnight.
    - He screened nightly for the largest winners over one, three, and five days, looking for multi-day or multi-week runners that were peaking.
    - He was often 200% short, using maximum margin, but exited if the stock made a new high or failed to move in his favor quickly.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--043"></span>**Goedeker’s defining risk rule is that a losing entry proves the entry wrong, so he exits rather than averaging up into a short squeeze.**
    - In chat-room examples, other traders added to shorts as stocks rose from $10 to $12 or $13, while Goedeker had already covered near $10.50.
    - He waits for stocks to break before shorting rather than selling into strength, because he wants the first entry to be green.
    - If stopped out, he may wait for a new breakdown and try again, usually no more than three or four times in practice.
    - He describes preferred losses as paper cuts and the career-ending loss as a guillotine cut.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--044"></span>**Goedeker’s 2011 trade review transformed his performance because he discovered that a tiny minority of A+ trades made the money while oversized mediocre trades caused most losses.**
    - After years of making roughly $500,000 annually while keeping only $100,000 in the trading account, he analyzed about 500 tickers from the prior year.
    - Only about 15 tickers generated big profits of $75,000 to $100,000 each, while over 400 had trivial results and the remaining significant losers often lacked a real setup.
    - He began sizing A+ trades larger and cutting B and C setup size by roughly 90%.
    - This was the first year he incorporated correct position sizing, and profits exceeded $1 million thereafter.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--045"></span>**Goedeker accepts that he likes trading too much to avoid suboptimal trades, but he neutralizes the damage by making them tiny and reserving meaningful size for A+ setups.**
    - A+ setups are infrequent, often only about one per month, so he continued taking smaller trades for activity, learning, and entertainment.
    - He tried news trades, breakouts, larger-cap shorts, and other methods, but concluded no trader can be great at everything.
    - His B and C trades were close to flat over his career because their size was too small to matter.
    - He explicitly calls his trading compulsion an addiction that helps his career but makes it hard to switch off mentally.
* <span class="pull-quote">I learned early on that if my entry is not right, then it’s wrong.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--046"></span>**Goedeker moved from active shorting to option premium selling because it offered a less screen-intensive way to trade, but he treats it as dangerous and suitable only with exceptional risk instincts.**
    - In 2016, he learned option selling from a chat-room trader in exchange for teaching his short-selling style.
    - He began with $200,000, quickly increased to $1 million and then $2 million, and made about $1 million in his first year trading options.
    - He sells mostly very deep out-of-the-money puts and some calls, focusing on stable mega-cap names such as Apple, Amazon, and Microsoft.
    - He avoids stocks like GameStop after losing $1 million selling calls in the type of stock that can rise hundreds of percent quickly.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--047"></span>**Goedeker’s option-selling risk management uses stock selection, distance from the market, time-to-expiration diversification, hedging, and the assumption that anything can happen.**
    - For two-week puts he may sell strikes around 10% below market; for 30-day options 20% to 25% below; for six-month options around 50% below.
    - He sells about 80% puts and 20% calls, having learned that calls can create trouble much faster.
    - After President Trump’s April 2025 “Liberation Day” tariff shock, he immediately bought 800 to 900 June SPY puts to hedge the entire portfolio while down only about $100,000.
    - "The one thing I will never say is that something can’t happen." —Phil Goedeker
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--048"></span>**Goedeker separates trading account risk from personal wealth risk, keeping only about 10% of total assets in the trading account to survive an unhedgeable crash.**
    - He acknowledges that an October 19, 1987-style Monday crash could wipe out or exceed the value of his short option account.
    - His defense is structural rather than tactical: most of his wealth is outside the account.
    - His primary goal each year is capital preservation, not maximizing profit targets.
    - He sets low annual financial goals because high targets can cause overtrading when the market does not provide setups.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--049"></span>**Goedeker’s best short-selling opportunities occur in bull markets because euphoria creates small-cap overextensions that later fail, contrary to the belief that short sellers need bear markets.**
    - In 2020, small-cap stocks sometimes gapped up over 1,000% in the morning and failed in the last hour, creating ideal shorts.
    - He says bear markets do not produce the same extreme overbought small-cap shorting opportunities.
    - He worries public discussion of the strategy could crowd it briefly, but expects inexperienced traders to fail and leave.
    - "Many people believe that you can’t make money going short in a bull market. The reality is exactly the opposite." —Phil Goedeker
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--050"></span>**Goedeker’s durability comes from consistency within a niche and adaptation when market structure changes, especially borrow costs and intraday behavior in small caps.**
    - He stayed consistent in his niche of shorting small caps for roughly a decade.
    - As borrow rates rose and brokers turned stock lending into a business, he shortened holding periods to avoid costs.
    - When early gap-up patterns shifted from morning rollovers to holding gains into afternoon, he changed timing rather than insisting on the old pattern.
    - His principle is to stay consistent while a methodology works, but adapt when market behavior changes.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--051"></span>**Goedeker attributes success to love of the game, thousands of hours of self-directed chart study, and a rule-oriented focus on still being around decades later.**
    - He printed charts of big annual winners and their tops, kept them in binders, and compared current charts to past examples.
    - He says successful traders he knows are addicted to markets and willing to research for 10 hours a day because they enjoy the process.
    - He doubts traders can succeed if money is the only motivation and they do not love the research and pattern-discovery process.
    - He eventually bought the Lake of the Ozarks property that originally symbolized the lifestyle he wanted.

## Kelvin Chiu

Chiu’s chapter explains how a fundamentally trained commodity trader learned to trade both positive and negative asymmetry by combining historical frameworks, timing, sizing, and emotional reframing. His evolution shows that risk-adjusted excellence can be too conservative if it suppresses high-conviction opportunity, but increasing risk safely requires a process strong enough to survive drawdowns and mistakes.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--052"></span>**Kelvin Chiu’s academic perfection initially hurt his trading because trading requires being wrong often, operating without structure, and developing resilience after failure.**
    - Chiu grew up in Hong Kong, excelled academically, and graduated as one of the city’s top students before attending Cambridge.
    - He entered Goldman Sachs with perfect academic credentials, expecting them to translate into trading success.
    - In 2010 and 2011, he lost just under $1 million each year while trying fundamentals, technicals, headlines, brokers, and piggybacking without a real edge.
    - He concluded that high expectations can create low resilience because students accustomed to straight As have little practice being wrong.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--053"></span>**Losing his Goldman trading book became a turning point because it removed P&L pressure and let Chiu learn commodity fundamentals, spread trading, and process discipline from experienced traders.**
    - After two poor years, a senior commodities partner merged Chiu’s book with a sugar trader’s book and made him junior trader.
    - The change hurt his ego but reduced fear and allowed curiosity to return.
    - He learned to analyze aggregate supply and demand and compare current fundamentals to historical analogues.
    - He also began tracking and reviewing his trades closely, a practice he retained throughout his career.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--054"></span>**Chiu settled on agricultural calendar spreads and relative-value trades because they matched his temperament better than options or outright speculation.**
    - Calendar spreads involve buying one futures month and selling another in the same commodity; relative-value trades involve one related market versus another.
    - He preferred grains and oilseeds because annual crop cycles force supply and demand toward balance within each crop year.
    - He left Goldman in 2014 because the Volcker Rule pushed the desk toward client business rather than proprietary trading.
    - At Vitol in Singapore, he could focus exclusively on prop trading and sometimes experienced daily swings of millions of dollars.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--055"></span>**The 2016 Kansas City versus Chicago wheat spread illustrates Chiu’s preferred convex trade: buy a spread near a boundary condition where downside is limited and upside is meaningful.**
    - A huge hard red winter wheat crop pushed Kansas City wheat to an unusually large discount to Chicago wheat.
    - The discount reached a level where commercial firms could buy Kansas City wheat, ship it to Chicago, and deliver it profitably against the Chicago contract.
    - This arbitrage implied buying Kansas City futures and selling Chicago futures in large size.
    - Chiu classifies such positions as convex because risk is constrained by economic boundary conditions while upside can be substantial.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--056"></span>**The 2016 soybean meal loss taught Chiu to fear concave trades, where profit is limited but losses can expand far beyond the original expectation.**
    - He shorted old-crop July soybean meal versus long new-crop December meal because the spread’s widening seemed unjustified by meal fundamentals.
    - He entered near 14, watched the spread reach 45, and exited at an average around 38 for a loss near $10 million.
    - The spread later collapsed back to 2, but the emotional and financial damage had already forced him out.
    - The experience led him to avoid trades whose downside tail was larger than the upside.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--057"></span>**Going independent in 2018 initially made Chiu focus on smooth, positive-asymmetry trades, but a missed 2019 corn opportunity convinced him he was sacrificing too much P&L for pristine return/risk statistics.**
    - Independent traders he met at US conferences inspired him to save capital and trade for himself with autonomy.
    - He left Vitol before reaching his $10 million savings target, with family also investing about $1 million.
    - In 2019, he passed on shorting an elevated July/December corn spread because it was concave, despite believing the balance sheet offered a wide margin of safety.
    - Missing that profitable trade made him decide to take concave trades again, but only with better timing and risk management.
* <span class="pull-quote">Good trading is an exercise in long periods of relative inactivity punctuated by short, intense periods of heavy risk-taking.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--058"></span>**Chiu’s 2022 Russia-Ukraine wheat spread shows how he reintroduced concave trades by waiting for a top, trading near expiration, and using conviction stops rather than price stops.**
    - After Russia invaded Ukraine, US wheat futures surged more than 50% with multiple limit-up days, but exports from Russia and Ukraine continued and US wheat priced itself out of demand.
    - He expressed the short view through short May and long July Chicago wheat, expecting the May contract to weaken into expiration.
    - He waited until price action weakened rather than trying to pick the top, then added as the spread declined and expiration approached.
    - A conviction stop means reducing or exiting when new information weakens the thesis, not when an arbitrary price is hit.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--059"></span>**CBT changed Chiu’s drawdown psychology by shifting self-worth away from P&L and toward values of growth, resilience, and courage.**
    - A 2021 drawdown of over 25% made him realize he still handled drawdowns poorly.
    - Cognitive behavioral therapy helped him identify the limiting belief that his self-worth equaled his achievements.
    - He reframed success as adhering to process and learning, not being defined by any single trade outcome.
    - "Because when I put on a trade, I either make money or learn." —Kelvin Chiu
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--060"></span>**Chiu expanded beyond agricultural futures after challenging the limiting belief that his edge applied only to grains, but he uses technical analysis for outright trades because fundamentals alone can destroy risk control.**
    - CBT helped him believe his analytical tools and intuition could apply to equities, copper, EU carbon allowances, and other markets.
    - For outright positions, he says fundamentals determine conviction and size, while technical analysis controls entry and exit.
    - He warns that if fundamentals are unchanged as price moves against a position, the logic of adding can lead to catastrophic losses.
    - Unexpected exogenous events such as Covid-19 or President Trump’s tariff announcement can overturn even correct fundamental analysis.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--061"></span>**Chiu’s main edge increasingly lies in sizing high-conviction trades aggressively while staying emotionally centered enough to listen to signal fear.**
    - He distinguishes hit ratio, power, and sizing, and says his edge lives primarily in sizing.
    - He describes great trading as long relative inactivity punctuated by short, intense risk-taking when the stars align.
    - A 2023 July/September corn spread loss of about 13% taught him to respect signal fear when a trade’s dominant factor changes, in that case from old-crop tightness to weather risk.
    - He uses meditation, yoga, exercise, ice baths, and journaling to keep emotions between overconfidence and desperation.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--062"></span>**Chiu’s analytical method filters public information into dominant drivers, separating regime changes that shock markets from persistent themes that trend over time.**
    - He does not claim better information; he filters common information to identify the factor likely to drive prices over the next month or two.
    - The 2022 wheat overshoot after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine is a regime-change trade where he sold a forced short-covering move.
    - Long US equities in 2020 and 2021 is a persistent-theme trade based on aggressive fiscal and monetary stimulus.
    - He says the best traders are historians who know how markets responded before while still discerning how this time is different.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--063"></span>**As Chiu’s capital grew, he began building a multi-strategy platform because the niche agricultural strategies that made him successful do not scale indefinitely.**
    - He tried a traditional family-office allocation but found buy-and-hold investing incompatible with his trader temperament.
    - He looked to scalable traders and firms such as Stanley Druckenmiller, Ken Griffin, Steve Cohen, and Israel Englander for models.
    - He has hired five traders, preferring self-funded traders with ingrained risk management, generalist instincts, and technical-analysis risk controls.
    - He is exploring small- and mid-cap Japanese equities and uranium as large opportunity themes as of May 2025.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--064"></span>**Chiu’s final lessons emphasize experimentation, gradual scaling, intuition, and resilience as acceptance of both losses and mistakes.**
    - He advises beginners to test strategies small, commit to one with edge and personal fit, scale risk gradually, and keep evolving.
    - His intuition helped him exit European carbon allowances after Russia invaded Ukraine when the market failed to rally on seemingly bullish news.
    - He defines resilience as accepting losses and mistakes, learning the lesson, forgiving oneself, and preventing disappointment from damaging subsequent trades.
    - "When you have high conviction, go big." —Kelvin Chiu

## Jason Berry

Berry’s chapter explains an unusually smooth prop-trading record through obsessive risk control, short-term edge discovery, relative-value trading, and constant renewal of strategies before old edges decay. His consistency comes from treating trading as a craft of preparation, review, innovation, and rapid loss cutting rather than from one secret setup.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--065"></span>**Jason Berry’s early trading success came from instinctively cutting losers even before he understood markets, a reflex that later became the foundation of his consistency.**
    - After a brief LIFFE floor-clerk stint, he traded a price-band breakout system on European markets from 2 a.m. to noon.
    - He made only about $3,000, but was the only positive trader among similar London traders using the system.
    - He attributes part of his early aptitude to video games, which trained focus, screen tolerance, hand-eye coordination, and reactions to stress.
    - "I got out of my losers. I didn’t hope. I didn’t get attached to anything." —Jason Berry
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--066"></span>**Berry’s prop-trading method is built around very short-term momentum and trades that work immediately, which he teaches as finding the “fantastic fill feeling.”**
    - He trades trends on 1-, 2-, and 5-minute bars and wants trades that instantly move in his direction.
    - A partial fill followed by the market running away is one sign of a high-quality trade.
    - He trades specific markets only during times of day when they tend to trend rather than chop.
    - He says he can tell when a market is behaving normally and when it is not because he has watched the same products at the same times for years.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--067"></span>**Berry’s edge is not a single strategy but a portfolio of researched short-term inefficiencies, many of which disappeared as technology, market structure, and competition changed.**
    - Obsolete examples include lag trades between NASDAQ and S&P futures after economic news, Nokia versus Eurostoxx 50, and S&P minis versus SPY using counterparty information.
    - He says the VIX trade that once generated about half his monthly P&L disappeared abruptly when the Ukraine war began in 2022.
    - He views edge erosion as normal and believes traders need a trainee mindset to keep replacing old alpha with new alpha.
    - "Nothing that I traded at the beginning of my career is relevant today." —Jason Berry
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--068"></span>**Berry prepares for market-moving news by writing scenario plans in advance, allowing him to respond instantly and exploit distorted initial reactions.**
    - He considers cases such as Trump imposing different tariff levels on the EU, China attacking Taiwan, or the Russia-Ukraine war ending.
    - Because much of his trading is relative value, he evaluates how each scenario should affect multiple markets relative to one another.
    - If an initial reaction overpays the more volatile market, he may take the opposite spread, such as buying S&P and selling NASDAQ if NASDAQ overreacts.
    - Preparation prevents him from doing analysis for the first time while markets are in turmoil.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--069"></span>**Daily and monthly review are central to Berry’s process because they convert trading experience into specific improvements in sizing, psychology, market selection, and strategy quality.**
    - At the end of each day, he writes a summary of trades seen, trades taken, results, size, holding period, management, and psychology.
    - He rereads the report before the next session to focus on what needs improvement.
    - Monthly reviews identify whether weak P&L came from poor execution, forcing trades, low opportunity, sizing errors, or strategy decay.
    - He also prints and annotates charts after market-moving news to build a personal historical record.
* <span class="pull-quote">If you don’t enjoy the process of innovation and research, digging through the markets to find an edge, you’re probably in the wrong business.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--070"></span>**Berry’s smooth equity curve comes from grinding many small low-risk trades with high reward/risk and refusing to sit in offside positions.**
    - From 2012 onward, he had only five losing months, with the worst monthly loss less than his average monthly return.
    - He trades many small price moves throughout the day rather than relying on a few large directional bets.
    - His best months are about 50 times larger than his worst months because losing trades are cut quickly.
    - He says his best trading often occurs in panics and bear markets because volatility creates opportunity, but size must be reduced as stops widen.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--071"></span>**Berry’s returns did not scale as much as the smoothness suggests because some strategies have limited capacity and he shares trade volume with other Positive Equity traders.**
    - His account allocation was small, around €100,000 through 2023 and €200,000 thereafter, because his short holding periods and moderate size did not require more capital.
    - He admits that he could have done better at increasing size in scalable trades.
    - He limits his own size in capacity-constrained trades when other Positive Equity traders use the same edges.
    - He wonders whether psychological limits on what he thinks he deserves may have constrained his earnings.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--072"></span>**Berry’s three Cs framework teaches traders to progress from controlling losses, to consistent sideways-to-up P&L, to confidence that permits larger size and broader opportunity.**
    - Trainees first learn to limit the loss on each trade to a predefined maximum so daily P&L stops going straight down.
    - After control comes consistency, where traders generate repeatable profitability in chosen markets.
    - Only after confidence can traders trade larger, hold longer, add to winners, and expand markets.
    - Berry applies the same framework to himself by retreating to markets where he has control when results become erratic.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--073"></span>**Berry’s training program treats trading as a multi-year apprenticeship that combines historical market study, simulation, personal edge discovery, and ongoing risk oversight.**
    - He has trained well over 200 traders and believes it takes roughly 10,000 hours to become proficient.
    - Trainees study how markets reacted to historical events, then trade in simulation for nine to 18 months before earning live trading.
    - Live trading requires a three-month average P&L threshold from daily grind rather than one-off spikes, and it can be revoked if edge does not transfer.
    - The full training program often takes two to three years, with current markets requiring a longer runway than earlier years.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--074"></span>**Berry hires and develops traders based on competitive temperament, grit, and personality rather than existing trading knowledge, because methods can be taught but personality cannot.**
    - A candidates have succeeded in competitive settings where they had to outwit or outplay others and have shown hard work, grit, and commitment to trading.
    - The firm improved success rates after tightening hiring standards and rejecting too many “maybe” candidates.
    - Traders who fear trading or cannot cut losers are easy to identify early.
    - He says traders do not age out; they effort out.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--075"></span>**Berry’s closing caveat is that even a remarkably consistent trading career imposes physical, psychological, and family costs because the market can demand attention at any hour.**
    - He trades around global events such as Putin attacking Ukraine at night and Trump posting market-moving messages near bedtime or wakeup.
    - He says a supportive spouse or life partner is essential because trading disrupts normal life.
    - He has experienced large losers that nearly made him pass out from stress despite his overall smooth record.
    - "I’m 50 and competing with 22-year-olds. I love it." —Jason Berry

## Kenny Sharkness

Sharkness’s chapter argues that constant tactical change can work when it is anchored by journaling, risk control, process goals, collaboration, and the ability to size rare high-expectancy opportunities far larger than ordinary trades. His success comes from matching strategies to changing regimes while using self-awareness to manage impatience and prevent small experiments from becoming dangerous.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--076"></span>**Kenny Sharkness entered trading through a prop-firm training path and gradually became profitable by absorbing methods from stronger traders and adapting them to his own multitasking style.**
    - After studying finance at Ithaca College, he moved to Hoboken and paid about $6,000 for SMB’s trader training program.
    - His parents funded living expenses, allowing him to focus on trading rather than holding a second job.
    - He learned from Gilberto Mendez at SMB, especially risk management, classic chart patterns, and checking multiple timeframes before acting.
    - He became profitable after about 10 months, first making about $40,000 annually before scaling over time.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--077"></span>**Sharkness’s earliest profitable style used tight stops and short-biased momentum or mean-reversion trades, reflecting a natural instinct that trades should work almost immediately.**
    - He traded tick charts at first and rarely held positions longer than an hour.
    - His main early strategy was high-volume breakouts from tight consolidation, often with stops so tight that he would enter and exit the same stock multiple times in a day.
    - He gravitated toward shorts because successful short trades often move quickly, fitting his impatience.
    - He was winning less than 50% of the time, but larger wins offset frequent small losses.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--078"></span>**The parabolic short strategy became an early specialty for Sharkness, but rising borrow costs and crowding forced him to reduce reliance on small-cap shorts as his account grew.**
    - He learned the strategy from Peter To and confirmed it by reviewing roughly 1,000 daily charts per day.
    - He looked for stocks several average daily true ranges above VWAP, then often shorted when they broke back below VWAP.
    - By around 2017, tighter and more expensive locates changed the strategy, making overnight shorts less safe and less affordable.
    - As his account grew, small-cap shorts also stopped moving the needle, pushing him toward scalable strategies.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--079"></span>**The SMB-Kershner merger expanded Sharkness’s view of what was possible by exposing him to traders making $25,000 to $50,000 per day and to a wider menu of scalable event and gimmick trades.**
    - Before the merger, he thought Gilberto Mendez making around $2,000 a day was extraordinary.
    - Kershner traders used strategies around computer errors, fat-finger anomalies, end-of-day order imbalances, index rebalances, IPO openings, and opening spikes without news.
    - Seeing larger P&L changed his mindset about scale and goals.
    - The internal system let him view accepted traders’ executions, giving him a way to learn how bigger traders actually implemented ideas.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--080"></span>**Sharkness’s unusual edge is the ability to run many strategies across timeframes, but he recognizes that most annual profits still come from a handful of large trades sized far above the rest.**
    - He trades strategies suited to trending, range-bound, overbought, and oversold environments.
    - He says most of his profits in a given year come from only five or six trading days.
    - He often has many small strategies active, but only one trade may have truly significant size.
    - He estimates that roughly 80% or more of profits come from trades where he deliberately sizes up.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--081"></span>**Sharkness manages impatience through sizing, journaling, and process goals rather than trying to become a different kind of person.**
    - He maintains a playbook of the best trade each day and an anti-playbook of the worst trade each day, following Mike Bellafiore’s SMB framework.
    - Weekly and monthly reviews identify patterns, mistakes, and concrete goals such as reducing overeagerness or pausing before exiting winners too early.
    - He worked with Dr. Brett Steenbarger to improve impatience, focus on strengths, and take larger positions when great setups appeared.
    - He uses rewards for process adherence, distinguishing motivational long-term goals from harmful short-term P&L targets.
* <span class="pull-quote">Everything works sometimes, and nothing works all the time.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--082"></span>**Sharkness’s worst losses reinforced the importance of not adding to losers and of treating short squeezes as potentially unbounded.**
    - In KBIO, he shorted a $2 stock based on valuation, watched it rise on Martin Shkreli’s reverse-merger plan, added to the losing position, and exited near $15 for a roughly $150,000 loss.
    - The stock later rose to around $40 before eventually collapsing, showing that being ultimately right would not have saved poor risk management.
    - In AMC in 2020, he was down $3 million on a short the day before the top but held within a weekly risk framework and recovered when the stock collapsed.
    - KBIO was pivotal because making back the loss over the following months restored confidence in his trading ability.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--083"></span>**Sharkness increasingly combines fundamentals, price action, sentiment, options, and catalysts to structure dynamic trades around extreme moves rather than static stock positions.**
    - He began incorporating fundamentals after Kershner traders explained their value in identifying catalysts and holding longer as his account grew.
    - He studies catalysts such as earnings, secondary offerings, developer conferences, legal developments, and macro shocks.
    - His CRWV plan used long calls, short puts, and potential short stock to exploit a possible final squeeze while limiting risk and managing volatility exposure.
    - He buys calls when implied volatility is cheaper, sells puts after volatility expands, and shorts stock only after the price reaches a better risk/reward zone.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--084"></span>**His best current trades often arise from extreme “rubber band” price velocity, whether on the short side after parabolic rallies or the long side after panic selloffs.**
    - SMCI was his best trade in 2024 after a parabolic move higher created a major short opportunity.
    - In 2025, most profits came from playing the bounce after the post-tariff selloff rather than from shorts.
    - He watches for two or three days of extreme velocity and a chart that looks stretched to its maximum.
    - Extreme sentiment and extreme price movement usually occur together in his framework.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--085"></span>**Sharkness’s 2022 drawdown came from failing to adapt after two unusually favorable years, showing that traders cannot force opportunity when market follow-through disappears.**
    - After great years in 2020 and 2021, he assumed the favorable environment might continue and took suboptimal trades when conditions deteriorated.
    - In 2022, stocks often appeared ready to run one day and then reversed sharply, creating poor follow-through for his style.
    - He eventually cut size sharply until he regained consistency and a good trade restored confidence.
    - He says large drawdowns often follow strong runs because ego and the desire to keep winning distort trade selection.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--086"></span>**Collaboration is central to Sharkness’s evolution because no trader can see every catalyst, sector, or strategy alone.**
    - He developed a collaborative rivalry with Lance Breitstein, learning intraday mean reversion and V-bottom trades from him.
    - He trains a team of five or six traders, sharing what he is doing while encouraging independent thinking.
    - Mentees sometimes improve his process by asking why he did not add or adjust at specific points.
    - He values firmwide idea sharing from macro specialists, gene-therapy specialists, and other teams because one person has limited bandwidth.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--087"></span>**Sharkness’s advice to beginners is to move slowly, prove edge at small size, find a mentor whose style fits, and accept that early trading will be uncomfortable.**
    - He says new traders should expect to be bad at first and should not scale until they have evidence of consistent profitability.
    - A mentor can help articulate an edge, but copying a mismatched style is dangerous.
    - He believes most top traders are competitive, growth-oriented, humble enough to be wrong, and often former gamers who treat markets as a game.
    - "Trading is a marathon, not a sprint." —Kenny Sharkness

## Rick Bandazian Jr.

Bandazian’s chapter shows that a non-scalable niche can still produce extraordinary risk-adjusted returns when a trader combines domain expertise, preparation, speed, and extreme risk aversion. His event-driven edge survives against machines because nuanced merger and corporate-action interpretation still rewards a prepared human who knows what a headline really means and exits within minutes.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--088"></span>**Rick Bandazian moved from banking and event-driven analysis to trading only after discovering that rapid headline interpretation could produce immediate P&L where his prior event work had stalled.**
    - He began in syndicated finance at J.P. Morgan, moved to equity capital markets, then became an event-driven analyst at First New York Securities.
    - He later worked on merger arbitrage at the National Bank of Canada before the 2008 crisis caused the bank to shut the prop desk.
    - Back at First New York, he received his own roughly $2.5 million trading line while also serving as analyst for a merger-arb PM.
    - Two quick wins on buyout-talk headlines made him abandon middling event-driven trades and focus on headline trading.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--089"></span>**Bandazian avoided the usual early trader blow-up because his extreme risk aversion made him exit within minutes when a trade failed to behave as expected.**
    - He says he cannot take pain and rarely holds overnight positions because event-driven outcomes are often binary and gap-prone.
    - His typical week consists of small wins and losses, scratches, and occasional larger gains when one event works cleanly.
    - His hard P&L stop is generally one unit, or $2,500, though illiquid pre-market conditions can create slippage beyond that.
    - His worst loss was about $12,000 in Allergan after he bought an Icahn-related headline that turned out to be old news.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--090"></span>**Bandazian’s original simple headline edge disappeared between 2016 and 2018 as bots became too fast, forcing him into more nuanced events where preparation matters more than raw speed.**
    - Before bots dominated, he could buy or sell simple newswire headlines ahead of the broader market.
    - By 2018, the simple headline game was over because algorithms reacted faster than any human to one-line news.
    - He adapted by building watch lists after announced deals, researching each company, and preparing responses for likely future catalysts.
    - He tracks time and sales after missed trades to understand whether information arrived earlier, how price moved before Bloomberg, and whether algos or other traders beat him.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--091"></span>**His durable edge in mergers and corporate actions comes from knowing which details matter before a filing or press release arrives, then acting when the market misprices the nuance.**
    - He has read merger agreements and proxy statements for nearly 20 years and tracks 75 to 100 announced deals at a time.
    - He categorizes deals by buyer type, tender or merger structure, regulatory approval, domicile, and other factors that affect fair value.
    - He maintains a database of historical bids to estimate where a stock should trade under specific scenarios.
    - If a stock trades away from his fair-value estimate after a release, he enters quickly and exits once the easy mispricing is corrected.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--092"></span>**The WSR trade shows Bandazian profiting when traders or algos overreact to a bid without understanding corporate-law context and company history.**
    - Whitestone REIT had attracted activist interest, so it was already on his list when a REIT-focused private-equity firm with a 9% stake bid $14 per share.
    - The stock surged, but Bandazian shorted because WSR was incorporated in Maryland, where companies have more ability to ignore activists than in Delaware.
    - WSR also had a history of rejecting activist approaches, making the bid less meaningful than the headline suggested.
    - The market figured this out within about 20 to 30 minutes, and the stock came back down.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--093"></span>**The Twitter trade demonstrates how SEC filings can still create human edge because bots struggle with buried, multi-contingent information and deal context.**
    - After Elon Musk took a Twitter stake in 2022, Bandazian researched whether Musk intended to buy the company and how arbs would price the risk.
    - When Musk filed a 13D around 6 a.m., Bandazian bought before the price moved too far and offered stock near his estimate of a 10% discount to the bid.
    - He argues bots excel at one-line headlines but struggle when the crucial details are buried in a filing and require judgment about deal complexity.
    - His truck license plate is 13D, reflecting how central filings are to his process.
* <span class="pull-quote">Every second that passes while I’m in a trade, I’m aware that my odds of making a profit decline.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--094"></span>**The Foot Locker and Humana examples show Bandazian’s strategy of preparing for either confirmation or denial headlines, not predicting the deal outcome beforehand.**
    - For Foot Locker in May 2025, he researched Dick’s Sporting Goods’ rumored $24 per share bid, antitrust issues, and deal timing before the official press release.
    - A buried election clause in the release meant FL could trade above a generic $24 model, and he bought quickly before other arbs processed it.
    - For Cigna-Humana in late 2024, he prepared to short HUM if Cigna denied a deal and acted when the press release confirmed no transaction.
    - He says he has no edge in predicting whether deals happen; his edge is in responding to releases faster and better prepared.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--095"></span>**Bandazian’s time stops are as important as price stops because his edge decays as other traders discover the information.**
    - He thinks of trades in hourglass terms: when he is first, almost all the edge is still available, but every passing second transfers edge away.
    - Most trades are exited within three or four minutes if they do not work; a 30-minute hold is long for him.
    - Winners average about five minutes, while losers are shorter.
    - If a stock spikes and pauses, he interprets the tape as a contest between waiting for the next buyer and being first out before others exit.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--096"></span>**Bandazian’s extraordinary consistency is possible partly because his strategy is small, narrow, and non-scalable, and because he refuses to use all the capital available to him.**
    - He has had no down months over 11 years while repeatedly withdrawing profits from an original $25,000 account.
    - He kept roughly a $2.5 million line because he did not need or want more capital for his style.
    - He estimates that a less risk-averse version of himself might make three or four times more, but with worse fills and more discomfort.
    - His approach often requires limited volume before a stock halts or reaches its expected level, capping scalability.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--097"></span>**His workday shows the cost of his edge: being prepared before others requires near-total vigilance from pre-market to evening.**
    - He checks news at 3:45 a.m., may return to sleep briefly, and is usually back at the desk before 5:45 a.m.
    - He watches releases all day, leaves around 4:30 p.m. if quiet, but keeps phone alerts active and often checks again from 7 p.m. to 8 p.m.
    - About two-thirds of recent trades occur outside regular stock-market hours, where algos tend to be less dominant and nuance may be less priced.
    - He is usually in bed by 8:30 p.m. to repeat the schedule.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--098"></span>**Bandazian’s volunteer firefighting reflects a parallel identity built around service, risk, and community rather than financial status.**
    - He has served as a volunteer firefighter for about 10 years.
    - He describes firefighting as a high calling because it involves risking oneself for one’s community.
    - If a life-or-death call arrives while trading, he exits positions and answers, while lesser calls may wait if he is engaged in a trade or release watch.
    - He sleeps at the fire station a few times per month when possible, accepting that he may miss trades.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--099"></span>**Bandazian advises traders to find a niche that fits their personality and is different from the crowd, because trading without edge is only entertainment with negative expectancy.**
    - He tells firefighters who ask about trading that his method is unlikely to fit most people and that Market Wizards is useful because it shows many different paths.
    - He recommends doing something different from everyone else rather than becoming the millionth person trying to predict Jerome Powell and the Federal Reserve.
    - He is developing a semi-systematic macro strategy based on “offsides” positioning, inspired by Jason Shapiro’s Unknown Market Wizards interview, to reduce dependence on screen time.
    - "Trading without an edge is OK if you’re doing it for entertainment, but it is a losing proposition." —Rick Bandazian Jr.

## Epilogue

The epilogue distills the book’s interviews into a warning that markets punish human emotion, rule-breaking, and fantasies of quick wealth, while rewarding rare traders whose passion, talent, resilience, and discipline make total commitment natural. It argues that readers should use the Market Wizards not as templates to copy but as mirrors for deciding what they truly want from markets and whether trading fits their nature.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--100"></span>**The speculator-at-heaven story frames the central emotional problem of trading: even someone who creates a rumor can still chase it from fear of missing out.**
    - The speculator tricks heavenly traders by shouting that tomorrow’s closing prices are available in hell, then follows them because the rumor might be true.
    - The story illustrates how greed, fear, and FOMO can override knowledge and judgment.
    - The epilogue connects this emotional reflex to real trader errors throughout the book.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--101"></span>**Russo’s Carvana loss is used as the clearest example that emotional trades can cause huge damage, while returning to rules can prevent total ruin.**
    - Russo shorted Carvana heavily despite lacking his normal conditions and violating his risk rules.
    - A bullish earnings report doubled the already large loss, eventually leading him to liquidate the entire position for a $50 million loss.
    - The exit saved him because continuing to ignore rules would have increased the loss roughly ninefold or enough to wipe out the account.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--102"></span>**Becoming a Market Wizard is limited by both innate traits and total commitment, so most people who want market riches do not actually want the life required to achieve them.**
    - The epilogue compares trading talent to elite athletic ability: not everyone has the emotional composition or innate skill required.
    - The interviewed traders were glued to screens, researched outside market hours, and treated markets as an all-consuming craft.
    - Passion for trading itself, not merely desire for money, is presented as necessary but not sufficient.
    - "Very few people possess the passion and the willingness to make the sacrifices that are required to achieve market wizardry." —Jack D. Schwager
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--103"></span>**Schwager uses his own life to argue that actions reveal true desires: he wanted to be a successful writer more than he wanted to be a full-time trader.**
    - He says he never devoted even one full week to trying to be a full-time trader, despite writing extensively about markets.
    - He did take a year’s sabbatical to write A Complete Guide to the Futures Markets, a 740-page analytical text with limited commercial prospects.
    - He contrasts his confidence about writing the best futures markets book with his lack of similar confidence about trading.
    - The example supports Ed Seykota’s statement that people get what they want out of markets.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--104"></span>**The final lesson is introspective rather than prescriptive: readers should define what they want from markets and use the interviews to progress toward that individual goal.**
    - Some readers may be suited to pursue Market Wizard-level performance, while others may want trading as a profitable hobby or may be better served by avoiding active trading.
    - The epilogue does not present one universal method, because the book’s traders succeeded through different markets, timeframes, and personalities.
    - The common lessons are discipline, emotional awareness, risk management, edge, and alignment between method and temperament.
    - "If you are an aspiring trader, you need to examine what you want out of the markets, which is an individual matter." —Jack D. Schwager
