## The Logic of Power

Power is misconceived when reduced to causality, coercion, or the neutralization of will; in truth it is a phenomenon of form and mediation in which the ego continues itself in the alter, so that its highest and most stable form coincides with the alter's freely willed 'Yes' rather than with violence.

* <span id="what-is-power-001"></span>**Power is a phenomenon pertaining to form, not causality: the mark of superior power is that those subjected to it want what the holder wants and affirm it with an emphatic 'Yes!' as their own action, so power operates like a field in which a body moves of its own accord rather than a mechanical push.**
    - "Not the inner 'No', but the emphatic 'Yes!' is the response to a superior power." —Byung-Chul Han
    - Power without violence takes its place in the soul of the other, influencing the environment of the alter's actions so that the alter voluntarily decides for the ego's will even without threat of sanctions.
* <span id="what-is-power-002"></span>**Power exists on a scale of mediation whose two end points are freedom and violence: increasing mediation generates more freedom and stability, while where mediation is reduced to nil power turns into violence and the alter becomes a passive thing on which power in the proper sense cannot be exerted.**
    - Power creates a continuity of self, allowing the ego to be with him- or herself in the other; obedience out of free choice makes this continuity stable, while coercion makes it fragile due to absence of mediation.
    - The king has both a mortal natural body and a politico-theological body coextensive with his empire; the loss of power shrinks him to a 'shabby piece of meat' and is experienced as a kind of death.
* <span id="what-is-power-003"></span>**Against Luhmann, Han argues power as a communicative catalyst that raises the probability of 'yes' is productive rather than repressive, but Luhmann wrongly ties power to a sanction both sides want to avoid and to a 'yes, alright then'; a truly powerful holder elicits enthusiasm and can freely execute sanctions, so free power is not an oxymoron.**
    - "The execution of physical violence is not an application of power, but an expression of its failure." —Niklas Luhmann
    - For Luhmann power always depends on an oblique glance at an avoidable sanction; for Han the ego's power peaks in an emphatic 'yes' that contains no trace of 'yes, alright then'.
    - Han rejects Luhmann's thesis that intensifying relations simultaneously increases the power of superior and subordinate; this raises efficiency and productivity through trust, not power.
* <span id="what-is-power-004"></span>**Power is dialectical and spatial rather than merely hierarchical and linear: the more power one holds the more one depends on subordinates and advisors, producing a structural dispersal of power, and often what is absent has more power than what is present, opening a space that gives actions direction before any conscious selection.**
    - Spatial power works like gravitation, arranging dispersed forces into a form and founding a location that precedes individual power relations, which Luhmann's linear model of conscious action selection cannot capture.
    - Even the very weakest can turn powerlessness into power through skilful use of cultural norms, and reciprocal dependence makes the ego's need for cooperation a source of power for the alter.
* <span id="what-is-power-005"></span>**Naked violence, severed from any communicative context, aims at the complete annihilation of alterity — the extinguishing of the other's doing, will, freedom, and dignity — and is thus distinct from power, which is a relation requiring the alter's minimal freedom to say yes or no.**
    - Canetti's archaic mana passes magically from a slain enemy to the victor without any communicative mediation, reifying power into an ownable substance; but killing the other ends the power relation, since power in the true sense is the absence of the fight to the death.
    - Canetti's cat-and-mouse image treats space, hope, watchfulness and destructive intent as 'power itself', but obsessed with death he forgets that power does not simply kill — it most of all lets live and provides time and space.

## The Semantics of Power

Unlike naked violence, which is stripped of all sense, power is eloquent: it inscribes itself into a hermeneutic horizon and founds a continuum of sense, achieving its greatest stability when it disappears into the taken-for-granted, the habitual, and the 'they' — power shines in its own absence.

* <span id="what-is-power-006"></span>**Following Nietzsche, sense is power: naming is a right of the master and every word is a command through which rulers found a continuum of sense that is at the same time a continuum of self, so that all structures of sense are perspectival evaluations serving the will to power.**
    - "To communicate oneself is originally to extend one's power over the other." —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - The first form of language is the physical language of injury; signs are originally wounds, and understanding means obeying — quick comprehension aims at 'receiving the fewest blows possible'.
    - Nietzsche's power is 'poetic', achieving eloquence through form as in architecture, but the problem with his monism is that he interprets all processes involving sense as also processes of power.
* <span id="what-is-power-007"></span>**Foucault shows power is productive rather than merely repressive: repression is only the mediation-poor form of power, whereas power produces reality, discourse, knowledge, and even pleasure, and 'man' is already the effect of a subjection more profound than himself, with the soul as the effect and instrument of a political anatomy.**
    - "In fact, power produces; it produces reality." —Michel Foucault
    - The dispositif of sexuality is not a prohibiting law but an 'inciting and multiplying mechanism' that renders the body eloquent and multiplies seeds of pleasure rather than silencing them.
    - Han faults Foucault's monism of power for limiting the dimension of the social, since social sense is not exclusively generated by power.
* <span id="what-is-power-008"></span>**Foucault's three technologies of power can be read through their semantic effects: sovereign power speaks through a mediation-poor 'symbolics of blood' inscribed on the tortured body, the power of civil law binds subjects through a circulating signifying system of ideas ('the chain of their own ideas'), and disciplinary power enters the body to create habitual routines and second nature.**
    - Sovereign power traces on the very body of the condemned man 'signs that must not be effaced'; the second technology uses the pen rather than the sword and the 'festival of the penal code' for ritual recoding.
    - "A stupid despot may constrain his slaves with iron chains; but a true politician binds them even more strongly by the chain of their own ideas." —Servan
    - Disciplinary power turns 'formless clay' into a 'machine', but Han insists the body is not only worked on but also described — its traces are always meaningful and inhabit the soul.
* <span id="what-is-power-009"></span>**Bourdieu's habitus shows power stabilizes itself at the symbolic level by producing a preconscious 'common sense': the socially disadvantaged incorporate the ruling order into body automatisms, experiencing the unavoidable as freely chosen taste and an 'amor fati', so that coercion is felt as freedom and power reproduces itself without rational justification.**
    - "It is because agents never know completely what they are doing that what they do has more sense than they know." —Pierre Bourdieu
    - Poverty becomes a freely chosen lifestyle; the unavoidable is turned 'into a taste of freedom', letting victims dedicate themselves to their socially allocated destiny.
* <span id="what-is-power-010"></span>**Read sociologically, Heidegger's 'they' (das Man) and 'public interpretedness' function as a continuum of sense that power must occupy: as a 'dictatorship of what goes without saying' it operates immanently through the habitual, levels down deviation, and works even at the affective and somatic level of moods, giving power that appears as 'nobody's' power maximal stability.**
    - "The 'they', which supplies the answer to the question of the 'who' of everyday Dasein, is the 'nobody' to whom every Dasein has already surrendered itself." —Martin Heidegger
    - Because Heidegger radically separates ontology and sociology he fails to see that thrownness (Geworfenheit) may be a submission (Unterworfenheit) to a ruling order's continuum of sense.
    - An absolute power would be one that never became apparent and blended completely into what goes without saying — power that works through the automatism of habit is more efficient than power that gives orders.

## The Metaphysics of Power

Power is metaphysically grounded in life and spirit as the capacity to be with oneself in the other: through internalization, digestion, grasping and thinking the self overcomes negativity and continues itself, so that at the highest mediation power becomes Hegel's 'free power' — yet religion at its roots points beyond this return-to-self toward a limitless, peaceful continuity of being.

* <span id="what-is-power-011"></span>**Against Foucault's late hedonistic-ludic concept of power as an 'open-ended strategic game', Han argues with Nietzsche and Heidegger that the essence of power is going-beyond-itself while going-together-with-itself — a self-assertion and will to increase power, whose pleasure derives from overcoming the trauma of impotence and dependency.**
    - "The pleasure of power derives from the displeasure, experienced a hundred times, of dependency, of impotence." —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - "Power itself only is inasmuch as, and as long as, it remains a willing to be more power." —Martin Heidegger
    - Foucault admitted he was not knowledgeable about anthropology; pleasurable play is not the anthropological foundation of power, which rests instead on the continuity of self that grows in parallel with power.
* <span id="what-is-power-012"></span>**Hegel elevates power to a feature of the living as such: the organism 'reaches over and beyond its other', assimilating the external through digestion, and spirit does the same through internalization, grasping (Begreifen) and thinking — turning being into having and producing a continuum of self in which spirit remains at home with itself in the other.**
    - "All activities of the spirit are nothing but different ways of leading the external back to the inwardness which is spirit itself." —G. W. F. Hegel
    - In pure intuition spirit is 'unfree to the highest degree' because it is engrossed in external material; in the idea and in thinking it returns into itself, sublating the otherness of the other into sameness and producing a continuum of sameness.
* <span id="what-is-power-013"></span>**Power is the capacity of the living to overcome non-being and remain with itself amid negativity: Tillich and Hegel locate the power to be in bearing and integrating negation, so the neurotic who can include only a little non-being flees into a 'small, narrow castle' while 'God' represents the highest, infinite point of mediation.**
    - "Spirit wins its truth only when, in utter dismemberment, it finds itself." —G. W. F. Hegel
    - The neurotic achieves continuity of self only through violence without mediation, whereas at intense mediation self-affirmation is integrating rather than negating or excluding.
* <span id="what-is-power-014"></span>**At the highest mediation power ceases to be violent and becomes Hegel's 'free power': the concept and the universal reach out to and embrace their other, reality, without doing violence to it because the other freely submits and affirms the embrace as its own truth — so that truth is power and absolute power rests on free submission.**
    - "The universal is therefore free power; it is itself while reaching out to its other and embracing it, but without doing violence to it." —G. W. F. Hegel
    - Grasping reveals what already exists in-itself in things; internalization is not violation but reconciliation, and the light of the concept does not blind reality because it is reality's own light.
    - Foucault's thesis that man is already the effect of a profound subjection becomes, in Hegel's terms, a primary submission whose 'yes' to the embracing power constitutes identity and the soul in the first place.
* <span id="what-is-power-015"></span>**Han criticizes Hegel for describing all religion through an economy of power (return-to-self-in-the-other) and instead, drawing on Bataille and Handke, locates religion in a limitless continuum of being — an intimacy or immanence, a friendliness and deep tiredness that dissolves the separating limit without the intentionality of a return to self.**
    - Bataille's sacrifice is dereification and removal of limits, returning the victim to the 'intimacy of the divine world'; animals already live in continuity, in the world 'like water in water', and so need no religion.
    - Bataille's continuity of being is orgiastic and regressive and amenable to limit-removing violence, so his 'divine' flame is anything but friendly; Han counters that spirit founds continuity of being without letting forms dissolve.
    - "The other becomes I." —Peter Handke
    - Handke's deep tiredness, in which 'no one and nothing dominates or commands', is a counter-figure to Hegel's spirit as pure 'doing' and 'absolute intervening' — a religion of deep tiredness that is friendliness.

## The Politics of Power

Political power is fundamentally spatial and rests on both subjectivity (the will to self) and continuity (acting together), so neither Schmitt's sovereign-exception model, nor Arendt's communicative power, nor Habermas's consensus model suffices; politics is always power politics because communicative action is always already strategic, and its practice is compromise rather than pure consensus.

* <span id="what-is-power-016"></span>**Schmitt's sovereign 'who decides on the exception' expresses an absolute self-affirming subjectivity, but the sovereign 'no' of the exception is the equivalent of Hegel's continuous sovereign 'yes' in the normal case — both express the will to self ('I will myself') constitutive of the state as a space of power.**
    - "Sovereign is he who decides on the exception." —Carl Schmitt
    - Kierkegaard and Schmitt hold the exception reveals sovereignty more clearly than the general, but Hegel, the philosopher of the normal case, shows all a monarch needs is someone to say 'yes' — the highest instance of formal decision.
* <span id="what-is-power-017"></span>**Schmitt wrongly hypostasizes power into an 'objective, autonomous factor' that exceeds human reality because he fixates on the exception and the isolating 'antechamber of power'; in fact modern societies subject power to radical distribution and decentralization, and the antechamber is itself a side room of the power chamber, evidence that no finite human power can wholly seal itself off.**
    - The old antechamber of ministers, confessors, physicians and mistresses gives way to new antechambers such as the lobby; modern media easily perforate any informational barrier, as in Bismarck's 1890 petition for release over how the Chancellor and Kaiser should be informed.
    - The media as such have no power in the proper sense — 'the power of the media' is misleading, for the media form only a diffuse fog of indirect influence lacking unambiguous intentional structure; influence is exerted at points, power is a phenomenon of space.
* <span id="what-is-power-018"></span>**Power creates a continuum and spatializes the ego's will, whereas violence takes place at a point and cannot spatialize itself: violence may capture but never create space, so revolution or the state may emerge through violence but is not based on it — only spirit, generating a common will and a 'we', can create the political.**
    - "Although the state may emerge through violence, it nevertheless is not based on it." —G. W. F. Hegel
    - Violence is a sign of impotence: an isolated ego with no power over the alter can only break the alter's will by force, whereas power lets the ego continue himself in the alter without resorting to violence.
* <span class="pull-quote">The term 'non-violent power' is, accordingly, not an oxymoron but a pleonasm.</span>
* <span id="what-is-power-019"></span>**Arendt rightly makes power a spatial 'space of appearance' arising when people act in concert and deriving legitimacy from the initial being-together, but her definition of extreme power as 'All against One' betrays that power is not primarily togetherness but a subjectivity, a determination to self that manifests when an Against comes into contact with the totality.**
    - "Power springs up whenever people get together and act in concert." —Hannah Arendt
    - When an Against opposes the collective, a contraction of power into itself takes place as in a state of exception; a minimal subjectivity that repeats itself must underlie any form of power to transform a space of being into a space of power.
    - 'The extreme form of power is All against One, the extreme form of violence is One against All' — the unexplained 'against' in the definition of power reveals its self-structure.
* <span id="what-is-power-020"></span>**Habermas reduces Arendt's power to communicative action oriented toward agreement and thereby overlooks its strategic-polemical dimension; Arendt's own examples — well-organized minorities ruling empires, masters' organized solidarity over slaves, the Vietnamese victory — show power depends on organization and strategy, so strategic action cannot be equated with instrumental violence or expelled from the political.**
    - "The basic phenomenon is not the instrumentalizing of another's will for one's own purposes but the formation of a common will in a communication aimed at agreement." —Jürgen Habermas
    - An 'ill-equipped but well-organized' opponent in Vietnam defeated enormous superiority in the means of violence, illustrating collective power based on strategy, not communicative understanding.
    - Habermas's abstract idea of pure undistorted communication makes any social asymmetry appear as 'structural violence' that erects unnoticed communicative blocks — but it is more realistic to assume communication is always already strategic.
* <span id="what-is-power-021"></span>**Because communicative action is always already strategic, politics is always power politics: subjectivity/self and continuity/continuum are invariable structural moments of both consensus and struggle models, which are two variations of one power differing only in internal mediation, and political action is constituted not by consensus but by compromise as the balancing of power and decision.**
    - Asymmetric situations result not from violence but from forms of power lacking mediation; violence is the limit-case where mediation is reduced to nothing and all feeling of freedom is removed, while narrative and legitimizing ideologies remain within the realm of power.
    - Compromittere means to leave the decision on a matter to the verdict of an arbiter; a being only oriented toward reaching understanding is an abstraction in politics, anthropology and ontology alike.

## The Ethics of Power

Power is inherently ipsocentric — it gathers everything toward the one self and produces off-sites — so an ethics of power cannot arise from power itself; only friendliness, an unconditional hospitality and self-emptying that is the 'other of power', can mediate beyond power's own capacity, moving Nietzsche's will to power toward an ethics of the no one.

* <span id="what-is-power-022"></span>**Power is orientation (Ortung): reading Schmitt's land-appropriation and Heidegger's 'site' (Ort) as the tip of a spear, Han shows every site gathers everything unto itself, forming an ipsocentric continuum that wills itself — an ipseity that Derrida too finds analytically included in the concept of power, and that persists in deterritorialized, digital, and market forms of land-appropriation.**
    - "The idea of force (kratos), of power, and of mastery, is analytically included in the concept of ipseity." —Jacques Derrida
    - Globalization loosens power's ties to territory but generates re-orientation (Re-Ortung); there is no essential difference between terrestrial and digital orientation, and mergers and take-overs do not fundamentally differ from land-appropriation.
    - Heidegger perceives the site's tip only as highest mediation and never as the coercion and repression a mediation-poor tip produces.
* <span id="what-is-power-023"></span>**Because power is ipsocentric it produces off-sites (Ab-Orte) that are de-sited and devalued, raising the ethical question of whether power can let off-sites become sites again; Derrida's 'autoimmunity' as a moderation of ipseity toward alterity is inadequate, since total self-destruction would be absolute evil and destroy the house (oikos) needed for hospitality.**
    - The absoluteness of power consists in absolute immunity, an inviolability of self; the ethical dimension requires that a site exceed its ipsocentric striving and grant residential space to the manifold and the adjacent.
    - Power tends to reduce openness rather than sustain it; strategic openness is not identical with the pleasurable openness of play, and fear of instability may fuel the desire for more power.
* <span id="what-is-power-024"></span>**Foucault's late ethics of power grounds power in freedom and care of the self, but Han faults its silent transition from freedom as a structural presupposition of power to an ethics of freedom: slavery in chains remains a power relation because the slave can still say 'no', and complete power over oneself does not rule out tyrannical violence, so care of the self remains trapped within the intentionality of the self.**
    - "If there are relations of power in every social field, this is because there is freedom everywhere." —Michel Foucault
    - Foucault conceives power processes as open contests lacking the 'tip of domination', keeping power in artificial playful hovering; unlike Hegel's 'free power' oriented to mediation and reconciliation, this moves away from real processes of power.
    - Foucault's care of the self ties care for others to the calculation that others' well-being serves one's own, an economic circularity that cannot include the other in the horizon of the self.
* <span id="what-is-power-025"></span>**Nietzsche makes exploitation, injury and appropriation the essence of being alive and the will to power, yet his notions of justice and 'power with a vast vision' point beyond ipsocentric power: justice gives to each his own, listens rather than judges, refrains from convictions and thus from oneself — a movement opposed to power's gathering that cannot originate from power as such.**
    - "Life is essentially a process of appropriating, injuring, overpowering the alien and the weaker." —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - Exploitative power's vast vision serves only the self; power can extend its gaze for the sake of vastness and plurality only if touched by something that is not power, not circling around the self.
    - Justice 'observes carefully from all sides' with a lofty, merciful objectivity and is 'an opponent of convictions'; refraining from judgement is a rare abstemiousness impossible for power, which is made up of judgements and convictions.
* <span id="what-is-power-026"></span>**Nietzsche's aristocratic friendliness and unconditional hospitality invoke a 'dangerous carelessness' that opens heart and house to anyone, but Han argues this cannot be derived from power's 'over-abundance' or 'self-aggrandisement': true overflowing would flood the self and make return-to-self impossible, so friendliness is the other of power — an over-power containing a self-suspension of power.**
    - "Give yourself away first, oh, Zarathustra!" —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - Aristocratic friendliness gazes from the windows of the castle that is also a prison of the self; unconditional hospitality knows no castle and welcomes 'beggars, or cripples, or kings', a giving that takes place unconsciously and without intention and cannot be re-appropriated by willing oneself.
    - Nietzsche's philosophy of the will to power leads to a 'nemology', an ethics and aesthetics of the no one (Niemandigkeit) — like a fruit that falls 'content and unawares', desiring nothing for itself and giving everything of itself.
