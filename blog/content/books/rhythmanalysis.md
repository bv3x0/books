## Rhythmanalysis: An Introduction (Stuart Elden)

Stuart Elden situates Lefebvre's rhythmanalysis within his lifelong philosophical project of rethinking space and time together, tracing its intellectual genealogy from Nietzsche, Proust, Bachelard, and Marx, and arguing that this work—Lefebvre's fullest treatment of temporality—complements The Production of Space by showing that non-linear time had always underpinned his spatial thinking.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-001"></span>**Rhythmanalysis, posthumously published in 1992, represents Lefebvre's fullest treatment of temporality and was explicitly conceived to complete the exposition of The Production of Space by providing the temporal dimension that his spatial analysis lacked.**
    - Lefebvre promised a future work on rhythmanalysis as early as the second volume of Critique of Everyday Life (1961), thirty years before its final realization.
    - The historical dimension of Lefebvre's spatial analysis is often neglected in contemporary appropriations of his work, which is seriously to misread him.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-002"></span>**Lefebvre's theory of 'moments'—significant times when orthodoxies are open to challenge—developed in opposition to Bergson's durée and drew primarily on Nietzsche's Augenblick (the blink of an eye as gateway where past and future collide), establishing his non-linear understanding of history decades before his spatial work.**
    - In Nietzsche's Thus Spoke Zarathustra, the moment is a gateway where past and future collide in the image of eternal recurrence.
    - Lefebvre's understanding of time as non-calculable and resistant to abstracting generalisation parallels his critique of Cartesian geometry as a reductive way of understanding space.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-003"></span>**Lefebvre theorized music as a relation of three terms—melody, harmony, and rhythm—arguing that rhythm, the most neglected of these, is of paramount importance and offers an alternative to purely mathematical models of calculation and measure.**
    - Lefebvre was a keen amateur musician who numbered Beethoven and Schumann among his favourite composers and was interested in the challenges posed by Pierre Boulez, Webern, and Schoenberg.
    - The French word la mesure captures both musical beat and mathematical measure, running throughout Lefebvre's argument that music gives us an alternative model of time.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-004"></span>**The term 'rhythmanalysis' was borrowed from Gaston Bachelard, who in turn took it from Portuguese philosopher Lucio Alberto Pinheiro dos Santos; Bachelard's Dialectic of Duration, which critiques Bergson's notion of duration as fragmentary rather than unified, was a direct intellectual source for Lefebvre's project.**
    - Bachelard's The Poetics of Space, along with Nietzsche and Heidegger, was one of Lefebvre's key resources for understanding space in The Production of Space.
    - Bachelard argued that duration, made famous by Bergson, is never as unitary and cohesive as Bergson suggested, but fragmentary and made up of disparate elements.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-005"></span>**Lefebvre's lifelong project of critiquing everyday life, which he considered his most important contribution to Marxism, provides the overarching framework for rhythmanalysis: the contrast between the capitalist system's imposition of linear, homogeneous time and the richer cyclical and bodily rhythms of daily individual life.**
    - The everyday carries a dual meaning in French—the mundane and the repetitive, what happens every day—making rhythmanalysis central to its analysis.
    - Lefebvre was concerned with the contrast between the capitalist system and the daily lives of individuals to the very end of his own life.

## Introduction to Rhythmanalysis

Lefebvre introduces the fundamental problem of rhythmanalysis by showing that rhythm cannot be reduced to mechanical repetition but involves the dialectical interplay of repetition and difference, cyclical and linear time, and that the commodity form has colonized social space and time by turning them into the abstract time and space of markets.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-006"></span>**Rhythm is universally misunderstood through reductive lenses—musicians reduce it to beat-counting, historians see only impersonal cycles, gymnasts see only muscle sequences—when in fact rhythm requires a unified concept of repetition-in-difference that links the organic to the rational.**
    - No rhythm exists without repetition in time and space—reprises, returns, measure—but there is no absolute identical repetition; difference always enters.
    - Even the simplest repetition of unity (1+1+1…) generates the infinity of prime numbers with specific properties, illustrating how repetition necessarily produces novelty.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-007"></span>**After the French Revolution, a new socio-economic organisation emerged in which the commodity prevails over everything, and social space and time—dominated by exchange—become the time and space of markets, entering into products as abstract rhythms.**
    - The commodity form transforms space and time from lived realities into abstract quantities measurable by market exchange.
    - Cyclical repetition originates in cosmic and natural sources (waves, tides, seasons, monthly cycles), while linear repetition originates in human activity and becomes the basis of the mechanical.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-008"></span>**Rhythmanalysis requires a triadic dialectical method—moving beyond simple dual oppositions to three-term relations like time-space-energy or melody-harmony-rhythm—because binary analysis is structurally incapable of grasping moving complexity without reducing it.**
    - Classical analysis isolates elements; structural analysis studies pairs in opposition; dialectical analysis separates out three terms in interaction—conflicts or alliances.
    - The triad 'time-space-energy' links three terms that remain distinct without fusing into a synthesis, unlike the Hegelian schema.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-009"></span>**The key conceptual framework for rhythmanalysis is built from paired and triadic categories: repetition/difference, mechanical/organic, cyclical/linear, continuous/discontinuous, and the derived notions of polyrhythmia (multiple simultaneous rhythms), eurhythmia (their harmonious interaction), and arrhythmia (their pathological disruption).**
    - Eurhythmia characterizes health; arrhythmia is simultaneously symptom, cause, and effect of a pathological state; these concepts apply to bodies, social bodies, and cities alike.
    - The human body itself is the paradigm case: a bundle of rhythms—respiration, heartbeat, hunger, sleep—that must be taken as the analyst's own reference point.

## The Critique of the Thing

Lefebvre argues that rhythm occupies the precise position between the purely logical and the dialectical, between the rational and the lived-carnal, and that rhythmanalysis must mobilize all the body's senses as its primary instrument in order to move from abstract categories to the concrete fullness of experienced time.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-010"></span>**Rhythm is both rational (governed by numerical laws, measurable) and irreducibly carnal (rooted in the lived body), and it is precisely this double nature that positions it as the central concept capable of bridging logical and dialectical analysis.**
    - Rational, numerical, quantitative rhythms superimpose themselves on the multiple natural rhythms of the body—respiration, the heart, hunger and thirst—though not without changing them.
    - The rhythm of a Chopin waltz or the aphorisms of Nietzsche cannot be reduced to either the purely natural or the purely artificial: they are both at once.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-011"></span>**The term 'rhythmanalysis' originates with Portuguese philosopher Lucio Alberto Pinheiro dos Santos, was taken up by Bachelard in The Psychoanalysis of Fire without full development, and is now given its full systematic unfolding by Lefebvre through an organised table of conceptual oppositions: repetition/difference, mechanical/organic, cyclical/linear, continuous/discontinuous, quantitative/qualitative.**
    - Bachelard borrowed the word from dos Santos but did not develop its meaning beyond a preliminary gesture.
    - These conceptual pairs converge in the central concept of measure (la mesure), which is both quantitative parameter and qualitative internal standard.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-012"></span>**For historical change to occur, a social group or class must intervene by imprinting a rhythm on an era—by force or insinuation—establishing that rhythmic transformation is the mechanism of historical transition, not merely its symptom.**
    - Exhaustion is visible sooner and more clearly than growth and innovation; the dominant rhythm is more apparent in its fatigue than in its genesis.
    - Dual (two-term) Marxist analysis staked too much on the bourgeoisie-proletariat opposition at the expense of the third term: the soil, agricultural property, peasants, and predominantly agricultural colonies.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-013"></span>**Four categories of rhythm structure social life: secret rhythms (physiological and psychological), public/social rhythms (calendars, ceremonies), fictional rhythms (eloquence, gestures, learning), and dominating-dominated rhythms (everyday or long-lasting rhythms that aim for effects beyond themselves).**
    - These categories are not mutually exclusive but interpenetrate, requiring the analyst to unwrap the bundle of rhythms rather than isolate any single strand.
    - The study of rhythms covers an immense area from the most natural (physiological, biological) to the most sophisticated, distinguishing what comes from nature and what is acquired, conventional, or sophisticated.

## The Rhythmanalyst: A Previsionary Portrait

Lefebvre sketches the figure of the rhythmanalyst as a transdisciplinary practitioner who thinks with the whole body rather than abstract intellect, using his own bodily rhythms as primary reference points to perceive and distinguish the polyrhythmic ensembles of the world, and who is closer to the poet than to either the psychoanalyst or the statistician.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-014"></span>**The rhythmanalyst must undergo a disciplined self-transformation—educating his perception, modifying his conception of time and environment—that borders on pathology but avoids it by remaining methodical, drawing data from psychology, sociology, ethnology, biology, physics, and mathematics.**
    - The rhythmanalyst will come to 'listen' to a house, a street, a town as an audience listens to a symphony.
    - He makes himself more sensitive to times than to spaces, pursues an interdisciplinary approach in relation to instruments supplied by specialists, and pursues his practice without privileging any single sense.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-015"></span>**The body is itself a polyrhythmic ensemble—a 'garland of rhythms' in metastable equilibrium—and the rhythmanalyst uses his own bodily rhythms (breathing, heartbeat, blood circulation, speech) as the foundational reference points for perceiving the rhythms of external bodies, social environments, and urban spaces.**
    - The eu-rhythmic body composed of diverse rhythms keeps them in metastable equilibrium, always threatened and often recovered; pathology is arrhythmia that progresses toward fatal de-synchronisation.
    - The rhythmanalyst should not neglect smells, scents, and impressions that society atrophies and neutralises; odours are part of rhythm and reveal them—odours of morning and evening, of hours of sunlight or darkness.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-016"></span>**Images—including photographs, paintings, and television—simulate presence rather than constituting it, and the rhythmanalyst's task is to transform things and images into presences by perceiving them within their rhythmic becoming rather than accepting them as static objects or simulacra of the present.**
    - The present sometimes imitates (simulates) presence to the point of mistaking itself for it, but presence survives and imposes itself by introducing a rhythm.
    - The act of rhythmanalysis transforms everything into presences, integrating this wall, this table, these trees into a dramatic becoming full of meaning, transforming them no longer into diverse things but into presences.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-017"></span>**The rhythmanalyst is closer to the poet than to the psychoanalyst or the statistician because his act is one of metamorphosis—reinstating the sensible in consciousness and thought—and could in the long term intervene in the everyday to accomplish a small part of the revolutionary transformation of society without any declared political position.**
    - Sensual desire falls into rhythms whose goal is placed outside the act; aesthetic desire has a rhythm whose goal remains internal to the act; both are rhythms in interaction.
    - The rhythmanalyst does not imprison himself in the ideology of the thing but perceives the thing in the proximity of the present as an instance of presence.

## Seen from the Window

Through sustained observation of a busy Parisian street from a window overlooking the rue Rambuteau near the Pompidou Centre, Lefebvre demonstrates that the city is not a visual spectacle but a polyrhythmic field—alternating intensities of pedestrian and vehicular flow governed by traffic lights and state power—while the window simultaneously reveals the omnipresent but invisible order of the State that structures urban rhythms.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-018"></span>**The Parisian street viewed from the window near the Pompidou Centre (Beaubourg) manifests a strict alternating polyrhythmia: two-minute intervals of pedestrian clusters followed by the explosive charge of vehicles (lorries toward Bastille, cars toward the Hôtel de Ville), punctuated by traffic light regulation—hard rhythms of silence and outburst.**
    - The noise grows in intensity until it becomes unbearable, then stops; pedestrians cluster like a clot, then race through the gap—arrhythmia reigns except for rare moments.
    - The disparate crowd includes tourists from Finland, Sweden, Portugal, shoppers, art lovers, and suburban commuters streaming between peak hours, all circling the metallic spectacle of the Centre Georges Pompidou.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-019"></span>**The garden viewed from the other window reveals that apparent spatial simultaneity and immobility are deceptive: each plant and tree has its own rhythm of flowering, leafing, and fruiting, so that what looks like a collection of fixed things, perceived carefully over time, becomes a polyrhythmic symphony of distinct temporal trajectories.**
    - The plum tree flowered white before turning green; the cherry tree flowered before its leaves, which survive the fruits and fall late; temporal diversity hides beneath visual simultaneity.
    - Modernity has enlarged and deepened the present while simultaneously dilapidating it: the media quasi-suppresses distances and waiting periods, amplifying the present but delivering only reflections and shadows.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-020"></span>**The window is not a mental or purely visual place but a practical, private, and concrete space that reveals the urban field as a set of diverse spaces affected by diverse times, and through which the analyst perceives that behind the visible rhythmic order stands the omnipresent State—the invisible organizer of urban time.**
    - The solitary cop at the junction induces the discourse of Order more and better than the facades of the Square; the police officer's presence arouses no protest, as everyone knows his uselessness in advance.
    - The monuments, palaces, and institutional buildings—from the Archives to the Bank of France—are meteorites from another planet imposing a political logic on the popular centre.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-021"></span>**To grasp urban rhythms from the window, one must use not only the eyes but memory, allowing the present to be held in relation to other moments and hours so it is not isolated but experienced in all its diversity—a method that dissolves the sharp philosophical opposition between subject and object into a lived practice of reference and displacement.**
    - The recollection of other moments and of all hours is indispensable not as a simple point of reference, but in order not to isolate this present and to live it in all its diversity.
    - Rhythms reveal and hide simultaneously; they are the music of the city, a scene that listens to itself—but no camera, no image, can show these rhythms.

## Dressage

Lefebvre analyzes 'dressage' (training/breaking-in) as the primary social mechanism by which institutions impose rhythms onto bodies, arguing that the triadic rhythm of activity-rest-entertainment constitutes a universal paradigm of social domination rooted in the military-Roman tradition, though this domination always generates resistant counter-rhythms, particularly in the rhythmic resistance of women against the virile model.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-022"></span>**Social dressage (training/breaking-in) operates by imposing rhythms on bodies through a universal triadic structure—(a) controlled internal activity under direction, (b) complete rest/sleep, (c) diversions and rewards—a paradigm established by Roman-military traditions and universally reproduced in armies, religious establishments, educational institutions, offices, and monasteries.**
    - To enter into a society or group is to accept values taught through the right channels, but also to bend oneself—to be broken in to its rhythms.
    - This rhythmic model establishes itself through dressage and subsequently perpetuates itself; the trinitarian character (activity-rest-entertainment) confers generality upon it as a physiologically grounded paradigm.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-023"></span>**Dressage operates not only through force but through the gradual rhythmic shaping of desire, need, sleep, and waking, so that externally imposed constraints appear to bodies as internal spontaneity and essential need—a dispossession of the body that Lefebvre identifies as a fundamental feature of modern everyday life.**
    - He who rises at six in the morning because his work has rhythmed him in this way is perhaps still sleepy; the interaction of the repetitive and the rhythmic sooner or later gives rise to the dispossession of the body.
    - The rhythmed organisation of everyday time is simultaneously what is most personal and most external—acquired rhythms are at once internal and social.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-024"></span>**Arrhythmia—the disruption and irregularity of imposed rhythms—is simultaneously symptom, cause, and effect of crisis; disruptions of bodily or social rhythms always have origins in the rhythms of institutions, growth, population, and exchanges, and could be traced in the rhythmic changes that followed revolutions like 1789 and 1830.**
    - Arrhythmia announces a lacuna, a hole in time, to be filled by invention and creation—individually or socially, it always takes place in crisis.
    - Were revolutions like 1789 and 1830 not themselves transformations in foods, gestures, costumes, and the rhythm of work and occupations?
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-025"></span>**Women's dressage was always particularly harsh, and their resistance to the virile model—the dominant code of existence propagated ideologically—has been carried out through rhythms for centuries, and this rhythmic resistance may be the source of feminine movements' capacity for renovation in contemporary societies.**
    - The figures of resistance ranged from adored goddesses to respected matrons, from the lessons of love to the suffragettes.
    - Creative activity, as distinct from productive activity, proceeds from the liberty and individuality that unfurl only in conditions external to dressage—liberty is born in a reserved space and time, sometimes wide, sometimes narrow.

## The Media Day

Lefebvre argues that the media's unceasing flow of information and images colonizes everyday time by displacing presence with the present—substituting simulacra for lived dialogue—and that producers of commodity information have learned empirically to exploit rhythms by fragmenting time and converting communication into a flow that masks rather than reveals the everyday while simultaneously contributing to its production.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-026"></span>**The media day has neither beginning nor end—an uninterrupted global flow of words and images—and its fundamental function is to occupy time with the present while simulating presence, substituting the simulacrum of the lived for actual lived experience.**
    - Imagery has replaced in the modern era the sacralisation of time and its occupation by rites and solemnised gestures; it succeeds in fabricating, introducing, and making accepted the everyday.
    - The present simulates presence and introduces simulation (the simulacrum) into social practice; it resembles the real as a photo resembles a photographed person—without depth, breadth, or flesh.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-027"></span>**The media's fragmentation of time into choices, channels, and fragments represents an empirical mastery of rhythms by producers of commodity information, who have cut up time and broken continuous duration into saleable, consumable units that displace vital interest without the consumer's awareness.**
    - The media enter into the everyday and contribute to producing it; however, they do not speak of it—they content themselves with illusions and mask their own action: the effacement of the immediate and of presence.
    - Listeners to local radio would actually know what one does not want to know: how people live, of what the everyday consists—but the form of communication eludes the content it so badly needs.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-028"></span>**Communication in modernity has devalued dialogue to the point of its forgetting: modern informational flow is fluent, instantaneous, banal, and superficial, destroying the intense reciprocal speech that Heidegger identified as the ground of human Dasein, while nonetheless carrying real social and historical weight that must be acknowledged rather than simply denounced.**
    - "The being of man is grounded in language, but this happens authentically primarily in dialogue; we are a dialogue; dialogue and its unity underlie our Dasein." —Martin Heidegger
    - Communication devalues dialogue to the point of its being forgotten; the critique of the informational constitutes a part of experiential knowledge, but the right to information cannot be set apart from citizenship.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-029"></span>**The modern contempt for the body—masked beneath hygiene ethics and sporting ideology—structures the media's relation to the everyday; capital's rhythm of production-and-destruction increasingly prioritises the destructive, killing nature, the city, artistic creation, and immediacy, while the media's technological conquests nourish ideology rather than making everyday life more alive.**
    - Capital has something more than maliciousness about it: the wills of property owners execute through them the death-dealing character of capital without full consciousness or clear intuition.
    - Capital kills social richness; it produces private riches while technologies kill immediacy—communication remains formal, content neglected, lost, wasted away.

## Music and Rhythms

Lefebvre deploys a detailed triadic analysis of music—moving through the paired oppositions of musical discourse to the fundamental triad melody-harmony-rhythm—to argue that rhythm historically tends toward supremacy over the other two elements, that this ascendancy is inseparable from music's relation to the body, and that musical rhythm performs an ethical function by compensating for the miseries of everydayness and restoring the sensible to its rightful place.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-030"></span>**Musical discourse is structured by a set of binary oppositions—low/high, vivace/adagio, verticality/horizontality, tied/staccato, logogenic/pathogenic (signification vs. emotion), sacred/profane—but these oppositions are insufficient for grasping musical reality, which cannot define itself simply as a language or writing, and requires the triadic analysis of melody-harmony-rhythm.**
    - Bach would be logogenic and Beethoven pathogenic, likewise Schumann; Ravel would fall under the significant and Mozart above and beyond these limiting significations.
    - Sound implies three terms: pitch (place in the range of frequencies), intensity (loud/quiet), and tone (harmonics emitted alongside the fundamental)—a G on the flute, piano, or violin is the same note yet utterly different in each.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-031"></span>**The historical development of Western music follows a dialectical movement in which first the verbal/metric dominated (Greek bards, Gregorian chant), then melody achieved autonomy (ars nova, sonatas, symphonies), then harmony took flight as an end in itself, and finally rhythm has ascended to dominance in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries—a real history whose periods are connected to social structures.**
    - Centuries were required for musical time to discern itself from verbal time, for musicians to give themselves rhythms distinct from spoken rhythms and gestures.
    - Beethoven made himself listened to in silence; not yet Mozart—music gains in strength what it loses in social listening, passing from chamber music to great public concerts and the glory of conductors.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-032"></span>**Musical measure (la mesure) is a means, not an end: it emerged from practical needs of dance (bourée, saraband) following oral traditions, was eventually codified in writing and notation (staves formalised in the eighteenth century), but the imposition of musical writing impoverished spontaneity and inspiration even as it enabled new compositional forms.**
    - Dances were for the most part peasant and popular dances—the bourée or the saraband—skilfully treated in Bach; the aristocracy and town-dwellers did not deprive themselves of dancing.
    - At the time of classical fashions there were only two types of measure: binary and ternary; writing obliges fidelity and does not favour inspiration or variation, however essential.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-033"></span>**Rhythm becomes worldly precisely because it is grounded in the universal physiological organisation of the human species—respiration, heartbeat—allowing jazz, African rhythms, and non-Western music to enter Western musical culture in a way that pure melody or harmony never achieved; rhythm is thus the universal of music because it is the universal of the body.**
    - Modern music finds itself back in the body; rhythm dominates, supplants melody and harmony without suppressing them—the body and its rhythms remain the site toward which musical creation returns through strange detours.
    - Musical rhythm has an ethical function: in its relation to the body, to time, and to the work, it illustrates real everyday life, purifies it through catharsis, and brings compensation for the miseries of everydayness.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-034"></span>**Political power constitutes an empirical rhythmanalysis: it knows how to utilise and manipulate time, dates, and timetables, combining the rhythmic unfoldings of individuals, groups, and entire societies—what is officially called mobilisation—and thereby demonstrates that rhythmanalysis is not an abstract science but a practical and political tool.**
    - Alliance supposes harmony between different rhythms; conflict supposes arrhythmia—a divergence in time, space, and the use of energies.
    - The authorities have to know the polyrhythmia of the social body that they set in motion—mobilisation is the extreme case revealing simultaneously official and empirical political and military rhythmanalysis.

## Conclusions (Résumé)

Lefebvre concludes that rhythmanalysis is not a separate science but a systematic theoretical practice that must enter into use, summarizing the key conceptual apparatus—isorhythmia, eurhythmia, arrhythmia—and affirming that thought strengthens itself only when it enters practice.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-035"></span>**Rhythmanalysis theorised constitutes not a separate science but a conceptualisation that must be grounded in partial studies and must enter into practical use; thought strengthens itself only when it moves from concept to application.**
    - The theory of rhythms as such has received solid support from the possibilities of reproducing rhythms—recording them on disc or cassette—enabling reflection on rhythms that no longer vanish whenever they appear.
    - The discriminatory capacity of the auditory and cerebral apparatus plays the primary practical and spontaneous role in the grasping of rhythms—this is not subjective apprehension, although objective rhythms translate themselves into our own rhythms.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-036"></span>**The essential conceptual vocabulary of rhythmanalysis is consolidated around three key terms: isorhythmia (equal or matching rhythms, as in an orchestra under a conductor's baton), eurhythmia (harmonious interaction of diverse rhythms in health), and arrhythmia (their pathological disruption leading toward morbid and fatal de-synchronisation).**
    - An orchestral ensemble provides the paradigm case of isorhythmia; the living body provides the paradigm case of eurhythmia—each organ having its own rhythm within a spatio-temporal whole.
    - Rhythmanalytic therapy would be preventative rather than curative, announcing and classifying the pathological state before it becomes fully lesional.

## The Rhythmanalytical Project

Written with Catherine Régulier, this essay argues that everyday life is the site of a fundamental conflict between the quantified linear time of capital and the cyclical cosmic rhythms that traverse it, and that rhythmanalysis must grasp this antagonistic unity—the measured measuring of one by the other—as the key to understanding how social time is produced and how its organisation simultaneously appears as both necessary and contingent.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-037"></span>**Everyday life is structured by the conflict between two temporalities: abstract quantitative time (the time of watches and clocks, introduced in the West with the invention of mechanical timepieces, which became the time of work and then subordinated all aspects of daily life) and the great cosmic and vital rhythms (day and night, months, seasons, biological rhythms) that continue to traverse it.**
    - Clock time emerged victorious since it supplied the measure of the time of work; from this historic moment it became the time of everydayness, subordinating sleep and waking, mealtimes, private life, and entertainment to the organisation of work in space.
    - The everyday remains shot through and traversed by great cosmic and vital rhythms, resulting in the perpetual interaction of these rhythms with repetitive processes linked to homogeneous time.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-038"></span>**Cyclical time and linear time are not merely analytical categories but characterise opposite types of repetition with different affective and social valences: the cyclical originates in cosmic nature and is perceived favourably (waves of the sea, seasons, heartbeat), while the linear is experienced as monotonous, tiring, and even intolerable—yet the two exist in an antagonistic unity, each measuring the other.**
    - Cyclical repetition is best measured by the duodecimal system (12 months, 12 hours, 360°, 12 signs of the zodiac); linear repetition is measured on the decimal base (the metric system).
    - The linear and its rhythms have a tendency to oppose that which becomes; according to Crevel, 'the returning is opposed to the becoming', while the cyclical is the rhythm of beginning again.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-039"></span>**Social time is a social product that divides itself into use-value and exchange-value like all products; a bitter and dark struggle around the use of time has led to nocturnal activities multiplying (overturning circadian rhythms), quantified time fragmenting into parcels (transport, work, leisure), and the dispossession of the body as externally imposed schedules are experienced as natural needs.**
    - Objects are consumers of time—a washing machine consumes a fragment of time and occupies a fragment of space; mealtimes differ by country but once habituated, hunger follows the clock rather than the body.
    - Automatism or spontaneity? We attribute what results from external constraints to an essential need; he who rises at six because his work has rhythmed him that way is perhaps still sleepy.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-040"></span>**Rhythm presupposes two necessary elements: (a) thoroughly marked, accentuated, contrasting temporal elements (strong and weak times) and (b) an overall movement that carries all these elements forward; without both, repetition remains merely mechanical—and because rhythm implies a measure, it implies a certain memory: not the identical return of the same, but the return with modification, difference within repetition.**
    - Mechanical repetition works by reproducing the instant that precedes it; rhythm preserves the measure that initiates the process and the re-commencement of this process with modifications—subordinating the same to alterity.
    - To grasp polyrhythmia, it is enough to look at the surface of the sea: waves carry numerous wavelets going in different directions, with superposition of small movements, powerful waves crashing, small undulations traversing one another—a polyrhythmic field.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-041"></span>**The human body is the measure of the world in the Protagorean sense: we perceive and can grasp only those aspects of the universe that correspond to our own rhythms and organs, but this is not a Kantian a priori limitation—it reflects that our scale (the scale of the earth's surface and its cycles) is itself embedded in the universe and that production on this scale can exceed or transfigure it.**
    - Man (the species): his physical and physiological being is indeed the measure of the world, as in the ancient dictum of Protagoras. It is not only that our knowledge is relative to our constitution, but rather that the world that offers itself up to us is relative to this constitution.
    - The spectre of wavelike movements extends indefinitely from the micro to the macro; we grasp and perceive only what corresponds to our own rhythms and organs, but we can make cameras that extend this field.

## Attempt at the Rhythmanalysis of Mediterranean Cities

Lefebvre and Régulier apply rhythmanalysis to Mediterranean cities, arguing that their enduring historical character derives from their solar temporality, their polyrhythmic forms of alliance and compromise (as opposed to the contractual-juridical bases of Nordic/oceanic towns), and from the ongoing struggle between rhythms 'of the self' and rhythms 'of the other'—a struggle in which urban public space becomes a theatrical site of appropriation through which citizens resist state-dominated linear time.

* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-042"></span>**Mediterranean cities are distinguished from oceanic/Nordic cities by their solar temporality: lacking significant tides, they are governed by the cyclical time of the sun rather than the lunar rhythms of tidal seas, producing a more intense urban life with stronger internal contrasts and a more available citizenry whose time is not wholly absorbed by contractual relations of exchange.**
    - The waves of the Mediterranean do not resemble those of oceans—a simple but significant detail, since these waves have and are rhythms; the climate similarly imposes a certain homogeneity: olive trees, vines, etc.
    - In Mediterranean towns, state-political power manages space and controls external relations without being able to prevent townsfolk-citizens from making use of their time and the activities that rhythm it.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-043"></span>**Mediterranean states historically oscillated between democracy and tyranny—brutal and weak, unificatory but shaky—because they could never form enduring alliances against common enemies; cities resisted all conquerors through time and rhythms, illustrating that urban times in the Mediterranean have consistently outendured politically dominated space.**
    - The victory of Athens against the Persians remains an exceptional event; whence the succession of empires that attempted to dominate the whole Mediterranean from antiquity to the present—all conquered the cities, but all the cities resisted.
    - Mediterranean towns have always lived and still live in a regime of compromise between political powers—a 'metastable' state that is the fact of the polyrhythmic.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-044"></span>**The rhythmanalyst distinguishes between rhythms 'of the self' (linked to private life, deeply inscribed rites, quieter and more intimate consciousness) and rhythms 'of the other' (activities turned outward toward the public, more formalised and restrained), but these poles cannot be separated—they interpenetrate through a series of complex transitions: bedroom, apartment, street, square, neighbourhood, city.**
    - In Mediterranean towns urban public space becomes the site of a vast staging where all these relations with their rhythms show and unfurl themselves; rites, codes, and relations act themselves out there.
    - Venice is the example par excellence of a theatrical city where audience and actors are the same but occupy a multiplicity of roles—a privileged form of civility and liberty founded on a dialectic of rhythms.
* <span class="pull-quote">Through a certain use of time the citizen resists the state. A struggle for appropriation is therefore unleashed, in which rhythms play a major role.</span>
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-045"></span>**Religious and social rites punctuate Mediterranean everyday time, each ritualisation creating its own rhythm and tempo while simultaneously intervening in cyclical time at fixed hours, dates, or occasions—a mechanism through which civil time resists state-imposed linear time and by which the analysis of rites becomes a key tool of urban rhythmanalysis.**
    - Religious rites (fasting, prayers, ablutions, the muezzin, the angelus, bells), socio-ritual rites (festivals, carnivals, convivial gatherings), and political rites (ceremonies, commemorations, votes) all enter the everyday to impress an extra-everyday rhythm without interrupting it.
    - A deserted street at four o'clock in the afternoon has as strong a significance as the swarming of a square at market or meeting times; in music and poetry too, the silences have a meaning.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-046"></span>**The case of Beirut illustrates the catastrophic extreme of arrhythmia: when relations of power overcome relations of alliance and rhythms 'of the other' make rhythms 'of the self' impossible, total crisis breaks out with the deregulation of all compromises and the implosion-explosion of the city—demonstrating that Mediterranean polyrhythmia is always a fragile and contested achievement, not a natural given.**
    - Fifteen or twenty years before its catastrophe, Beirut was a place of compromise and alliance that today appears miraculous: the place of a polyrhythm realised in an apparent harmony.
    - Polyrhythmia always results from a contradiction and from resistance to this contradiction; there is a struggle between the tendency towards homogenisation and that towards diversity, the latter being particularly vigorous in the Mediterranean.
* <span id="rhythmanalysis-space-time-and-everyday-life-047"></span>**Tourism is the modern prolongation of historical conquest but paradoxically does not destroy Mediterranean urban rhythms: it distorts space without managing to deform lived time, and cities like Venice, Barcelona, Palermo, Naples, Marseille, and Syracuse fiercely resist homogenisation by adapting while preserving the deep rhythms of their identity.**
    - Tourism in Venice does not suppress the theatricality of the city; it reinforces it, even if it makes dramatic representation pass for something decidedly silly—it does not succeed in altering its profundity.
    - The long predominance of commercial and cultural exchanges produced a melting pot of diverse populations, migrations, and cohabitations that consolidates the forms of alliance found in compromise.
