### TRANSCRIPT

The Christian Interpretation of the Cabala in the Renaissance
[medium pause]

Joseph Leon Blau's 1944 study, The Christian Interpretation of the Cabala in the Renaissance, argues that Christian cabalism was an intellectual fad rather than a lasting achievement. Renaissance thinkers, beginning with Pico della Mirandola, took Jewish mystical doctrines and selectively adapted them so that they seemed to confirm Christian theology. But in Blau's account, the movement quickly proved to be a blind alley. It flourished in a culture still open to occult speculation, and then lost force as scientific habits of thought displaced that older world.

The story is about how Renaissance Christians learned to use a Jewish mystical vocabulary for Christian purposes. The terrain includes the sephiroth, Pico, Reuchlin, Christian Hebraism, occultism, and early modern magic. But the central question is not whether Renaissance scholars found cabala fascinating. They clearly did. The question is what they did with it, why it attracted them, and why, despite its rapid spread, it produced so little of lasting intellectual consequence.

[medium pause]
In the Beginning
[medium pause]

Cabala began from three core Jewish doctrines. The first was divine transcendence. The second was the literal inspiration of Scripture. The third was messianic redemption. Out of these doctrines came occult techniques such as gematria, notarikon, and themurah. Christian interpreters found cabala unusually adaptable because they could substitute Jesus for the Messiah still to come, and they could reinterpret the highest sephiroth as the Trinity. In Blau's view, that required only a modest reframing of structures already present in Jewish mystical thought.

The names in this opening section are dense, but the basic pattern is straightforward. Cabala gives religious thinkers a way to bridge distance: the distance between God and the world, and the distance between the visible text of Scripture and its hidden meanings.

Blau begins with religious seriousness itself. Serious religion, as opposed to naive religiosity, begins with a doubt about the moral relation between the human soul and God. The question is not necessarily whether God exists. It is whether God is remote, or near at hand. Bennett's formulation is that true religion begins in doubt about God's nearness, not about God's existence. That doubt can be resolved in several ways. It may produce orthodoxy. It may produce mysticism. Or it may produce an idiosyncratic occult construction whose adherents think of themselves as the elect.

Occultism, in this framing, is not simply heresy. It is better understood as a distortion produced by exaggerated emphasis inside an existing religious tradition. It is vociferous toward basic religious principles rather than straightforwardly opposed to them.

The Jewish concept of God then moved historically from the anthropomorphic immanence of Genesis toward increasing transcendence. The decisive problem was how an exalted God could still remain related to the world and to the Jewish people. The Palestinian mind, Blau says, was not metaphysical in the strict sense. However exalted God became, He was never conceived as wholly transcendent, and His relation to Israel remained close.

Philo of Alexandria supplied a more philosophically elaborate solution. In the keener intellectual environment of Alexandria, Philo developed a middle-Platonic theory of emanation, reconciling God's eminence with God's immanence. That solution became foundational for later cabalistic thought.

The central cabalistic structure was the doctrine of the sephiroth. These were ten emanations through which the unknowable En Soph manifested itself, and through which God could intervene in human affairs. The sephiroth therefore supplied both a path from man to God and a path from God to man.

The En Soph is infinite and utterly unknowable. It manifests by voluntary self-contraction, or zimzum, first in keter, the crown and highest sephirah, and then through nine further emanations down to malchuth, the kingdom. This system of ten sephiroth is repeated on four levels: aziluth, or emanation; beriah, or creation; yetzirah, or formation; and asiyah, or material action. Taken together, these yield forty regressions from God to the material world.

The terms matter, but the structure matters more: divine reality descends in ordered stages, and those stages become a map for thinking about creation, Scripture, and redemption.

Blau's larger setting is that the study of the Bible and the study of classical humanities were the two poles between which the Christian interpretation of cabala arose.

The doctrine of literal inspiration also mattered. Because Scripture was treated as inspired in every letter and accent, cabalists developed elaborate exegetical techniques. Gematria worked through numerical equivalence. Notarikon worked through acrostics and abbreviations. Themurah worked through letter transposition. These techniques made every letter, accent, and arrangement of Scripture capable of hidden meaning and miraculous power.

Gematria let cabalists substitute words whose Hebrew letters shared the same numerical sum. Once Hebrew, Latin, and Greek were all brought into the exercise, the method could support almost any interpretation. Themurah went even further. By combining alphabetic codes with anagrammatic exchange, it could produce an almost infinite number of letter combinations from any Hebrew word.

The Zohar then became the central document of cabala in the late thirteenth century, despite being thoroughly unsystematic. It is a thirteenth-century pseudepigraph, ascribed by its actual author, Moses ben Shem Tob de Leon, to the Palestinian Tanna Rabbi Simon ben Yochai. For Pico della Mirandola, the most important source was not the Zohar directly, but its popularizer Menahem Recanati. That meant Christian cabalism was built from the beginning on a simplified and degenerate form of zoharic thought.

Moses Cordovero's Garden of Pomegranates, written in the sixteenth century, represented the high point of systematic cabalistic thought. But Cordovero came too late to shape the Christian interpreters. They had already fixed their understanding of cabala on older, less systematic sources.

Christian interpreters adapted cabala by changing its messianic center. They substituted the Messiah who had come, Jesus, for the Jewish Messiah who was still to come. They reinterpreted the three highest sephiroth - keter, chochmah, and binah - as the Trinity. They also identified the sixth sephirah, tifereth, with the incarnate Jesus. In this way, cabala could be made to appear to confirm Christian doctrine rather than challenge it.

The most drastic alteration was the replacement of the Jewish eschatological ma'aseh merkavah, the work of the chariot, with the Book of Revelation. Christian cabalists produced, in effect, a Christian work of the chariot. By identifying the Son both with divine wisdom, the second sephirah, and with the incarnate redeemer, the sixth sephirah, they could distinguish two aspects of Christ's nature while also arguing that the Trinity emanates from, rather than exists inside, the Limitless God.

[medium pause]
The Phoenix of His Age
[medium pause]

Pico della Mirandola first introduced Hebrew cabala to the Christian humanist world through his nine hundred theses of 1486. He treated cabalistic speculation as confirmation of Christian doctrine, including the Trinity and the Messiahship of Jesus. His own knowledge was limited, derivative, and unsystematic, but his influence dominated the Christian interpretation of cabala for nearly two centuries.

Before Pico, there were scattered references to cabala in Christian intellectual life. These appeared in works associated with Raymond Lull, though that attribution is disputed; in Lodovico Lazarelli; and in manuscript translations by Flavius Mithridates commissioned for Pope Sixtus the Fourth. None of these references attracted broad scholarly attention, and none defined how cabala would be Christianized.

The pseudo-Lullian treatise De auditu kabbalistico was written in a fifteenth-century hand. It shows familiarity only with the name cabala, and it presents a kind of Ars magna-style letter mysticism with no real relation to Hebrew cabala. Lazarelli's Crater hermetis, written before 1494, refers to the Zohar and Sefer Yetzirah and knows cabala as an oral tradition, but his knowledge seems secondhand and unsystematic.

Pico's nine hundred theses changed the scale of the subject. In 1486, they included forty-seven derivative propositions from Hebrew cabalistic sources and seventy-two original deductions. Pico framed them as conclusions that greatly strengthened the Christian religion. That framing established from the start that Christian cabalism's primary purpose was apologetic: it was meant to confirm Christian doctrine.

Pico announced his original deductions as, in his wording, seventy-one cabalistic conclusions according to his own opinion, derived from the fundamental ideas of the Hebrew sages and greatly strengthening Christianity. His repeated phrases, such as "No Hebrew cabalist can deny" and "Whatever other cabalists say, I," show that he understood he was moving beyond his sources into a very different universe of discourse.

Pico's cabalistic system was encyclopedic in ambition but limited by over-dependence on Recanati's simplified commentary on the Zohar. Blau calls that source a watered-down version of the cardinal document of cabala. The result was so unsystematic that even a sympathetic critic could hardly derive a consistent philosophy from it.

The major Hebrew source for Pico's conclusions was Recanati's Bible commentary, which was not an original work but a simplification of the Zohar. Pico's Heptaplus, a rhapsodic treatment of the Biblical account of creation, is cabalistic only in relation to the doctrine of worlds. Pico himself suggests that this doctrine came from classical antiquity rather than from the cabalists.

Pico was tried for heresy in thirteen of his conclusions. The most famous was his claim that no science makes us more certain of the divinity of Christ than magic and cabala. In the Apologia, he defended himself by explaining that he meant non-revealed knowledge only. Even so, he was convicted, and he was cleared only by papal pardon.

The Christian doctrines Pico thought cabala could prove included the mystery of the Trinity, the Word made flesh, the divinity of the Messiah, original sin and its expiation through Christ, the heavenly Jerusalem, the fall of the demons, the orders of angels, and the punishments of hell. Pedro Garzia, bishop of Ussel, published a formal resume of the evidence and of his heresy finding. The Franciscan Archangelus of Borgo Nuovo wrote three volumes defending Pico's cabalistic conclusions.

In May 1492, Pico encountered Hebrew books brought by a Sicilian Jewish refugee. This refugee had almost certainly been displaced by the imminent expulsion decree of Ferdinand and Isabella. The timing matters: the expulsion decree for Jews from Spain and its territories, including Sicily, was issued on March 31, 1492, only weeks before the Sicilian Jew appeared.

Pico studied the books every day and every night for a week, saying that they had almost blinded him. The episode suggests that his cabalistic knowledge was about to become much deeper. But he died two years later, having written nothing further on cabala.

[medium pause]
Out of the Ashes of the Phoenix
[medium pause]

Pico's limited but catalytic introduction of cabala generated a broad wave of followers across Europe. Franciscan monks, humanists, theologians, and precocious scholars used Pico's work as a framework for their own synthetic projects. The spread itself is important: even superficial and derivative cabalistic knowledge could move rapidly and function as intellectual currency in Renaissance culture.

Franciscus Georgius of Venice followed Pico's suggestion that cabala paralleled Pythagorean number symbolism. He extended the parallel by suggesting a correspondence between the ten sephiroth and the ten Aristotelian categories. In De harmonia mundi, cabalistic references were decorative elements in a universal eclectic synthesis, not the result of rigorous study.

Georgius applied gematria to divine names, discussed the sephiroth and their associated names of God, and asserted that the name of God is the same as God Himself. But he relied on a limited number of sources and used them as ornament rather than foundation. The similarity between Georgius's and Archangelus's lists of cabalistic authorities goes beyond shared names. They use precisely the same descriptive phrases, which indicates direct textual dependence.

Augustinus Justinianus Genuensis showed more genuine competence in Hebrew cabalistic sources than most of his contemporaries. In his Polyglot Psalter, he cited the Zohar, Sefer Yetzirah, and Siphra ditzenioutha. Even so, he remained ambivalent about whether Judaism was religion or superstition, and he refused to endorse magical uses of divine names.

Justinianus derived seventy-two divine names from the conjunction of Exodus, chapter fourteen, verses nineteen through twenty-one. That was a sophisticated cabalistic technique, probably drawn from Gikatilia or from Paul Ricci's translation of the Sha'are orah. Yet despite this erudition, Justinianus dissociated himself completely from wonder-working, magical, and theurgical uses of the names of God. Blau's dry judgment is that a good bishop would not compromise his orthodoxy for a mess of miracles.

John Colet's engagement with cabala was largely plagiaristic. His discussion in Two Treatises on the Hierarchies of Dionysius is nearly a word-for-word transcript of a passage from Pico's Apologia. John Fisher, by contrast, shows how the idea of cabala moved into mainstream Protestant polemic. In a sermon against Luther, Fisher used the word cabala to mean a secret oral tradition handed down from man to man by mouth only, not in writing. For Fisher, such oral authority could supplement written Scripture against Protestant claims about Scripture alone.

Colet himself wrote to Erasmus about Reuchlin's De arte cabalistica, saying that much of it seemed to him to be more miracles of words than of deeds, because Hebrew words seemed to contain mysteries in their characters and combinations. The remark captures both fascination and skepticism.

Later Italian and German followers of Pico repeated the same core moves. Peter Bongus, Johannes Baptista Crispus, Cesare d'Evoli, and the precocious Charles Montecuccolus identified the three highest sephiroth with the Trinity, equated the sephiroth with Pythagorean and Platonic concepts, and used cabala to confirm Christian doctrine against philosophical heterodoxy.

Bongus asserted without original study that almost all of Pythagoras's philosophy came from the cabalists, relying entirely on Pico, Georgius, and Reuchlin. D'Evoli defended the cabalistic sephiroth as superior to Platonic ideas because the sephiroth are individually differentiated, while Platonic ideas are all of like character. That argument shows how cabala could be recruited into internal Renaissance philosophical debates.

[medium pause]
Pythagoras Redivivus
[medium pause]

John Reuchlin became the defining figure of Christian cabalism. At first he was an amateur who could not correctly list the sephiroth. But through his two dialogues and his Hebrew scholarship, he gave Christian cabalism its most influential shape. His basic claim was that cabala preserved the lost doctrines of Pythagoras. He also used cabalistic techniques, especially gematria applied to the Tetragrammaton, to prove the Messiahship of Jesus. His influence spread from England to Poland and lasted a century and a half.

Reuchlin's first cabalistic dialogue, De verbo mirifico, appeared in 1494. It is driven less by genuinely cabalistic concerns than by Pythagorean ones. Its climax is the argument that the name of Jesus, YHSVH, is the wonder-working word because it adds the Hebrew letter shin to the Tetragrammaton, YHVH. The addition fuses the divine name with the element of fire and unites God and man.

The dialogue also reveals Reuchlin's early limitations. The Jewish character Baruch cannot correctly list the sephiroth. Reuchlin omits yesod entirely, treats variant names as separate sephiroth, and reverses the order of chesed and malchuth. At this stage, his cabalistic knowledge was too thin to support the arguments he was making.

The wonder-working word argument depends on the change from YHVH to YHSVH. As a five-letter name, it partakes of all the virtues of the number five. Reuchlin even argues that peoples who worshipped fire were, in that way, approaching the truth.

By the time of De arte cabalistica in 1517, Reuchlin had genuinely mastered Hebrew and had deeply studied Joseph Gikatilia. He could correctly list and explain the sephiroth. He presented the three exegetical methods: gematria, notarikon, and themurah. He also argued that the Tetragrammaton contained the entire doctrine of the Trinity in the names Ab, Ben, and Veruach Hakadosh: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

His most elaborate Christian cabalistic reinterpretation of a Jewish text was the forty-two-letter name flowing from the Tetragrammaton: Ab Elohim, Ben Elohim, Ruach Hakadosh Elohim, Shelosha Beachad, Achad Beshelosha. In English, that means: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God; Three in One and One in Three.

Here again, the point is less the sound of the formula than the move Reuchlin is making. A Jewish sacred-name tradition is being recoded so that it seems to speak the language of Christian Trinitarian doctrine.

Reuchlin's political motivation was explicit. Dedicated to Pope Leo the Tenth, De arte cabalistica argued for preservation of the Talmud by distinguishing it from cabala. The Talmud concerned the sensible world; cabala concerned the intellectual world. If cabala contained Christian truth, then that became a reason for papal protection of Hebrew books more generally.

Reuchlin's central interpretive thesis was that cabala preserved the lost doctrines of Pythagoras, and that Pythagorean number philosophy and the cabalistic sephiroth were fundamentally identical. This drove his synthetic project. But it also created a persistent confusion, throughout the later tradition, between numerical mysticism and genuine cabalistic thought.

In the second day of De arte cabalistica, the Pythagorean character Philolaus and the Mohammedan Marranus spend an entire day finding Greek philosophical parallels for the teachings of Simon, the Jewish cabalist. Their conclusion is that out of the infinite sea of the cabalists, Pythagoras brought his river into the fields of the Greeks. Reuchlin knew Gikatilia's Genat egoz and Sha'are orah, both in Hebrew and in Paul Ricci's Latin translation. But he seems to have known the Zohar only by name. That meant he was working from a pre-Zoharic layer of cabalism while believing that he had access to the full depth of the tradition.

Reuchlin's influence spread widely. It reached Georgius, Archangelus, Agrippa, English scholars such as Colet, Digby, and Howard, Earl of Northampton, and even a French conversation primer for Princess Mary. His Dominican opponents, especially Hoogstraten and the Cologne faculty, represented the opposite conviction: that cabalistic techniques were inherently dangerous to orthodox Christianity.

This conflict also reflected a structural Franciscan-Dominican divide. Franciscans such as Georgius, Archangelus, Thenaud, and Crispus were drawn to cabala through Joachimite number symbolism. Dominicans defended Thomistic rationalism and led the censorship of Hebrew books. The Reverend Henry Smith's sermon, around 1590, shows how Reuchlin's ideas could be transmitted, distorted, and reauthorized across generations. Smith misattributed to "Rabbi Hacadosh" a proof that the Messiah's mother would be a virgin named Mary. The proof actually derived from Reuchlin's De verbo mirifico.

[medium pause]
The Cabalistic Apostates
[medium pause]

A number of converted Jews used insider knowledge of cabala as a missionary tool. The most important was Paul Ricci, but Blau also discusses Rittangel, Philip d'Aquines, and Elchanan Paul. These converts used cabala to prove Christian doctrine to Jewish audiences. Ricci produced by far the most systematic and spiritually serious Christian cabalistic synthesis. He transformed cabala from a technique of doctrinal proof into a mystical itinerary of individual salvation.

Ricci's Introduction to the Lore of the Cabalists was the first genuinely systematic treatment of cabalistic doctrine in the Christian tradition. He presented cabala as a unified allegorical science of the Law of Moses. Through it, the individual soul moved from the loss caused by the Fall, through penitence, and finally to full redemption at the second advent of Jesus.

Ricci organized the Introduction around sixty-six numbered theses. These move from the purpose and nature of the Law, to the doctrine of the sephiroth, to the consequences of Adam's sin. Adam's sin separates malchuth from yesod and loses access to the three highest sephiroth. The movement culminates in messianic restoration, when the slave returns, the possession reverts, the reed pipes answer each other, and the great trumpet of the jubilee resounds.

What distinguishes Ricci from his predecessors is that he turns cabala into a way of life. It becomes a mystical path from sense to God for the individual cabalist. That is a transformation of Jewish racial and social mysticism into Christian individual soteriology.

This transformation also reveals a deep incompatibility between the Jewish sources and their Christian appropriation. Jewish mysticism is fundamentally social and racial in its conception of salvation. It is concerned with world-history, the redemption of all Israel, or the redemption of all mankind. Christian cabalism, especially in Ricci, recasts those collective doctrines as an itinerary for individual souls.

The difference is not simply the intensity of individual ecstasy. It lies in interpretation. Jewish mystics gave a social or racial meaning to their experiences. Ricci's Christian cabala centers on the individual's adherence to the Most High. Ricci also criticized Pico directly as not a great authority on cabala. The convert with genuine linguistic access to Hebrew sources understood how thin the Pican tradition really was.

The conversion of Jews through cabalistic argument was a recognized missionary strategy in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The standard arguments tried to prove the Trinity and the Messiahship of Jesus from the sephiroth. But the strategy ultimately failed, which suggests either that the arguments were too obscure for broad persuasion, or, as Mersenne argued, that cabalistic interpretation falsified the true appeal of Christianity.

Louis Carretus's Hebrew letter of 1553 to his son, explaining his conversion, rests entirely on the familiar argument that the superior triad of the sephirotic tree - crown, wisdom, and understanding - represents the Trinity. Jesus, as the second person, is equated with divine wisdom. The possibility of mass conversion on a cabalistic basis is shown by the conversion of 514 followers of Jacob Frank in the eighteenth century. But Blau treats that as possibly a historical accident rather than a validation of the intellectual strategy.

[medium pause]
The Fantastic Cabala
[medium pause]

Henry Cornelius Agrippa and the broader Renaissance magical tradition adopted cabala as part of occult philosophy. But Agrippa read Reuchlin through the lens of a magical system he had already formed. In his hands, cabala became a numerological adjunct of alchemy and astrology. Paracelsus went even further, using the word cabala while having no real conception of its meaning. By this point, the name could detach from the doctrine.

This is where cabala begins to drift from a doctrine into a prestige term. The word still carries authority, but its connection to Hebrew cabala becomes increasingly thin.

Agrippa's engagement began with a close study of Reuchlin's De verbo mirifico for his own magical purposes. His Three Books of Occult Philosophy, written in 1510 and published in 1531, incorporated the sephiroth and divine names from Reuchlin's better De arte cabalistica. But the whole system was subordinated to natural, celestial, and intellectual magic. Cabala served as one ingredient among many.

Agrippa organized the three worlds - elementary, celestial, and intellectual - according to Pico's schema from Recanati. In the third book, on the celestial world, he discussed divine names and sephiroth in a way thoroughly reminiscent of Reuchlin. The Abbot Tritheim warned Agrippa in 1510 not to publish his occult philosophy, advising him to speak of public things to the public, but of lofty and secret things only to the loftiest and most private friends. Tritheim's image was: hay to an ox, and sugar to a parrot.

In De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum, published in 1526, Agrippa renounced cabala as a rhapsodical superstition allied to theurgic magic. His argument was that since Jesus had come, the wonder-working name was simply Iesu, and elaborate cabalistic speculation was vain. But he reversed that position by 1531, when he published the revised Three Books of Occult Philosophy.

Agrippa's renunciation defined cabala as a double science. One half was cosmology. The other was Marcana, a symbolic theology of God, angels, sacred names, letters, numbers, figures, things, ornaments of letters, lines, punctuation, and accents, all taken as signs of profound mysteries. The brief skeptical period between Agrippa's two credulous phases, when the skeptic was uppermost in him, shows how cabalism's appeal could temporarily collapse under the weight of its own contradictions and then reassert itself.

Paracelsus marks the extreme limit of cabala's separation from its actual content. He used the word cabala to mean magical astronomy, a part of magic, a kind of practice not identical with necromancy, and something related to the astral body. None of these meanings bears any relation to Hebrew cabala. By the mid-sixteenth century, cabala had become a free-floating term of occult prestige.

A. E. Waite observed that Paracelsus did not respect or quote authorities and owed little to tradition. When he concerned himself with the Kabbalah, Waite said, he became his own Kabbalist. The Jesuit Martin del Rio later identified four types of cabalists. He concluded that the fourth group, who invoked seventy-two angelic names to produce miracles, practiced an entirely blasphemous and magical cabala. Even the sephirotists, his third group, began to smooth the path for superstition.

[medium pause]
The Very Christian Cabala
[medium pause]

Jean Thenaud's manuscript poem La Saincte et trescrestienne cabale, written around 1519, was the earliest complete French work on Christian cabalism. It used the sephiroth and the doctrine of worlds as scaffolding for moral instruction to King Francis the First. Thenaud transformed the Hebrew mystical system into a Christian hierarchical cosmology, while explicitly condemning magical and Jewish "vicious cabalas." His work shows how cabala could circulate in manuscript as courtly and didactic material.

Thenaud dedicated the poem to Francis the First and presented it as a revelation from the spirit of the king's deceased father. Its cabalistic content is organized into three books. The first reinterprets the sephiroth as triangular representations of the Trinity and the angelic orders. The second treats the three worlds, with man as a miraculous fourth. The third presents a number system linking divine unity, angelic tenaries, and celestial composite numbers.

Thenaud transforms the three highest sephiroth - keter, chochmah, and binah - into the incomprehensible triangle of divine glory representing the Trinity. He does this without naming them explicitly. The priority is not exact cabalistic exposition but trinitarian confirmation. His account of man is also telling. Man is the miraculous microcosm who has neither form nor place of his own, but can make himself whatever he wishes to be. That is Thenaud's solution to the problem of free will inside a deterministic doctrine of worlds, and it echoes the cabalistic doctrine of Adam kadmon.

Thenaud explicitly condemns vicious cabalas. These include Hebrew magical practice, the works of Hermes Trismegistus, and Arabic occultism. He defines the true and holy cabala as belonging to Christians who follow the Evangelists. This move empties cabala of its Jewish content while claiming its authority.

His polemic is sharp. If the Hebrews really possess mastery of magical cabalistic technique, he asks, why have they not used it to improve their own situation in exile and persecution? Pererius used a similar argument. Thenaud declares that the true cabalists are Christians who receive the Evangelists, receive what God preached in this lower world, and worthily receive the sacraments.

The broader French context around Thenaud includes predecessors such as Lazarelli and Lefevre d'Etaples, and successors such as Postel, Champier, Pontus de Tyard, Philip de Mornay, and Jean Belot. Each touched cabala in different ways. Blau treats Thenaud as a surrogate for a much larger body of manuscript cabalistic writing in Europe that remains unanalyzed.

Guillaume Postel made the most valuable French scholarly contribution to cabalism. He translated the Sefer Yetzirah into Latin and also translated sections of the Zohar. He used cabalistic material in works on world concord and the truth of Christianity. More broadly, the concentration of cabalistic manuscripts in European and American libraries is heaviest in the mid-seventeenth century, when printed publication declined. That suggests the tradition retreated into private scholarly circulation as its public credibility weakened.

[medium pause]
The Erudites
[medium pause]

By the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, cabalistic knowledge had spread from the pioneering interpreters into a broader class of European scholars. Historians, philologians, exegetes, anti-magic polemicists, and theologians used it as intellectual currency, as proof-texting for doctrine, or as a target of learned refutation. Even a fad with no lasting philosophical significance could achieve remarkable geographic and disciplinary breadth.

Historians of culture such as Ricchieri in 1517, Garzonus in 1588, and Horn in 1655 incorporated cabalistic material from Pico and Reuchlin into encyclopedic works. They repeated the same lists of authorities, the same descriptions of gematria and notarikon, and the same identification of cabala as the oldest Hebrew wisdom. Within a generation of Pico, cabalistic knowledge had become standardized reference material.

Ricchieri asserted in 1517 that cabalistic knowledge was the oldest and truest form of Hebrew thought, because established opinion held that it had been made known to Moses by God. That was only twenty-five years after Pico introduced the subject. Garzonus, in 1588, repeated Reuchlin's praise of Joseph Gikatilia, saying that no one had written of this science more skillfully, distinctly, or clearly than Rabbi Joseph ben Abraham Castiliensis of Salerno, in the book called A Garden of Nuts.

Writers on magic and the occult sciences engaged cabala primarily in order to police the boundary between legitimate religious mystery and demonic magic. This included Agrippa's critics, such as del Rio and Pererius; his defenders, such as Dorneus; and students of divination, such as Peucer. Peucer is especially revealing because he condemned practical wonder-working claims while still appreciating numerological proofs about the date of Christ's birth derived from the Hebrew letters of "Breshith."

Peucer condemned the cabalistic claim to perform miracles through divine names as no lie more offensive or insulting to God. Yet he found merit in cabalistic numerology when it produced the claim that Christ was taken up into heaven in the year 3996 after creation, just four years short of the prophesied four millennia. Della Riviera uniquely learned from Reuchlin the doctrine of Adam kadmon. He presented the sephiroth as both the limbs of primordial man and as a tree whose roots are the three highest sephiroth, whose trunk is tifereth, and whose branches are the remaining six.

The controversy over the antiquity of Hebrew vowel points drew cabalistic texts into academic philological debate. Cappellus, in 1624, argued that cabalists never derived interpretations from vowel points. Since they worked from consonants and letters alone, by inversion, metathesis, and many kinds of permutation, the vowel points must be a later invention. Buxtorf the Younger replied that the Zohar and Bahir were ancient texts and that they treated vocalization as part of the Oral Law.

Fabricius's commentary on Schem Hamphorasch supplies one of Blau's harshest examples. Blau calls it perhaps one of the foulest comments ever published in a theological work. Fabricius says that the sacred name has degenerated into "Scham happeresch" - there is the dung.

Trinitarian theologians such as Peter Allix used cabalistic material because it offered Jewish sources that could be twisted into support for Trinitarian doctrine against Unitarians. The main resources were Reuchlin's De arte cabalistica and Recanati's commentary. Allix quoted Recanati more than thirty times and searched Reuchlin for every possible Trinitarian suggestion. At the same time, he frankly described cabalistic methods as justly ridiculous and more vain or useless than almost any other occupation.

Johannes Hoornbeeck rejected cabala as producing great follies with great effort, yet still treated it systematically in his missionary work on converting Jews. Across the erudites, whether they were ardent opponents, neutral encyclopedists, or cautious users, the pattern confirms Blau's thesis: to refer to cabala was a form of scholarly ostentation. It showed that a writer was au courant.

And in Conclusion
[medium pause]

The Christian interpretation of cabala was never a unified movement. It was a collection of individually motivated projects. Pico wanted universal synthesis. Reuchlin wanted a Pythagorean revival. Agrippa wanted occult philosophy. Ricci wanted a missionary tool and a path of salvation. Thenaud wanted moral instruction for a monarch. Cabala's chameleonic quality let each of them find what he was already seeking.

No single stereotype describes the Christian interpreters of cabala. They came from many fields of knowledge and were moved by different intellectual projects. Yet each believed he had found in cabala the aid he needed. That shows that cabala's appeal lay partly in its adaptability to diverse agendas, not in the coherence of the doctrine itself.

Blau's five primary types are Pico, the universal synthesizer; Reuchlin, the Pythagorean revivalist; Agrippa, the occult philosopher; Ricci, the missionary converter; and Thenaud, the moral instructor of a monarch. None of the Christian interpreters knew much about cabala. Even so, each could draw from its philosophical system, canons of interpretation, techniques, or hermeneutic rules the assistance he wanted.

The speed of diffusion is one of the book's final points. Cabalistic knowledge spread across Europe within a generation of Pico, moving from Italy to England, France, Germany, and eventually Scandinavia, without mechanical means of diffusion. That demonstrates the Renaissance's capacity for cultural transmission through humanist networks, travel, and the book trade.

The period was transitional. The old order was changing, but the new order had not yet taken its place. At such a moment, any road could look worthy of exploration, especially one associated with Hebrew: the oldest language, and the language of divine revelation. Many Christian interpreters of cabala were pioneer Christian Hebraists. They were drawn either by humanist interest in language itself, or by Biblical study's concern with Hebrew as the language of Scripture. Those two motivations gave cabala its entrance into Christian scholarship, even though the movement itself did not endure.
