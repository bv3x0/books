# Hyperculture

## Metadata
- Thesis: Globalization, accelerated by new media, de-sites and de-facticizes culture, dissolving culture's ties to blood, soil, and place into a borderless 'hyperculture'—a dense side by side of heterogeneous forms in which the human becomes a 'hypercultural tourist,' a homo liber freed from thrownness and origin.
- Topics: globalization and culture, de-facticization and de-siting, hybridity and hyperculturality, identity and inwardness, critique of Heidegger and site-fundamentalism
- Categories: philosophy, social science
- Author: Byung-Chul Han
- Year: 2022
- Collections: Byung-Chul Han
- Mode: quick

## Tourist in a Hawaiian Shirt

Han opens with the suspicion that the very concept of culture may be dissolving, and asks whether the emerging 'tourist in a Hawaiian shirt' marks the end of culture or a new culture of open, boundless freedom.

* <span id="hyperculture-001"></span>**Han uses Nigel Barley's claim that fundamental concepts like culture will cease to exist and that 'we are all more or less tourists in Hawaiian shirts' to frame the book's central question about what culture becomes after the end of place-bound culture.**
    - "the true key to the future is that fundamental concepts such as culture will cease to exist" —Nigel Barley
    - Microsoft's slogan 'Where do you want to go today?' serves as the book's epigraph and recurring emblem of de-facticization

## Culture as Home

Han contrasts Hegel's genealogical insight that Greek culture arose from the 'advent of foreigners' with Hegel's later insistence on Europe as 'home,' arguing that place-bound 'national happiness' depends on a constitutive blindness and deafness to the foreign.

* <span id="hyperculture-002"></span>**Hegel holds that Greek culture arose from heterogeneity—'the advent of foreigners' and a colluvies (mud, hotchpotch, confusion)—so that the presence of the foreign is necessary for the formation of one's own, since spirit acquires the power to realize itself only through 'heterogeneity in itself.'**
    - Prometheus originates from the Caucasus; the Greek people developed 'from a colluvies'
    - Regarding European identity, Hegel abandons this and invokes Europe as 'home,' degrading the foreign to mere 'matter'; by his own theory this self-contentment would lead to spiritual rigor mortis
* <span id="hyperculture-003"></span>**Herder argues European culture is impure—'a plant sprung from roman, greek, and arabic feed,' a bastard culture—and that 'national happiness' depends on a soul's deafness that forgets its own manifold dispositions and elevates a part to the whole, responding to the foreign with contempt and disgust as 'sickness.'**
    - "all the cultivation of the east, west, and north of Europe, is a plant sprung from roman, greek, and arabic feed" —Herder
    - The soul creates a 'concert' from a few awakened tones and no longer senses the unawakened ones that silently support it, so happiness rests on blindness and deafness
* <span id="hyperculture-004"></span>**By contrast the tourist in a Hawaiian shirt has a happiness born of the abolition of facticity and detachment from the 'here,' inhabiting a hypermarket of culture where the foreign is not sickness but something new to be appropriated—raising whether this de-facticization increases freedom or whether happiness ultimately requires boundaries and sites.**
    - Han asks whether de-siting will provoke a new age of natives, hermits, ascetics and 'site fundamentalists'

## Hypertext and Hyperculture

Drawing on Ted Nelson's hypertext, Han argues that culture, like reality, is 'deeply intertwingled' and now bursts its borders into a hyperculture organized by links rather than boundaries, a de-naturalized 'more' of culture that provokes fundamentalist backlash.

* <span id="hyperculture-005"></span>**Ted Nelson's hypertext theory treats the world itself as a hypertext—everything 'deeply intertwingled' in a centreless 'structangle'—so that linear and hierarchical structures are forced and artificial, and hypertext promises liberation from that compulsion.**
    - "Everything is deeply intertwingled" —Ted Nelson
    - Nelson names his hypertextual system 'Xanadu' after Coleridge's Kubla Khan, sketching a golden X resembling McDonald's arches that welcomes the 'mindhungry traveller'
* <span id="hyperculture-006"></span>**Culture today has lost the story, theology, and teleology that gave it homogeneous unity; globalization de-distances cultural space, condensing heterogeneous contents side by side so cultures implode into a hyperculture—'more culture' precisely because de-naturalization (liberation from blood and soil) intensifies culturalization.**
    - Globalization accumulates and condenses across both space and time, de-distancing not only different sites but different time frames into a simultaneous side by side
    - The feeling of 'hyper' (not trans-, inter-, or multi-) is the most precise expression of today's culture
* <span id="hyperculture-007"></span>**A hyperculture without centre, God, or sites triggers resistance experienced as the trauma of loss, so re-theologization, re-mythologization and re-nationalization are common reactions—hypercultural de-siting must confront a fundamentalism of sites.**
    - Coleridge's Kubla Khan hears 'ancestral voices prophesying war' amid the seething earth—read by Han as a possible war of cultures against hyperculture

## The Eros of Interconnectedness

Using Flusser's typology of time, Han argues the information society's 'universe of bits' has no mythical or historical horizon but offers an overabundance of possibilities that de-facticizes Dasein—releasing it from Heidegger's 'thrownness' into a freer, projected 'homo liber.'

* <span id="hyperculture-008"></span>**Flusser distinguishes three forms of time—the plane-like time of the image (mythical), the linear time of the book (historical), and the point-like time of the bit—and argues today's de-theologized 'universe of points' surrounds Dasein with freely hovering possibilities, promising greater freedom because the future is everywhere one turns.**
    - "the void that I am is not passive, but like a vortex it sucks in all the possibilities that surround it" —Vilém Flusser
    - Interconnectedness expands the future by putting one's own future at the disposal of the other and thereby gaining the other's future—Flusser links this to acknowledgement and love (Eros)
* <span id="hyperculture-009"></span>**The excess of possibilities exceeds facticity and de-facticizes Dasein into a homo liber: contemporary existence is 'being projected' rather than 'thrownness,' dis-inheriting Dasein from heritage and tradition, which Microsoft's slogan 'Where do you want to go today?' encodes.**
    - Against Heidegger's resoluteness that comes back to inherited factical possibilities, Han asserts excess has a de-facticizing effect that increases freedom
    - The 'hypercultural tourist' is already a tourist at home—still here, already there—and never arrives at a final destination
* <span id="hyperculture-010"></span>**Heidegger sees interconnecting media as leveling differences into a 'dictatorship of the they,' but Han rejects the idea that media produce mere monotony, noting Heidegger's philosophy of dwelling and site is an attempt to re-facticize Dasein against diversity.**
    - "This Being-with-one-another dissolves one's own Dasein completely into the kind of Being of 'the Others'" —Heidegger

## Fusion Food

Han argues globalization proceeds dialectically—it 'allows dialects to emerge'—so that hyperculture is not a bland monoculture but a diversifying pool of forms; the McDonald's/Coca-Cola homogenization thesis misreads a dynamic that actually thrives on difference.

* <span id="hyperculture-011"></span>**Catchphrases like 'McDonald's culture' misrepresent the cultural dynamic: there are probably more Chinese restaurants than McDonald's branches, Parisians eat more sushi than beef burgers, and even McDonald's must vary its menu locally, while American 'fusion food' draws freely on the hypercultural pool to create diversity impossible under purity.**
    - 'Fusion cuisine' thrives on differences rather than throwing everything into one pot; the non-traditionalist local becomes 'Hawaiian toast with veal sausage'
    - Against George Ritzer's The McDonaldization of Society, Han argues globalization is not the same as rationalization and cannot rationalize the diversity of tastes and smells out of existence
* <span id="hyperculture-012"></span>**The creation of difference is intrinsic to taste and enjoyment—cultural homogenization would end enjoyment and make no economic sense—so hyperculture de-facticizes taste itself, opening it to the new; fusion food makes one think of design rather than Being, since hyperculture de-facticizes Being into design and strips it of thrownness.**
    - Heidegger constantly tries to re-facticize the world—invoking the 'scent of the oak wood' in 'The Pathway'—against the world's hyperculturality

## Hybrid Culture

Han examines Bhabha's concept of hybridity and Heidegger's tropes of bridge and boundary, arguing that hybridity remains too dialectical and power-bound (colonizer/colonized) to capture hyperculture's playful, aesthetic realm of free appropriation.

* <span id="hyperculture-013"></span>**The hybridity of culture is nothing new: Herder's bastard European culture and Hegel's Greek colluvies already reveal that spirit itself is hybrid, and etymologically 'spirit' (Geist) points toward uncanniness (Un-heim-lichkeit)—the ghost, half alive and half dead—rather than the peace of being-at-home.**
    - 'Spirit' originally means excitation, agitation, losing one's composure, and is another word for 'ghost'
* <span id="hyperculture-014"></span>**Homi Bhabha's hybridity treats identity as an effect of differences produced in an 'interstitial passage,' using the metaphors of the stairwell and Heidegger's bridge, and grants hybridity subversive force that lets 'denied knowledges' enter dominant discourse and estrange its authority.**
    - "The hither and thither of the stairwell...prevents identities at either end of it from settling into primordial polarities" —Homi K. Bhabha
    - Bhabha quotes Heidegger's bridge but omits 'before the divinities,' distorting Heidegger's theological trope of gathering
* <span id="hyperculture-015"></span>**Heidegger's bridge and boundary are unsuitable for hybridity because his world is governed by a strict dialectical symmetry—Here and There in antagonistic tension, gathered 'before the divinities'—that permits no hybrid buzzing of voices; his theologization of the world de-hybridizes it and preserves 'authenticity,' 'origin' and 'essence.'**
    - For Heidegger the relation precedes the things: a bridge does not connect pre-existing banks but creates them, a 'prior casting-over' within which spaces emerge
    - In acoustic terms the hybrid is 'noise,' but Heidegger's gathering world would be silent
* <span id="hyperculture-016"></span>**Bhabha remains too dialectical—still in thrall to the agonal tension of colonizer/colonized, master/slave—so that hybridity is tied by its conceptual history to the racist-colonial complex of power and cannot reach the playful, aesthetic realm of hyperculture, which Han identifies with Schiller's 'third happy realm of play and semblance.'**
    - "the aesthetic impulse to form constructs unnoticed a third happy realm of play and of semblance in which the fetters of all circumstance are taken from man" —Friedrich Schiller
    - This realm gives 'freedom by means of freedom' and would be populated by homines liberi et hilari

## The Hyphenization of Culture

Han proposes Deleuze and Guattari's rhizome, not Bhabha's dialectical interstice, as the model for hyperculture: a centreless, memoryless, non-signifying 'logic of the AND' that hyphenates culture into a friendly, netlike hyphen-culture.

* <span id="hyperculture-017"></span>**Bhabha's model of antagonistic, conflict-laden 'negotiation' cannot capture the hypercultural side by side, which is governed not by 'either/or' or contradiction but by 'as well as' and mutual appropriation; hyperculture is more open and less dialectical than hybrid culture.**
    - "When I talk of negotiation rather than negation, it is to convey a temporality...a dialectic without the emergence of a teleological or transcendent History" —Homi K. Bhabha
* <span id="hyperculture-018"></span>**Deleuze and Guattari's rhizome—a non-centred plurality where any point connects to any other, illustrated by the mutual 'becoming' of orchid and wasp—de-substantializes and de-internalizes culture into a memoryless, rooted-yet-scattered structure that models hyperculture better than the bridge, stairwell, or negotiation table.**
    - "any point of a rhizome can be connected to anything other, and must be...very different from the tree or root, which plots a point, fixes an order" —Deleuze and Guattari
    - A rhizome has no memory; national culture as tree-like culture would cover up the underlying rhizomatic structures
* <span id="hyperculture-019"></span>**The rhizomatic in-between is not antagonistic but conjunctive—a 'stream that undermines its banks and picks up speed in the middle'—so its non-signifying 'logic of the AND' hyphenates culture into a friendly hyphen-culture; the pun on 'hyphae' (fungal filaments forming a centreless, unrooted mycelium web) captures this de-sited weave.**
    - "the fabric of the rhizome is the conjunction, 'and...and...and...'. This conjunction carries enough force to shake and uproot the verb 'to be'" —Deleuze and Guattari
    - Greek hyphé means 'woven'—a net or web; hyphae fuse into a rootless mycelium with little inwardness

## The Age of Comparison

Han reads Nietzsche's 'age of comparison' as anticipating the de-siting of culture after the death of God, but notes Nietzsche's aestheticism tends toward re-teleologization by demanding that 'lower' moralities die out for 'higher' ones—posing whether hyperculture's future rests on selection or on the friendliness of the AND.

* <span id="hyperculture-020"></span>**Nietzsche foresaw that the death of God presaged the end of the site's significance (God was also the God of sites), producing an 'age of comparison' in which the less men are bound by tradition, the more all cultures, moralities and artistic genres can be experienced side by side—something impossible when each culture enjoyed only localized domination.**
    - "Who is there who now still feels a strong compulsion to attach himself and his posterity to a particular place?" —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - Nietzsche imagined a comparative philosophy uniting European-American restlessness with inherited Asian tranquillity, lifting the barriers of religion, states, and prejudices of race
* <span id="hyperculture-021"></span>**For Nietzsche the age of comparison is not merely a side by side but an age of selection governed by 'aesthetic sensibility' that eliminates lower moralities for a higher one, so his aestheticism tends toward re-teleologization and re-theologization; Han counters by asking whether the higher morality of the future will instead rest on the friendliness of the AND rather than an economy of selection.**
    - "This is the age of comparison! It is the source of its pride - but...also of its suffering" —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - The problem is how the distinction between lower and higher forms can be drawn at all

## The De-Auratization of Culture

Han argues globalization does more than exchange between sites—it de-internalizes the site itself, extinguishing Benjamin's aura through a site-less simultaneity of styles and producing 'hyperreality' rather than a mere loss of depth to be lamented.

* <span id="hyperculture-022"></span>**Software slogans like Microsoft's 'Where do you want to go today?', Linux's 'Where do you want to go tomorrow?' and Disney's 'Are you ready to go?' register a seismic shift in Being, bidding farewell to a specific Here that gave Being its auratic depth—or the semblance of an aura.**
    - "In post-Biblical times, God is called 'site'." —Peter Handke
* <span id="hyperculture-023"></span>**Benjamin derives aura from an object's 'unique existence in a particular place'; contemporary globalization changes the site as such, de-internalizing it and removing the 'tip' that gives it soul, so that cultural forms detach from their sites and join in a hypercultural simultaneity where the here-and-now yields to site-less repetition and aura deteriorates.**
    - "aura is the unique apparition of a distance" —Walter Benjamin
    - The decay of aura follows the masses' desire to 'get closer' to things through de-distancing (Ent-Fernung); de-siting and de-distancing cause each other
* <span id="hyperculture-024"></span>**De-auratization is de-facticization: de-sited cultures are not mere authenticity-less replications but achieve another Being that shines in the absence of the auratic—hyperreality—exemplified by Hearst's Xanadu-like castle at San Simeon, where forgeries seamlessly combine with genuine articles, sublating fake and genuine into a third category of Being.**
    - Umberto Eco in 'Travels in Hyperreality' condemns Hearst for 'bringing the past down to the level of today's life' and calls Xanadu a 'masterpiece of bricolage, haunted by horror vacui'; Han reframes this bricolage as the hypercultural logic of the AND
    - Hearst's Xanadu, like Disneyland, is part of California's touristic landscape and oddly resembles Nelson's hypertextual Xanadu

## Pilgrims and Tourists

Against Bauman's claim that modern humans are pilgrims, Han argues the hypercultural tourist abolishes the pilgrim's asymmetry of pain between Here and There, dwelling fully in a symmetrical space of Being-Here with neither longing nor fear.

* <span id="hyperculture-025"></span>**Han rejects Bauman's thesis that the modern human is a pilgrim: the pilgrim (peregrinus) is a stranger on earth, on the way to a special There amid uncertainty and the possibility of going astray, whereas modernity overcomes this asymmetry between Here and There by progressing toward a better Here.**
    - "lending continuity to the episodic and making a whole out of the fragmentary" —Zygmunt Bauman
    - Pilgrimage is a pre-modern trope that re-theologizing thinkers like Heidegger fall back on; Heidegger's 'Pathway' was a pilgrim's route that re-auratizes and re-theologizes 'site-ness'
* <span id="hyperculture-026"></span>**The hypercultural tourist inhabits a symmetrical space of immanence with no asymmetry between Here and There and no 'asymmetry of pain,' moving from one Here to another Here—so the place he is at is not a site in the strong sense, and the Here should be lower-cased or struck through to mark de-auratization and de-theologization.**
    - Against Heidegger's crossed-out Being that re-auratizes, the struck-through Here takes away Being's auratic depth
    - Because the hypercultural tourist seeks no definitive arrival, he has always already arrived and travels in a hyperspace of events—experiencing culture as 'cul-tour'
* <span id="hyperculture-027"></span>**Bauman's tourists remain romantic pilgrims who postulate a home that stays forever in the future tense, torn between homesickness (a 'dream of belonging') and the fear of home-boundedness, but he fails to recognize the wholly different hypercultural tourist who lives fully in the present tense and knows neither longing nor fear.**
    - "Homesickness is a dream of belonging - of being for once, of the place, not merely in" —Zygmunt Bauman

## Windows and Monads

Han reads Nelson's hypertext as an exercise in freedom that 'de-internalizes' the reader through windowing, contrasting it with Leibniz's windowless monads, but warns that screens can also produce isolated monads-with-windows who may again need God.

* <span id="hyperculture-028"></span>**Nelson conceives hypertext as emancipation: linear-hierarchical order rests on compulsion and forces the reader into passivity, whereas hypertext's 'windowing' lets the 'active' reader lay coloured pathways through the text according to his own inclinations, so different articles become different versions of and pathways through the same work.**
    - "sequential text, to which we are funnelled by tradition and technology, forces us to write the same sequences for everyone" —Ted Nelson
    - Experience of the world is a 'step through the window'; in this hypertextual universe there are no isolated units and thus no 'subjects'
* <span id="hyperculture-029"></span>**The hypertextual universe contrasts with Leibniz's, whose monads are windowless 'substances' closed on all sides and communicating only through God's pre-established harmony; windowing robs the house of its monadic inwardness and de-internalizes its inhabitant into a hypercultural tourist.**
    - A window both opens to the outside and seals me off, so windowing can also produce monads-with-windows whose Being-in-the-world becomes a Being-before-a-window, approaching the old windowless monads—will they too have to call on God?

## Odradek

Han reads Kafka's Odradek and the northern nomads as hybrid, houseless counter-figures to the 'father of the house,' but argues Kafka's negativity produces only ghosts haunting the house rather than the hypercultural tourist—though Odradek's patchwork, 'coloured Self' anticipates hypercultural identity.

* <span id="hyperculture-030"></span>**Kafka's Odradek embodies a hybrid, patchwork identity—hybrid in name (Slavonic/German), in form (a star-shaped spool of knotted broken threads of varied colours), and above all in refusing the boundaries of the house—so he becomes the care of the 'Hausvater,' guardian of home, nation and fatherland.**
    - "'And where do you live?' 'No fixed abode', he says and laughs." —Odradek (Kafka)
    - Odradek's identity is cobbled together, in his 'own way perfectly finished'; his laughter has no lungs behind it and sounds like the rustling of fallen leaves
* <span id="hyperculture-031"></span>**Kafka's northern nomads in 'An Old Manuscript' are the wholly other—abominating dwelling houses, incapable of language or sign, communicating by screeching like jackdaws—and, like Odradek, reduce the father-figure (Emperor, fatherland) to helplessness; but neither is a hypercultural tourist, since Odradek always faithfully returns to the house and no 'windowing' occurs in Kafka.**
    - The negativity of Kafka's nomadism only produces ghosts that haunt the house, so Kafka remains a hostage of the 'house' or the 'father'
    - Yet Odradek's friendly, serene hybridity and 'coloured Self' of knotted threads of varied colours resemble the patchwork structure of hypercultural identity

## Hypercultural Identity

Han argues the collapse of meaning-giving horizons produces point-like time and fragmentation but also enables a new practice of freedom: individuals cobble together multicoloured 'patchwork' identities and religions from the hypercultural pool.

* <span id="hyperculture-032"></span>**The present age is marked by the collapse of horizons and contexts that provide meaning, producing fragmentation, pointillism, pluralization and a 'naked,' point-like time devoid of narration; the decay of horizons is felt as a painful void and narrative crisis but also makes possible a new practice of freedom.**
    - Against Leibniz's universe where every being has a fixed place in divine harmony and no monad looks out a window, today's Being dissipates into a hyperspace of possibilities that only whizz around
* <span id="hyperculture-033"></span>**When the horizon disintegrates into multicoloured possibilities, individuals 'window' from one possibility to another and cobble together a multicoloured 'coloured Self,' a patchwork identity; patchwork religion and additive hypercultural art likewise draw from the pool, pursuing no strong truth and having nothing to reveal.**
    - A plurality of colours is not always vitality—in religion it may herald the end, annihilation
    - Hyperculture does not homogenize but triggers increasing individualization, as individuals follow their own inclinations to assemble identities from the hypercultural pool of life-forms

## Interculturality, Multiculturality and Transculturality

Han distinguishes hyperculturality from inter-, multi-, and transculturality, arguing all three presuppose an essentialist, inward, place-bound notion of culture, whereas the Far East's relational, non-substantial self-understanding tends 'naturally' toward the borderless side by side of hyperculture.

* <span id="hyperculture-034"></span>**Interculturality and multiculturality are Western phenomena tied historically to nationalism and colonialism and philosophically to an essentialist notion of culture that gives culture a 'soul'; the 'inter' means dialogue between essentialist cultures, while multiculturality merely manages given differences through 'integration' and 'tolerance,' offering little mutual penetration.**
    - Interculturality functions like intersubjectivity/interpersonality, establishing a relation only after subjects or persons are already there
    - Bhabha's 'interstitial transition' is a first step toward de-substantialization but does not lead to hypercultural windowing
* <span id="hyperculture-035"></span>**The ontology of culture as substance is alien to the Far East, which sees the human not as a person with a soul but as a 'between'—the Chinese sign for human includes the sign for 'between'—so before any 'inter' the human is already a between; this porous, open, non-inward, non-remembering culture tends naturally toward hyperculture and toward technical networking.**
    - There is no fixed or consistent translation for 'interculturality' in the Far East, only artificial-sounding paraphrases
    - Far Eastern thought is based on relation not substance and thinks in networks, which is why networking progressed faster there; its globalization is not 'place polygamy' or Western multiculturalism yet still tends toward hyperculturalism
* <span id="hyperculture-036"></span>**Transculturality (Welsch) stresses the 'crossing of borders,' but hyperculturality involves no such crossing, hiking, or transit: it is an immediate borderless side by side where one does not hike but 'browses' what is presently available, producing a singular here—today's culture is marked not by trans, multi, or inter but by the hyper (accumulation, networking, compression).**
    - "today's cultural formations...pass across, transgress the classical borders between cultures as a matter of course" —Wolfgang Welsch
    - Welsch claims culture always had a 'transcultural design,' but hyperculturality presupposes specific historical, technical and media processes and is a phenomenon of today—Greek, Roman and Renaissance cultures were not hypercultural

## Appropriation

Against the reigning taboo on appropriation as reduction of the Other to the Same, Han argues appropriation is essential to education, identity, renewal and consumption, and that hyperculture replaces the alien with the novel and timidity with curiosity.

* <span id="hyperculture-037"></span>**The recent paradigm of the 'radically Other' makes appropriation seem sinful and understanding suspect, tabooizing or apotheosizing the Other; but appropriation is not per se violent—colonial exploitation, which destroys the Other for the Same, must be strictly distinguished from appropriation, which is essential to education and identity, since only an idiot or a god could live without it.**
    - Appropriation transforms both the appropriating subject and the appropriated Other, producing difference rather than perpetuating the Same; the exoticizing gaze, by contrast, merely consolidates one's own
* <span id="hyperculture-038"></span>**Hyperculture knows no 'radically Other' that causes terror; the alien makes way for the novel and timidity is replaced by curiosity, so the destructive division between one's own and the alien relaxes into a division between old and new, and one's own is acquired from the hyperspace rather than inherited.**
    - What one seeks to protect and exclude from exchange becomes mere folklore
    - Consumption is a practice of appropriation: the things we surround ourselves with create the substance of our selves, and criticism of consumption presupposes a myth of pure inwardness (a 'soul') unknown in the Far East, where the inward is seen as an effect of the outward

## On Lasting Peace

Han reads Kant's guarantee of perpetual peace through 'Nature' and the 'spirit of commerce' alongside Nietzsche's call to amalgamate nations, arguing that the blending of races, religions and languages driven by globalization troubles power and can secure a lasting peace against 'artificial nationalism.'

* <span id="hyperculture-039"></span>**For Kant the state of nature is a state of war, so peace must be formally constituted; yet 'Nature, the great artist' guarantees perpetual peace—paradoxically by keeping nations separate through linguistic and religious differences and equilibrium of forces, and by uniting them through the 'spirit of commerce,' which is incompatible with war.**
    - Just years earlier in the Critique of Judgment, Kant praised war as sublime and condemned prolonged peace for breeding a 'mere commercial spirit' with 'base selfishness, cowardice, and softness'
    - The 'lasting' peace of commerce that weakens morality becomes the guarantee of the 'perpetual' peace that should rest on morality, so there is not such a big difference between them
* <span id="hyperculture-040"></span>**The blending of races, religions and languages—which Kant would not approve—also contributes to lasting peace because it is detrimental to power: power presupposes continuity, and in a discontinuous, ever-changing space power is hard to establish, so blending troubles forms of power that construct a pure culture or race to legitimize rule.**
    - "these circumstances are necessarily bringing with them a weakening and finally an abolition of nations...a mixed race...must come into being" —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - Nietzsche calls 'artificial nationalism' a forcibly imposed state of siege requiring cunning, force and falsehood, and urges working 'for the amalgamation of nations'; Nature would help this mixing without resorting to power
    - Nietzsche did not always affirm the mixing of cultures, elsewhere claiming it leads to 'much ugliness' and a 'darkening of the world'; hyperculture, as un-bounding, is a culture beyond beautiful and ugly

## Culture of Friendliness

Han argues that since hypercultural diversity allows no common horizon or rules, coexistence requires 'friendliness'—an unregulated openness that actively appropriates the other—rather than irony, tact, politeness or toleration, all of which merely preserve monadic inwardness and the status quo.

* <span id="hyperculture-041"></span>**Rorty's liberal ironist has continuing doubts about her 'final vocabulary' and avoids humiliating others, but irony produces only a community of considerate monads with sensitive feelers, not a networking effect; its negativity and consciousness of contingency belong to modernity/postmodernity, not to hypermodernity, and cannot grasp hyperculture's infinite affirmation.**
    - "the recognition of a common susceptibility to humiliation is the only social bond that is needed" —Richard Rorty
    - The interculturally blended spices and smells are not ironic—there is no ironic sense of smell—so at its deepest level culture is not ironic
* <span id="hyperculture-042"></span>**Tact (Gadamer), politeness and toleration all exhibit only limited openness: tact and politeness are bound to cultural codes and keep others at a distance, while toleration—practised by the normalizing majority against deviating minorities—perpetuates the distinction between one's own and the other and tacitly solidifies the status quo, making all three conservative and not friendly.**
    - "the achievement of undemonstrable tact is to hit the target and to discipline the application of the universal, the moral law" —Hans-Georg Gadamer
    - Toleration merely tolerates minorities as something base and inferior, whereas friendliness actively affirms, appropriates and lifts up what lies outside as part of one's own
* <span id="hyperculture-043"></span>**Friendliness acts without rules, and precisely through this irregularity produces maximum cohesion with minimum connectedness—a 'continuum of discontinuities' that makes the hypercultural side by side of what is different a liveable space; friendliness is the openness capable of the windowing that opens and connects, taking the place of Leibniz's God for the windowless monads.**
    - Where the shared horizon disintegrates into diverse identities, friendliness initiates a singular form of participation and gives the monads windows

## Hyperlogue

Han argues the World Wide Web transforms the sea from Hegel's symbol of horrifying uncertainty into a navigable hypermarket, so the user is a browsing consumer-tourist rather than an adventurous seafarer, and logos gives way to 'hyperlogue.'

* <span id="hyperculture-044"></span>**The World Wide Web turns the world into a seascape, but not the fearful, unfathomable ocean of Hegel—for whom philosophizing meant throwing oneself into an endless ocean 'seized by horror' guided only by the mind's inner North Star—since surfing is the very opposite of an adventurous maritime journey into uncertainty.**
    - "it throws itself as if into an endless ocean, all the bright colours, all footholds have disappeared...mind, being alone with itself, is seized by horror" —Hegel
    - The old Netscape Navigator showed a starlit sea and a lighthouse; 'logging on' could be called 'embarking'
* <span id="hyperculture-045"></span>**On the Web the user is a consumer and tourist who 'browses' the hypermarket of information rather than an adventurous seafarer, so the sea is no longer that of Homer or Hegel and the mind's inner star pales; logos gives way to 'hyperlogue,' which leaves behind the old order of logos entirely—resonating less with logos than with 'log-in,' 'logo,' or 'logo-s.'**
    - 'Browsing' is not a maritime image like 'surfing'; the user has the attitude of the consumer, not the seafarer
    - The hyperlogue is the new order of hyperculture and is not a continuation of dialogue or polylogue, both of which remain attached to the old logos

## The Wanderer

Han reads Nietzsche's 'wanderer' as a free spirit in a de-teleologized world whose loss of a single horizon opens a new 'hypervision' of surfaces, but argues the wanderer's painful, desert-crossing gait still lacks the serene leisureliness of the hypercultural tourist.

* <span id="hyperculture-046"></span>**Nietzsche's 'wanderer' is a free spirit who cannot feel other than a wanderer on earth—but not a traveller to a final destination, for this destination does not exist—so he lets his heart adhere to nothing and, freed from ultimate meaning (homo liber), learns for the first time to look around and see 'what is really going on in the world.'**
    - "He who has attained to only some degree of freedom of mind cannot feel other than a wanderer on the earth - though not as a traveller to a final destination: for this destination does not exist" —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - The absence of a telos liberates vision into a 'hypervision' of vast surfaces and multicoloured appearances, distrusting the myth of depth or origin; etymologically 'Sinn' (meaning) suggests a walk or journey
* <span id="hyperculture-047"></span>**The wanderer's form of existence does not resemble the hypercultural tourist's: his walking lacks the tourist's leisureliness, and his world is still peppered with deserts, abysses and 'bad nights,' so despite his faithfulness to the earth Nietzsche remained a pilgrim on a via dolorosa made only more painful by the loss of God.**
    - "dreadful night may sink down upon the desert like a second desert, and his heart grow weary of wandering" —Friedrich Nietzsche

## Threshold

In the closing chapter Han reads Heidegger's collection of 'things,' his mute rural counter-world, and his petrified 'threshold' of pain as a re-facticizing resistance to hyperculture, and asks whether the future human should be the serene homo liber tourist or remain, with Heidegger and Handke, a homo dolores frozen into a threshold.

* <span id="hyperculture-048"></span>**For Heidegger hyperculture would be the end of culture; he laments the loss of homeland and blames the media (radio, television, movies) for turning humans into homeless tourists by simulating a world that is in truth 'no world,' de-facticizing the primordial being-in-the-world that precedes ideas and images.**
    - "those who have stayed on in their homeland...are still more homeless...chained to radio and television" —Heidegger
    - In 'Why Do I Stay in the Provinces?' the world only is 'when one's own Dasein stands in its work'—for the film-watcher or tourist who only beholds, the world does not exist
* <span id="hyperculture-049"></span>**Heidegger's collection of 'things' in 'The Thing' (jug, plow, tree, heron, roe deer, mirror and clasp, book, crown and cross) reveals a strictly ordered, mostly monosyllabic, alliterative counter-world of stern simplicity that is really a romantic projection onto the peasants—making Heidegger himself a pilgrim-tourist on his way to an imaginary There.**
    - He includes only native harmless animals, no insects or vermin (Ungeziefer, literally animals unfit for sacrifice), avoids composite words that would 'hyphenate' the world, and admits the two-syllable heron only for its assonance
    - 'Crown' and 'cross' point to hierarchical and religious order, and 'book' represents a closed, stable, repeatable nomos—Heidegger's world is a world of the book, hostile to plurality
* <span id="hyperculture-050"></span>**Heidegger's things have mirrors but no windows—the mirror and clasp are figures of inwardness and return to oneself, counter-figures to the hypertext's window—and his world is mute, with things reflecting silently rather than windowing or intertwingling, so 'windowing' would be wholly alien to Heidegger.**
    - A mirror reflects only what is one's own and is a counter-figure to the window; the closed circularity of the clasp is the figure of the return to oneself
* <span id="hyperculture-051"></span>**Heidegger's 'threshold' makes crossing to the alien a dramatic act: it is the load-bearing ground-beam guarding the inside of the house, hardened because 'pain has petrified it,' so his openness is limited to a hesitating threshold turned inward—the hypertextual absence of thresholds is alien to him.**
    - "The threshold, as the settlement of the between, is hard because pain has petrified it...The pain presences unflagging in the threshold, as pain." —Heidegger
* <span id="hyperculture-052"></span>**Han closes by posing the book's decision: the human of the future will most likely no longer cross thresholds with a face contorted in pain but will be a tourist smiling serenely—should we welcome this homo liber, or, following Heidegger and Handke, remain a homo dolores petrified into a threshold?**
    - "When you feel the pain of the thresholds, then you are not a tourist; the crossing is possible." —Peter Handke
