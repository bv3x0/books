# Market Wizards: The Next Generation: The World’s Top Young Traders Reveal How They Beat the Market

## Metadata
- Thesis: Extraordinary trading performance comes less from one superior method than from a rare combination of edge, temperament, disciplined risk control, adaptation, and obsessive work. The interviews show that great traders survive by cutting losses, matching strategy to personality and market regime, and changing when capacity, volatility, or life priorities change.
- Topics: risk management, trading psychology, market inefficiency, position sizing, strategy adaptation
- Categories: business & economics, biography & autobiography, psychology
- Author: Jack D. Schwager and George F. Coyle
- Year: 2025
- Mode: quick

## Kristjan Kullamägi

Kullamägi’s path from repeated account blow-ups to a nine-figure equity peak shows that simple, well-researched setups can produce extreme returns when paired with tight risk limits and favorable market regimes. His worst losses came not from ignorance of his rules but from overconfidence after success and failure to obey them.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--001"></span>**Kullamägi turned early failure into a training loop by using his Stockholm security-guard job to fund new accounts and study charts until his blow-ups became slower and more informative.**
    - A summer security shift at the Nasdaq Stockholm building made him curious about markets, and he dropped out of biomedical laboratory science with only months left because trading had become his focus.
    - He began with about $5,000, lost 90% in two months, then repeated the cycle of working, saving, and blowing up two more times before becoming profitable.
    - Each failed attempt exposed a different defect: all-in sizing, weak stops, high commissions, and insufficient selectivity; the learning was visible in the increasing time it took to blow up.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--002"></span>**His core edge settled into three recurring stock patterns: episodic pivots, momentum breakouts, and parabolic shorts, all selected from volatile names with enough news, volume, or overextension to move far.**
    - Episodic pivots require major news, a sharp gap higher, and explosive volume that forces the market to reassess a neglected stock.
    - Momentum bursts buy leading stocks after short consolidations, with the expectation that most of the move will occur in three to five days.
    - LAES in the December 2024 quantum-computing rally produced both an episodic-pivot opportunity and, after a five-day surge from about $2 to $11, a parabolic-short opportunity.
    - Kullamägi credits Pradeep Bonde of Stockbee for the episodic-pivot and momentum-burst frameworks rather than claiming originality.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--003"></span>**Risk control transformed Kullamägi’s volatile setups from account-killers into scalable trades because every entry had a nearby invalidation point and a position size tied to account risk.**
    - For long entries he used the day’s low as the stop, for shorts the day’s high, and skipped trades when that stop was farther away than the average daily true range.
    - He typically risks 0.5% or less of account equity on a trade and caps risk near 1% on larger opportunities.
    - His win rate is only about 25% to 30%, but the method works because average winners are much larger than average losers.
    - Partial profits and moving-average trailing stops let him keep a remainder position for multi-week moves rather than harvesting every first burst of strength.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--004"></span>**Kullamägi’s 2022 collapse was caused by violating his own market-regime filter after a euphoric 2020-2021 run detached him from his process.**
    - His account grew from about $3.5 million at the start of 2020 to $36 million by year-end, later peaking near $105 million in November 2021.
    - From the $105 million peak to mid-2022, he lost roughly $60 million, about half from retracing open profits and half from subsequent trading losses.
    - His 10-day and 20-day moving-average rule would have kept him largely out of long breakout and episodic-pivot trades during the downtrend, but he kept trading them anyway.
    - The drawdown came from many small and mid-sized losses rather than one catastrophic trade, showing that rule violations can compound even without a single blow-up.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--005"></span>**The chapter’s broader lesson is that a trader need not invent a method, but must verify it personally, match it to temperament, and keep doing the focused work even after wealth creates distractions.**
    - After buying a yacht and spending more time traveling, Kullamägi missed high-quality 2024 opportunities such as SMCI because his attention and chart-review routines had weakened.
    - He kept Evernote screenshots of the best episodic pivots, breakouts, and parabolic shorts, reviewing them to refresh conviction about what an A+ setup looked like.
    - His phrase-like arc from living to trade to trading to live captures the tradeoff between maximizing returns and building a life after financial success.
    - Schwager stresses that Kullamägi’s success depended on defined entry, exit, regime, and risk rules, while Coyle emphasizes the power of simple methods executed with discipline.

## Lance Breitstein

Breitstein’s career shows how obsessive process improvement, mentorship, and fast pattern recognition can turn day trading into a high-edge craft, while also revealing the health and life costs of screen-bound success. His later shift to fewer, larger, longer trades illustrates the difference between the highest-probability game and the most scalable game.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--006"></span>**Breitstein chose Trillium’s low-paid proprietary trading seat over a conventional Wall Street offer because he judged the long-term expected value of learning from proven day traders to be higher than immediate salary.**
    - He turned down a Bank of Montreal sales-and-trading offer above $100,000 for a Trillium salary of about $26,000.
    - He researched Trillium through current traders, looking for empirical evidence that young discretionary day traders there could make seven figures consistently.
    - He moved to Princeton rather than New York City so he could sit near the firm’s top trader, trading nightlife and comfort for direct mentorship.
    - His childhood memory of family financial stress during the Great Recession supplied the urgency behind the decision.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--007"></span>**His day-trading edge came from combining fast execution with nuanced interpretation of news, charts, volume, and context rather than treating technical patterns as generic signals.**
    - Simple breakouts, exhaustion gaps, bouncy-ball patterns, and headline trades gained power only when aligned with multiple details such as news catalysts, volume, volatility, market sentiment, and multi-timeframe structure.
    - The November 2024 Bitcoin breakout after the pro-crypto U.S. presidential election combined all-time highs, a political catalyst, rising lows, and reflexive buying interest.
    - His breaking-news trades included shorting SMCI after its auditor resigned and buying Oracle after President Trump announced the Stargate AI project.
    - Custom hotkeys let him send limit orders through the offer by preplanned percentages and sizes, reducing the execution gap between perception and action.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--008"></span>**Breitstein turned early underperformance into competence by treating trading like athletic film study, using screen recordings and Sunday reviews to compress experience.**
    - He lost money for 11 consecutive months and remained near breakeven after roughly 18 months, even while working harder than peers.
    - He recorded key live-trading moments, replayed them at slower speeds to study decision points, and practiced them at faster speeds so real-time action felt easier.
    - A 2013 Tesla exhaustion-gap short, where he made $10,000 while his mentor made far more on the same trade, gave him proof that sizing and skill could close the gap.
    - A 2014 Allergan after-hours short created a roughly $100,000 fat-tail loss because the office lacked access to the news source that moved the stock.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--009"></span>**Breitstein’s best risk lessons center on sizing: tight stops usually define the trade, but oversized exposure can turn a valid setup into a career-worst loss.**
    - For intraday trades he often used two-minute-bar stops; for multi-day trades he often used prior-day lows or highs, with smaller size when stops were absent.
    - His November 2021 Avis short fit his euphoria-reversal playbook, but he sized it too large because he was trying to reach a $20 million annual profit goal.
    - The Avis trade lost over $2 million in less than an hour, while the stock later ran to about $550 after he stopped out.
    - He frames performance targets as dangerous when they pressure a trader to impose personal needs on a market that only offers what it offers.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--010"></span>**After leaving Trillium, Breitstein made more money with far fewer trades by applying short-term entry skill to scalable multi-day opportunities and by using options around panic levels.**
    - He generated about $46 million at Trillium, then made about $71 million in five years trading his personal account on net first-year deposits of $5 million.
    - The August 5, 2024 Nikkei futures panic became his largest trade because he judged yen-driven forced selling as far less fundamentally damaging than the price move implied.
    - His option-selling rule is to sell puts only at prices where he would want to own the stock, usually after a panic has already inflated volatility.
    - He still believes the shortest time frames have the highest probability edges, but their capacity constraints make longer holding periods more attractive for large capital.
    - His later focus on nonprofit work, a trading course, health, sleep, and longevity reframes trading as only one chapter rather than the final life objective.

## Simon Russo

Russo’s chapter is a study in extreme talent paired with recurring risk-control failure: his edge in shorting and later trading parabolic moves produced enormous wealth, but his largest losses repeatedly came from entering trades outside his criteria and refusing to stop out. His evolution toward hard risk systems, reduced trading, and philanthropy turns the chapter into a warning that even a wizard cannot rely on willpower alone.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--011"></span>**Russo’s music background explains his trading success more than formal finance training because both pursuits rewarded obsession, independent learning, and intolerance for superficial mastery.**
    - He entered USC Thornton School of Music for composition and arranging, but trading displaced music because it offered a similarly difficult path of self-discovery with financial upside.
    - His formative music teacher modeled mastery, humility, curiosity, and craft devotion, traits Russo later carried into SEC-filings research and trading case studies.
    - Russo’s strengths were calm under stress, long-duration focus, skepticism, and love of exhaustive research, all well suited to his contrarian trading style.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--012"></span>**His early blow-ups all followed the same causal chain: a valid money-making intuition was paired with oversized short positions, no enforceable stop, and stubborn hope after the trade moved against him.**
    - He first lost his $3,000 life savings, later made about $300,000 in his father’s account in 2013, then repeatedly destroyed accounts funded with $50,000, $35,000, or profits from prior runs.
    - In 2015 he grew $50,000 to about $550,000 in four months, then lost it on a biotech short that began as a $2,000 scalp idea and ended in margin liquidation.
    - AQXP in August 2015 pushed his account negative before an accidental remaining buying-power anomaly let him trade back near zero.
    - The deeper error was selection drift: the blow-up trades did not meet his criteria for shorting weak companies rallying on meaningless catalysts with bearish financing structures.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--013"></span>**Russo’s mature small-cap short method used fundamental microstructure to identify rallies likely to become selling opportunities rather than simply shorting whatever was up the most.**
    - He looked for superficially bullish but insignificant news, poor cash positions, high burn rates, convertible debt, warrants, and identifiable holders likely to sell into strength.
    - SEC filings became the core evidence base: cash, capital structure, convertible instruments, and deal participants revealed hidden supply and dilution risk.
    - The premarket routine from roughly 7 a.m. to 9 a.m. was devoted to rapidly filtering candidates so he would know before the open which names were worth trading.
    - If he could not explain why a stock was moving, he treated that ignorance as a reason to avoid the trade rather than assume the move was fake.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--014"></span>**The 2016 MGT trade marked Russo’s transition from talented but fragile to professional because he combined selection, stops, reentry, and pyramiding into one coherent process.**
    - He lost on the first MGT short attempt, returned the next day when the parabolic move showed weakness, and added as the stock broke down while moving stops lower.
    - The MGT trade made about $30,000, roughly matching his entire year-to-date profit at that point and validating his process.
    - His later pyramiding method added size only after price action allowed the stop to be lowered, increasing profit potential while keeping original dollar risk roughly constant.
    - Broken shorts turned some would-be short candidates into longs when trapped shorts, tight consolidations, and new highs signaled that upside capitulation had not yet occurred.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--015"></span>**Russo’s later career proves that massive success does not eliminate old vulnerabilities: his worst trades came from overconfidence, off-method entries, and mental stops that willpower could not enforce.**
    - From a $40,000 post-loan stake in late 2015, Russo generated over $500 million in cumulative profits over roughly a decade.
    - A short SPY mean-reversion trade near the end of 2023 lost about $50 million because it was outside his normal strategy and heavily leveraged.
    - A Carvana short also lost about $50 million after he reentered without his usual conditions and then exited around $68 before the stock later rose far higher.
    - In the postscript, a sub-A+ short snowballed into a peak unrealized drawdown of about $120 million after he re-shorted, doubled down, and repeatedly added without a plan.
    - The resulting fail-safe system tiers every trade by risk, automatically liquidates portions at defined loss multiples, alerts on failed orders, and triggers a panic override after extreme gaps.

## Lukas Fröhlich

Fröhlich’s chapter explains how one trader successfully migrated from hyper-short-term small-cap shorting to euphoric bull-market longs and then to contrarian long-term investing by matching strategy to market regime and account size. His extreme returns are presented not as magic but as the compounded result of volatility, research, pyramiding, risk limits, and willingness to abandon a strategy when the environment changes.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--016"></span>**Fröhlich’s early trading was driven by a desire for freedom from school and authority, but his first lessons came through brutal leverage mistakes rather than insight.**
    - He began trading at age 13 with about $500 saved from gifts, using his father’s account because he was too young to trade legally.
    - He traded currencies and leveraged CFDs, at one point losing 200% of an account overnight by shorting the DAX with 100-to-1 leverage.
    - To cover a negative account balance he sold his bicycle and borrowed from a friend, an episode he later saw as evidence of his devotion to trading.
    - Repeated small blow-ups pushed him toward explicit limits on per-stock losses and daily portfolio losses, which stopped the cycle of wiping out.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--017"></span>**His first durable edge came from combining small-cap short technical patterns with fundamental research on companies that needed financing and were vulnerable to dilution.**
    - He looked for companies with looming working-capital needs, unfavorable financing terms, warrants, convertibles, and press releases that could facilitate underwriters or holders selling into price spikes.
    - Tim Grittani’s emphasis on small-cap shorting and quantifying patterns influenced Fröhlich’s research process; Miah Lee became a research collaborator.
    - SONN on April 14, 2020 fit his textbook pattern: huge gap, weak financials, convertibles and warrants, likely selling pressure, and an intraday collapse he traded around.
    - He used small initial entries, mental stops, a maximum of three failed short attempts in a day, and pyramiding only after weakness appeared.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--018"></span>**The 2020-2021 bull market made Fröhlich’s mid-cap breakout strategy extraordinarily profitable because liquidity, stimulus, and social-media themes rewarded long exposure to speculative stories.**
    - He focused on $1 billion to $5 billion market-cap stocks with small floats, catalysts, 40% to 100% validation moves, high volume, and consolidations in the upper half of the range.
    - GRWG in late 2020 and early 2021 combined cannabis-sector attention, strong earnings catalysts, moving-average convergence, pyramiding entries, and a final exit on signs of hysteria.
    - Pyramid additions had to qualify as independent setups; a failed add-on made the whole trade suspect and could trigger a full exit.
    - The strategy works in the euphoric late stage of a bull market but became dangerous once the bull market stalled in mid-2021.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--019"></span>**Fröhlich’s current investment style blends contrarian value with trend-following confirmation, seeking hated sectors where a catalyst can reverse bearish consensus and begin a durable trend.**
    - He looks for strong companies with discounted valuations, high short interest, growth themes, and an expected surprise catalyst the market is not pricing in.
    - BABA in 2024 offered low valuation, earnings growth, cloud potential, buybacks, and the prospect of Chinese government stimulus after severe macro pessimism.
    - CNC in summer 2025 represented a healthcare panic where he expected political actors to preserve ACA subsidies and reverse Medicare cuts rather than allow the catastrophic scenario the market priced.
    - The 200-day moving average is a key implementation filter: he may probe below it, but major size usually waits for a reclaim or successful retest.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--020"></span>**The chapter’s main risk lesson is that strategy migration must follow both market regime and account scale, while optimism must be balanced by defense against bear-market duration.**
    - An independent analysis found his 2020 Modified Dietz return was about 1,132,045%, and his account balance grew 37,127% after withdrawals.
    - From January 2020 through December 2025, his account compounded at about 309.3% annually with a maximum drawdown of 35.5%.
    - In 2022 he suffered a roughly 40% drawdown after buying too early, sizing too large, and adding to losing positions in a bear market.
    - He now treats volatility as opportunity for trading but distinguishes it from investing, where long-term business performance matters more than short-term chaos.
    - The Kelly criterion discussion frames his 5% to 10% risk on high-conviction themes as aggressive but not necessarily excessive if edge and payoff are high, while warning against overestimating probabilities.

## Phil Goedeker

Goedeker’s career turns two supposedly dangerous strategies, short selling and selling option premium, into a long-term compounding machine through reflexive loss-cutting, setup selectivity, and conservative wealth segregation. The chapter’s causal lesson is that negative-asymmetry trades can survive only when the trader treats small losses as routine and catastrophic losses as unacceptable.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--021"></span>**Goedeker’s impatience made trend-following longs a poor fit, but it also helped him discover a short-selling style where good trades worked almost immediately.**
    - After seeing Lake of the Ozarks wealth as a teenager, he began reading business pages and market books because he wanted a faster path to financial independence.
    - He lost two separate $3,000 college accounts buying short-term uptrends before saving $5,000 for a third attempt.
    - His failure on longs came from demanding immediate payoff without an entry edge; that same intolerance for delay later became useful in exiting bad shorts quickly.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--022"></span>**His first short trade in Host America revealed the power of overextended small-cap reversals and launched a high-risk but tightly managed phase of compounding.**
    - Host America rallied from about $3 to $15 or $16 on a misleading Walmart-related press release, was halted, and reopened near $5 after corrected claims.
    - Goedeker turned $5,000 into more than $1 million in about nine months during his senior year of college.
    - He screened nightly for the biggest one-day, three-day, and five-day winners, then shorted only after the move stalled or broke down.
    - The early compounding used 200% short exposure on nearly every trade, a level of aggression that also led to a later 50% account drawdown.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--023"></span>**The critical performance upgrade came when Goedeker analyzed his own trades and discovered that only a tiny subset deserved large size.**
    - Reviewing about 500 tickers from 2010, he found only about 15 generated major gains while many other tickers produced noise or avoidable losses.
    - He began sizing A+ shorts of trashy small caps after parabolic rallies much larger while cutting B and C trades by roughly 90%.
    - He still took smaller suboptimal trades for activity and experimentation, but made them too small to meaningfully damage P&L.
    - Schwager links this sizing distinction to Michael Marcus and Ed Thorp: varying bet size can matter as much as entry method.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--024"></span>**Goedeker’s option-selling strategy is dangerous in theory but survivable in his hands because he sells premium where gap risk is lower and hedges immediately when conditions change.**
    - He began option selling in 2016, started with $200,000, soon increased to $1 million and then $2 million, and made about $1 million in his first option-trading year.
    - He primarily sells deep out-of-the-money puts on stable mega-cap stocks such as Apple, Amazon, and Microsoft, and is far more cautious selling calls.
    - A $1 million GameStop call-selling loss taught him not to sell options on stocks capable of extreme squeeze moves.
    - After President Trump’s April 2025 tariff announcement, he bought hundreds of June SPY puts on the next opening to neutralize the whole option portfolio while down only about $100,000.
    - He keeps only about 10% of total assets in the trading account, so even a 1987-style crash would threaten the account but not his overall wealth.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--025"></span>**Goedeker’s consistency comes from treating capital preservation as the main goal and accepting that markets, not annual targets, determine opportunity.**
    - He grew a $5,000 start to about $53 million and had only one down year in 20 years, a roughly $30,000 loss.
    - His paper-cut-loss mindset allows thousands of small exits while avoiding the one guillotine loss that can end a career.
    - He sets deliberately low annual financial goals because ambitious targets can induce overtrading when the market does not provide A+ setups.
    - The 2020 stimulus-driven bull market created frequent small-cap gaps of 1,000% or more, showing why short sellers can need bull-market euphoria rather than bear-market weakness.

## Kelvin Chiu

Chiu’s chapter traces a trader’s evolution from academically conditioned perfectionism to process-driven risk-taking across commodity spreads and broader markets. His distinctive lesson is that both positive and negative asymmetric trades can be profitable, but only when structure, timing, conviction, and emotional self-management are consciously aligned.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--026"></span>**Chiu’s elite academic record initially hurt his trading because it created expectations of being right, while trading demanded resilience in a field where being wrong often is normal.**
    - He grew up in Hong Kong, ranked among the city’s top students, and then graduated from Cambridge with perfect grades before joining Goldman Sachs.
    - In 2010 and 2011, his Goldman trading book lost just under $1 million each year while he searched for a method.
    - Negative self-talk and low resilience followed from a lifetime of academic success, making small losses feel like personal failure rather than information.
    - The loss of his book and reassignment under a senior sugar trader removed pressure and created the conditions for curiosity, mentorship, and process learning.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--027"></span>**His Goldman and Vitol years taught him to see agricultural markets through supply-demand history and to prefer spreads where fundamentals constrained the downside.**
    - Calendar and relative-value spreads let him trade how one futures contract should move versus another rather than taking pure directional exposure.
    - In 2016, a huge hard red winter wheat crop pushed Kansas City wheat to such a discount versus Chicago wheat that rail delivery arbitrage created a boundary-condition long Kansas City, short Chicago trade.
    - At Vitol he learned what aggressive commodity risk-taking could look like, with traders experiencing multi-million-dollar daily swings.
    - His best year at Vitol produced about $35 million in profits, though he notes some energy traders there made far more.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--028"></span>**A $10 million soybean meal loss crystallized Chiu’s distinction between convex trades with limited downside and concave trades where the fat tail is adverse.**
    - In 2016 he shorted old-crop July soybean meal versus long December because the meal spread had widened in sympathy with soybeans without matching meal fundamentals.
    - He entered near 14, watched the spread rise to 45, exited around an average of 38, and lost about $10 million before the spread later returned to 2.
    - The trade was concave because the potential gain was limited but the adverse price move could become much larger than expected before fair value reasserted itself.
    - His boss’s light-touch response helped him recover, and Chiu later traded back to positive P&L for the year.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--029"></span>**As an independent trader, Chiu first maximized smooth return-risk through convex spread trades, then deliberately reintroduced concave trades when he realized he was sacrificing too much total profit.**
    - He left Vitol in 2018 to trade independently before reaching his original $10 million capital target, supplemented by family investment of about $1 million.
    - Convex agricultural bull spreads bought nearby futures and sold forward futures near full carry, creating limited downside and large upside if nearby scarcity emerged.
    - He skipped the 2019 July/December corn spread short even though fundamentals and margin of safety were favorable because his rules prohibited concave trades.
    - The missed trade forced a choice between maximizing return-risk statistics and maximizing P&L while keeping blow-up probability near zero.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--030"></span>**Chiu’s mature approach to concave trades relies on timing, expiration mechanics, conviction stops, and emotional reframing rather than fixed price stops alone.**
    - After Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, he waited for Chicago wheat to show weakness before shorting May and buying July as export flows continued and U.S. wheat priced itself out of demand.
    - He eventually reached about 11,000 short May/long July spreads and viewed a renewed move to 150 as roughly a 25% account-risk scenario, though he now tries to cap high-conviction trade loss near 15%.
    - A conviction stop means reducing or exiting when new information weakens the thesis, especially as the spread’s path to expiration fair value changes.
    - Cognitive behavioral therapy helped him separate self-worth from P&L by grounding identity in growth, resilience, and courage.
    - He now treats sizing as a central edge: long inactivity punctuated by aggressive risk when fundamentals, technicals, and intuition align.

## Jason Berry

Berry’s near-frictionless equity curve comes from ultra-short-term trading that pairs reflexive loss-cutting with many small, researched edges across markets, times of day, and spreads. His chapter argues that career-length trading requires continuous innovation because every short-term edge eventually erodes.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--031"></span>**Berry’s instinctive risk control appeared before he had a formal method, allowing him to survive and outperform peers in his first trading roles.**
    - After a brief and unpleasant stint as a LIFFE floor clerk, he moved into electronic trading in Dublin and followed a breakout system with discretion over signals and exits.
    - He attributes early comfort with screen focus, hand-eye coordination, and losing without emotional attachment partly to video games.
    - When the risk manager noticed Berry was one of the few traders with positive results, the difference was not a secret system but his willingness to cut losers and avoid hope.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--032"></span>**Berry’s trading method depends on recognizing very short-term momentum and relative-value opportunities at specific times when a market normally trends rather than chops.**
    - He trades 1-, 2-, and 5-minute bars, but only during market-specific windows where years of observation show higher trend potential.
    - He calls his best entries fantastic fill feeling trades: partial fills or immediate movement in his favor signal that supply-demand pressure is real.
    - Obsolete edges included news-driven intermarket lags, Nokia versus FESX relationships, and S&P minis versus SPY counterparty information, all eroded by speed and market structure change.
    - Spread trading is central because relative mispricings between related markets can offer better risk control than pure directional bets.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--033"></span>**Preparation before news lets Berry act faster and more rationally than traders who begin analysis during volatility.**
    - He writes scenario plans for possible events such as tariffs, China attacking Taiwan, or the Russia-Ukraine war ending, then maps likely relative impacts across bonds, equities, currencies, and spreads.
    - If news should be more bullish for the S&P than NASDAQ but NASDAQ initially jumps more because of volatility, he may buy S&P and sell NASDAQ.
    - He often trades after algos move first because the larger move may still be under way, or he fades initial reactions when the news is less meaningful than the price response.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--034"></span>**Daily and monthly review make Berry’s discretion auditable, turning psychology, sizing, exits, and missed opportunities into a continuous improvement process.**
    - His daily summary records what he saw, traded, missed, how he sized, how long he held, how he handled exits, and what his psychology was like.
    - Month-end review distinguishes poor trading from low-opportunity markets and points to changes such as cutting faster, taking more or less risk, or dropping inefficient strategies.
    - He also annotates charts after market-moving news to build his own historical record of how markets respond to events.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--035"></span>**Berry’s work as trainer and firm builder reflects his belief that edge is learned through time, simulation, risk control, and constant replacement of decaying strategies.**
    - He estimates he has trained well over 200 traders, with current trainee success rates averaging above 50% after improving hiring standards.
    - His three Cs framework moves trainees from control of P&L, to consistency, to confidence, where they can trade larger, hold longer, add to winners, and expand products.
    - The training program typically takes two to three years, with simulation lasting about nine to 18 months before live trading is earned and continually re-earned.
    - He argues traders do not age out; they effort out, because maintaining edge requires constant screen time, research, and renewal.
    - Positive Equity teaches 20 to 30 structural strategy frameworks while insisting that most of a trader’s edge must come from independent discovery and refinement.

## Kenny Sharkness

Sharkness succeeds with an unusually broad and changing portfolio of strategies because he treats trading as an adaptive game of expected value, journaling, collaboration, and selective sizing. His chapter shows that frequent activity can coexist with discipline only when the largest size is reserved for the few trades that truly matter.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--036"></span>**Sharkness entered trading through games, finance, and SMB’s training environment, then learned that his natural impatience required methods where bad trades failed quickly.**
    - After Ithaca College, he moved to Hoboken, paid for SMB’s training program, and lived frugally with support from his parents while learning to trade.
    - Gilberto Mendez taught him risk management, classic chart patterns, multiple timeframes, and the idea that stocks expend and regain energy.
    - He became profitable after about 10 months; his first profitable year made about $40,000, enough to pay bills but not yet a strong income.
    - His early stops were extremely tight because he wanted trades to work immediately; if they did not, he exited and tried again later.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--037"></span>**The merger of SMB and Kershner changed Sharkness’s beliefs about what was possible and expanded his strategy set from basic momentum to a continually evolving playbook.**
    - Seeing Kershner traders make $25,000 to $50,000 on some days reset his scale expectations beyond the $2,000 daily profits he had admired at SMB.
    - He absorbed strategies involving order-flow anomalies, end-of-day imbalances, index rebalances, hot IPO opens, early no-news spikes, and catalyst-driven news reactions.
    - He kept learning from stronger traders, social media, and firm execution data, then adapted strategies rather than copying them wholesale.
    - Many gimmick and rebalance trades lost edge as more traders and algorithms discovered them, reinforcing that strategies must be updated or discarded.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--038"></span>**Journaling, playbooks, and coaching turned Sharkness’s personality weaknesses into process goals rather than recurring excuses.**
    - He maintains a playbook of the best trade each day and an anti-playbook of the worst trade, both recorded while details are fresh.
    - Weekly and monthly reviews turn recurring mistakes, such as overeagerness or exiting winners too fast, into specific process goals.
    - Dr. Brett Steenbarger helped him work on impatience, increase size on great setups, and focus on strengths as well as weaknesses.
    - He treats motivational goals differently from annual P&L targets, because the latter can pressure a trader to force trades when opportunity is absent.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--039"></span>**Although Sharkness monitors many strategies, most profits come from rare high-conviction trades sized far larger than the surrounding activity.**
    - He estimates that roughly 80% or more of his profits come from trades where he decides to size up.
    - Smaller trades keep him engaged and prepared, but one materially sized idea usually dominates the risk and profit potential at a given time.
    - His 2022 drawdown came after the strong 2020-2021 period because he kept trying to win at prior levels in an environment with poor follow-through.
    - When consistency broke, he reduced size dramatically until a good trade restored flow and confidence.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--040"></span>**His newer stock-and-options tactics around parabolic moves show how he uses options to soften timing risk, define risk, and manage volatility exposure dynamically.**
    - In CRWV, he bought out-of-the-money calls while expecting a further squeeze, rolled them higher as the stock rose, and considered shorting the stock only if the move became extreme enough.
    - Buying calls when implied volatility is cheaper and selling puts after volatility spikes lets him express directional views while offsetting long-volatility risk.
    - SMCI was his best 2024 trade, beginning with long calls and later making most of the money on the short side after the parabolic rally broke.
    - He does not rely heavily on Greeks; he asks where the stock can go and whether options create a better risk-reward expression than stock alone.
    - The post-tariff 2025 bounce shows the same rubber-band logic can work long as well as short when velocity and sentiment become extreme.

## Rick Bandazian Jr.

Bandazian’s chapter shows that a human can still beat machines when the edge depends on nuanced event interpretation, prior deal research, and immediate risk control rather than raw speed alone. His astonishing consistency is inseparable from an exhausting, non-scalable routine built around vigilance, small losses, and rapid time stops.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--041"></span>**Bandazian’s edge emerged from event-driven Wall Street training, but it became profitable only when he discovered that fast reaction to specific headlines matched his risk-averse temperament.**
    - He moved from J.P. Morgan syndicated finance to equity capital markets, then to event-driven analysis at First New York and merger arbitrage at the National Bank of Canada.
    - After returning to First New York in 2008, two takeover-related headline trades produced in minutes what felt like months of salary and redirected his focus.
    - He stopped trying middling event-driven and momentum ideas and concentrated on trades where preparation plus speed created immediate expected value.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--042"></span>**The original simple-headline edge disappeared as bots improved, forcing Bandazian to shift toward nuanced merger and SEC-filing interpretation that algorithms and casual arbs still misread.**
    - He says the simple headline game eroded gradually between 2016 and 2018 and was effectively over by 2018.
    - At any time he tracks roughly 75 to 100 announced deals, updating files on catalysts, regulatory hurdles, domicile, deal structure, and expected fair value.
    - In WSR, he shorted a takeover-bid rally because Maryland incorporation and prior management behavior made the bid easier to ignore than uninformed buyers assumed.
    - In Twitter, he bought after Elon Musk’s 13D because he had already analyzed the bid scenario and estimated where the stock should trade relative to the offer.
    - In Foot Locker, he exploited a press-release detail about cash or stock election that made the deal trade differently from a generic $24 bid model.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--043"></span>**His risk management is built around tiny dollar pain, quick time decay of edge, and execution controls that prevent a bad fill from becoming a disaster.**
    - He reports no down month over 11 years, with average winner hold time around five minutes and losses generally shorter.
    - A unit for him is $2,500, and a one-unit loss is normally the hard P&L threshold that gets him out.
    - His edge decays like an hourglass: being first to understand a release may offer very high probability, but each passing minute lets others catch up and reduces the advantage.
    - He avoids market orders and uses hotkeys with bid-plus limits, such as buying only up to a defined amount above the current bid.
    - His worst loss was about $12,000 in Allergan, caused by buying a Carl Icahn headline that turned out to be old news and then facing premarket illiquidity.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--044"></span>**Bandazian’s consistency requires an extreme workday because his niche depends on being present for sporadic releases across premarket, market hours, and after-hours windows.**
    - He monitors markets from about 3:45 a.m. to 8 p.m. on weekdays, usually sleeping early so he can repeat the routine.
    - In recent years, about two-thirds of his trades have occurred outside regular stock market hours, when algos are less active and nuanced mispricings can persist longer.
    - He uses scanners and alerts for monitored companies, returns to the desk when needed, and normally trades only one to a few times per day.
    - Unlike many traders, he does not use other traders as idea sources because in his style any news passed through someone else is already stale.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--045"></span>**The chapter’s broader lesson is that a tiny, non-consensus niche can beat the market for a long time, but it may be hard to scale, hard to automate, and hard to sustain personally.**
    - Starting with $25,000 and continually withdrawing profits, he supported his family from trading while keeping size effectively independent of account balance.
    - His risk aversion limits his size even where liquidity exists, but the same trait is central to his record of avoiding meaningful drawdowns.
    - Coyle emphasizes that Bandazian sits in a niche too sophisticated for ordinary retail and too small or idiosyncratic for large quant shops to fully industrialize.
    - Bandazian worries that AI and algos may eventually invade even his nuanced corner, so he is developing a more sustainable semi-systematic macro strategy around offsides positioning.
    - His volunteer firefighting offers a contrasting form of risk: he is personally risk-averse in markets but willing to answer life-or-death community calls.

## Epilogue

The epilogue distills the book’s trader profiles into a warning: markets exploit ordinary human emotions, and only a small minority have both the temperament and commitment to turn passion into durable edge. Its final lesson is introspective rather than tactical: traders must know what they truly want from markets before confusing fantasy with vocation.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--046"></span>**The speculator-at-heaven anecdote frames the book’s core behavioral problem: even people who know a rumor is false can still be pulled by greed, fear, and FOMO.**
    - The speculator invents a rumor that tomorrow’s closing prices are available in hell, watches others rush out of heaven, and then follows them because the rumor might be true.
    - The joke illustrates how emotional reflex can override knowledge, making discipline and prebuilt methods essential rather than optional.
    - Russo’s Carvana loss is used as a real example: an emotion-based short broke his rules, but finally liquidating prevented a far larger, potentially ruinous loss.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--047"></span>**Market Wizardry is rare because it requires both innate emotional composition and an all-consuming commitment that most people neither possess nor should necessarily want.**
    - Talent and emotional makeup are compared to athletic endowment: effort matters, but not everyone has the required raw material.
    - The second barrier is sacrifice: the traders profiled spent years glued to screens, researching outside market hours, and shaping life around markets.
    - Wanting quick money is different from loving trading itself; the latter made long hours feel like craft rather than punishment for the traders in the book.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--048"></span>**Schwager uses his own life as a counterexample to show that not pursuing trading seriously can itself reveal what a person actually wants from markets.**
    - He never devoted even a week to full-time trading, yet took a year’s sabbatical to write A Complete Guide to the Futures Markets because writing was where his confidence and desire lay.
    - His impatience and lack of special trading talent made trading a poor fit, while writing about traders became his true market vocation.
    - Ed Seykota’s idea that people get what they want from markets is used to reframe apparent failure as revealed preference.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-the-worlds-top--049"></span>**The proper endpoint for readers is not imitation of a specific wizard but introspection about edge, temperament, desired lifestyle, and the kind of market participation that fits their lives.**
    - Some readers may be suited to pursue elite trading, others may prefer profitable hobby trading, indexing, or adjacent market work; the right goal depends on self-knowledge.
    - The book’s lessons are meant to improve the reader’s path, not promise that passion alone can overcome lack of edge or incompatible temperament.
    - Even Market Wizards make emotional mistakes, so the standard is not perfection but systems and discipline that prevent mistakes from becoming fatal.
