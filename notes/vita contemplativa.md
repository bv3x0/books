# Vita Contemplativa: A Praise of Inactivity

## Metadata
- Thesis: The contemporary crisis of meaning stems from the disintegration of being under the compulsion to act, produce, and communicate; only a recovery of contemplative inactivity—festivals, listening, wonder, and lingering—can restore being, community, and a reconciled relationship with nature.
- Topics: inactivity and contemplation, the crisis of being and meaning, critique of the performance society, festivals, ritual, and the Sabbath, reconciliation of humans and nature
- Categories: philosophy, social science
- Author: Byung-Chul Han
- Year: 2022
- Collections: Byung-Chul Han
- Mode: quick

## Absolute Lack of Being

The digital-informational age dissolves being into contingency and information, destroying the narrative, symbolic, and temporal structures that stabilize life; only contemplation, praise, and the festival can restore the fullness of being that action and survival have eclipsed.

* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-001"></span>**The root of the present crisis is the disintegration of everything that gives life meaning and orientation, expressed in Rilke's line 'For nowhere is there a staying'; life has become radically transient because digitalization and informatization fragment time into a mere additive succession of point-like presences without narrative structure.**
    - "Its cosmology is a cosmology not of being but of contingency." —Niklas Luhmann
    - Being grows slowly under conditions of lingering; today's short-termism dismantles being, and information—registered only fleetingly—represents the highest point of being's atrophy.
    - The human being is an animal narrans, a narrating animal, but in the absence of a binding narrative we are well informed yet without orientation.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-002"></span>**Symbols create shared things that enable a 'We,' since common feeling (sym-pathos, com-passion) arises only through the symbolic and aesthetic; the symbolic-narrative void therefore fragments society into mutually indifferent individuals and intensifies the lack of being.**
    - Plato's Symposium: Aristophanes says humans were originally round and were cut in half by the gods; each half (symbolon) seeks reunion, and this longing is love—the symbol promises a wholesome, healing totality.
    - "the symbol is that other fragment that has always been sought in order to complete and make whole our own fragmentary life." —Hans-Georg Gadamer
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-003"></span>**The neoliberal performance society and digitalization undermine being-with: economic competition isolates people into a battle for survival, while unlimited digital connectivity turns the Thou into an It, producing a fundamental loneliness whose congested libidinal energy refluxes into the self as anxiety.**
    - A real tie forms when we invest an object with libidinal energy; without proper ties this energy streams back into the self, which begins to circle around itself in a worldless movement—only Eros can defeat anxiety and depression.
    - Being connected is not being together; despite growing connectivity we are lonelier than ever.
* <span class="pull-quote">Being forms only under conditions of lingering. Information represents the highest point of being's atrophy.</span>
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-004"></span>**The lack of being drives an excess of production: the mania for health and optimization merely extends bare life into survival, and capitalism is nurtured by the illusion that more capital creates more life; by contrast the highest happiness lies in contemplation, in the wondering gaze that beholds what there is.**
    - "I maintain, Parmenon, that the happiest man is the one who, after happily viewing these impressive phenomena—the sun, the stars, the rain, the clouds and the lightning—soon returns to the place from which he came." —Menander
    - Anaxagoras answered that he was born 'for contemplation' (eis theorian); natality is founded on looking, not acting—being born means seeing the light of the world.
    - For Aquinas the active life serves the vita contemplativa, which is 'the end of the whole human life'; even politics ultimately aims at inactivity and the contemplation of truth.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-005"></span>**Praise is the ultimate purpose of language and the 'Sabbath of language': it has outgrown all wooing, striving, and care, whereas today all communication is advertising as a form of survival triggered at the zero point of being.**
    - "To praise, that's it! Called to praise, he came like ore out of the silence of stone." —Rainer Maria Rilke
    - Rilke distinguishes praising from wooing—'No more wooing! Voice, you've outgrown wooing'—for wooing belongs to the anxious, care-driven creature.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-006"></span>**Festive time is intensified attention and a we-feeling that founds community, deriving from theoria—the looking of the theoroi, envoys sent to divine festivals—so that contemplation is a divine, self-sufficient activity that, like the gods, does not act.**
    - The Greek theoria (to look) derives from theoroi, envoys who travelled to attend festivals of the gods; Aristotle compares the philosopher's theoria to the event at Olympia and the Dionysian festivals.
    - "Now if you take away from a living being action, and still more production, what is left but contemplation?" —Aristotle
    - "how instrumental were all the labor and history of man, to be crowned, if crowned at all, only in intuition." —George Santayana

## Views of Inactivity

Inactivity is not a deficiency or mere absence of activity but an intensity and capacity in itself—the source of culture, festivity, luxury, experience, truth, grace, and creativity—which the compulsion of work and performance has degraded into exploitable 'leisure time' and dead time.

* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-007"></span>**Because we view life exclusively through work and performance, inactivity has decayed into 'leisure time'—a derivative of work and dead time we kill to avoid boredom—rather than free, living time; a politics of inactivity is needed to reclaim genuinely free time that stands outside the order of production.**
    - Under capitalism inactivity returns only as an 'encapsulated outside' called leisure time, tied to the logic of work; what disappears is free time that does not belong to production.
    - When life follows stimulus-response, need-satisfaction, and goal-action it atrophies into naked biological life; inactivity is what makes any doing genuinely human.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-008"></span>**The origin of culture is the festival, not war; adornment, not the weapon; culture's core is ornamentation—'decorazione assoluta' that adorns nothing—and life receives its divine radiance from this absolute, purposeless decoration that insists life is more than survival.**
    - "It ceases to decorate anything and is, on the contrary, nothing but decoration." —Theodor W. Adorno
    - Debord: the present age presents itself as a series of festivities but knows nothing of real festivals, for a society lacking community and luxury cannot join in a luxurious expenditure of life.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-009"></span>**Luxury—understood as luxation, a deviation from the necessities of mere life—suspends economic compulsion and constitutes true happiness, which we owe to the useless, purposeless, and superfluous; 'ceremonious inactivity' means we do something but to no end, and this freedom from purpose is the basic formula for happiness.**
    - Adorno's train bleu: the voluptuousness of travel—the goodbye-waving, the ceremonial of mealtimes—passed away as rampant technology eliminated luxury.
    - Benjamin's flâneur is characterized by inactivity, irresolution, and doubt; like the butterfly's hesitant wing, the human gait owes its grace to moments of hesitation, and resolution or haste deprive it of grace.
    - Liberated from the 'in-order-to,' walking becomes dance and hands cease to grasp and instead play or form pure gestures.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-010"></span>**Ritual practices in which inactivity plays a major role—fasting, asceticism, contemplative eating—elevate us above mere life by suspending survival and enlivening the senses, but under the imperative of health even fasting becomes a form of survival that optimizes naked life.**
    - Plutarch's ritual for exorcizing ravenousness (bulimu exelasis), in Agamben's reading, expels beastly devouring to clear space for human, festive eating—a 'Sabbath of nourishment' (menuchah), not an activity directed toward an aim (melachah).
    - Benjamin, involuntarily fasting in Rome, felt he could unleash his senses into the folds of the most unassuming fruit and vegetables to identify a scent he had never known.
    - Even sleep is now treated as an activity ('power nap'), and dreams are optimized through 'lucid dreaming'—extending the compulsion of performance into sleep.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-011"></span>**Sleep, dreams, and boredom are privileged sites of truth and experience because they suspend wakeful separations and require passivity: truth is a relational process in which the sleeper's hands take hold of the key that acting hands never find, and boredom is 'the dream bird that hatches the egg of experience.'**
    - "For a long time I would go to bed early." —Marcel Proust
    - Proust: truth and life are attained only when, comparing a quality common to two sensations, we extract their common essence and reunite them within a metaphor—the mémoire involontaire is an epiphany at home in inactivity.
    - "Boredom is the threshold to great deeds." —Walter Benjamin
    - Experience presupposes listening and passivity; the noise of information and communication ends the 'community of listeners'—no one listens, everyone plays to the gallery.
* <span class="pull-quote">Life receives its radiance only from inactivity.</span>
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-012"></span>**Genuine waiting, ignorance, and worklessness (désœuvrement) are the ground of art and truth: Orpheus owes his song to Eurydice's withdrawal and to death as the utmost intensification of inactivity, and Nietzsche declares ignorance the 'nucleus of life' without which living things could not survive.**
    - "Waiting begins when there is nothing left to wait for, not even the end of waiting itself. Waiting waits for nothing." —Maurice Blanchot
    - Blanchot places désœuvrement close to death; only in being-towards-death does the literary space open—writing is always writing towards death.
    - "a great solid globe of ignorance must surround you." —Friedrich Nietzsche
    - Kleist's marionettes owe their grace to a not-doing-anything; the young man loses his gracefulness the moment he becomes aware of his posture in the mirror.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-013"></span>**Mastery, happiness, and the mimetic faculty require the abdication of the will and the surrender of the self: the master 'exercises the will away' so the happy hand acts by itself, and the genuinely inactive give up their names to become no one, dispossessed as subjects and surrendered to a subjectless taking-place.**
    - "To weary the master to the point of exhaustion... so that at long last his body and each of his limbs can act in accordance with their own rationality: this is what is called 'practice.'" —Walter Benjamin
    - Benjamin's parable 'Do Not Forget the Best': the busy man throws away his watch, practices being late, and joyous things come to him unbidden—'the best is not-doing.'
    - Barthes on a haiku ('Sitting peacefully doing nothing / Springtime is coming'): a grammatical anacoluthon dispossesses the subject; 'To be able, at certain moments, to no longer have to say I.'
    - Benjamin's Chinese painter disappears into his own picture; his magic smile signals the mimetic openness to make oneself similar to the other.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-014"></span>**Inactivity is the dialectical threshold to the genuinely new: boredom and silence create zones of indeterminacy in which something not-there-before can emerge, whereas the compulsion of activity and communication merely reproduces the same and perpetuates conformism as an efficient means of rule.**
    - "Repressive forces don't stop people expressing themselves but rather force them to express themselves. What a relief to have nothing to say, the right to say nothing." —Gilles Deleuze
    - Nietzsche: inventive people live differently from active ones; they need time for purposeless, unregulated activity—it is the proportion of inactivity in their activity that makes the altogether different emerge.
    - "Because time for thinking and quietness in thinking are lacking, one no longer ponders deviant views: one contents oneself with hating them." —Friedrich Nietzsche
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-015"></span>**Poetry suspends language as information and lets it rest within itself in contemplation, while capital is the pure form of activity that separates from life a bare life that works, degrading the human into an animal laborans; the politics of inactivity liberates the immanence of life so that it rests in itself as bliss.**
    - "poetry is precisely... the point at which language, which has deactivated its communicative and informative functions, rests within itself, contemplates its power of saying." —Giorgio Agamben
    - Marx: free competition sets free not individuals but capital; the individuals who imagine they are free are the sexual organs of capital, the means of its procreation.
    - "We will say of pure immanence that it is A LIFE, and nothing else... it is complete power, complete bliss." —Gilles Deleuze
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-016"></span>**Cézanne's painting embodies an ethos and utopia of inactivity: the ideal painter empties himself of will and intention to become no one, so that the landscape paints itself and things—apples not fit for consumption, jugs that are not tools—shine in a non-anthropomorphic order of reconciliation ('the marriage of everything to everything').**
    - "His entire will must be silent... He must forget, forget, be quiet, be a perfect echo. Then the full landscape will inscribe itself on his photographic plate." —Paul Cézanne
    - What destroys the continuity of being is human intention and judgement; Cézanne: 'Why do we divide the world? Is it a reflection of our egotism? We want everything to serve us.'
    - Handke's and Deleuze's small children radiate an immanent life of pure power and bliss, and Handke's 'ethereal tiredness' is spirit in the mode of contemplation, doing something for nothing.
    - 'The Card Players' and 'The Bathers' present a utopia of inactivity in which humans and nature fuse and the world appears in a state of redemption.

## A Marginal Note on Zhuangzi

Eastern and Heideggerian thought converge on an ethics of inactivity in which one acts by not willfully intervening but by drawing on the possibilities already dormant in things and nature—preserving the earth within the sphere of its own possibility rather than forcing the impossible upon it.

* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-017"></span>**Zhuangzi's master cook butchers oxen not by chopping with force but by cutting into the spaces between the joints, remaining inactive and merely present at a process that comes apart 'as if by itself'—a model of doing that works through the possibilities latent in things rather than willful intervention.**
    - "until—flop! the whole thing comes apart like a clod of earth crumbling to the ground." —Zhuangzi
    - Masanobu Fukuoka's 'do-nothing farming' applies this teaching: its first principle is NO CULTIVATION—no ploughing—because the earth cultivates itself and maintains its fertility naturally if left to itself.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-018"></span>**Heidegger's 'releasement' comes close to Zhuangzi: saving the earth means leaving it within the developed sphere of its possibilities rather than tearing it out of its 'inconspicuous law of the possible' and driving it, through technology and will, into the impossible.**
    - "It is first the will which arranges itself everywhere in technology that devours the earth in the exhaustion and consumption and change of what is artificial." —Martin Heidegger

## From Acting to Being

The Anthropocene results from making human action absolutely fundamental, so the required corrective is a philosophy of not-acting; Heidegger's 'turn' from the pathos of resolute action toward contemplative reflection, mood, releasement, and timidity supplies an ethics of inactivity that could save the earth.

* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-019"></span>**The Anthropocene marks the moment nature is completely absorbed into and exploited by human action, subjecting nature's lawfulness to the unpredictability of the human will; Arendt admits she has no solution and calls only for reflection, yet she never questions the superior status of action that is itself responsible for the catastrophe—what is needed is a philosophy of the capacity not to act.**
    - Arendt: acting into nature carries human unpredictability into a realm once thought ruled by inexorable law, producing a complete loss of control.
    - Benjamin's angel of history faces the piling wreckage of catastrophe but is driven forward by the storm called 'progress'; only an 'angel of inactivity' could arrest the apocalyptic continuity of the status quo.
    - "That things are 'status quo' is the catastrophe." —Walter Benjamin
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-020"></span>**Heidegger's 'inactive reflection' (untätige Besinnung) does not push forward like action but leads back to the primordial 'there' (Da-Sein) where we always already are—an over-close radical immanence accessible only through waiting, presentiment, and contemplative lingering, not propositional knowledge.**
    - "Waiting is a capacity that transcends all power to act. One who finds his way into the ability to wait surpasses all achieving and its accomplishments." —Martin Heidegger
    - 'Goosebumps (Gänsehaut) come closer to it than the retina (Netzhaut)'—the there is disclosed at the pre-reflexive level.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-021"></span>**Mood (Stimmung) is a primordial ontological passivity that precedes and de-termines all activity and thinking: it is not a subjective state but the world itself, disclosing being before any object-directed intentionality, so that even thinking is a pathos and correspondence—a listening and thanking in which the will abdicates.**
    - "If the basic mood is lacking, then everything is a forced clatter of concepts and of the mere shells of words." —Martin Heidegger
    - pathos is connected with paschein—to suffer, endure, be borne along, be determined by; because it cannot suffer or endure, artificial intelligence cannot think and knows only on and off, being transparent rather than woven of light and shadow.
    - Han plays on the German root 'stimmen': mood be-stimmt (attunes/determines) thinking; renunciation and 'liking' (Mögen) ground capacity (Vermögen) and the possible (möglich).
* <span class="pull-quote">In this way, what is not done is sometimes more powerful than what is said and done.</span>
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-022"></span>**Heidegger's ethics of inactivity reinterprets 'saving' (retten), 'sparing/preserving' (schonen, derived from schön, beautiful), and 'dwelling' as letting things be in their own presencing; the earth is not a resource to preserve but a beautiful thing whose language we must learn to listen to, making inactivity politically significant.**
    - "Real sparing... takes place when we leave something beforehand in its own essence... The fundamental character of dwelling is this sparing and preserving." —Martin Heidegger
    - Heidegger's Black Notebooks: 'Beyng is the aether in which mankind breathes'; forgetfulness of being reduces the human to an animal laborans and human activity to breeding like cattle—a biopolitics based on the history of being.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-023"></span>**Heidegger's 'turn' marks the transition from acting to being: in Being and Time anxiety, boredom, and death are read as calls to resolute self-assertion and action, but after the turn the same moods reveal Being, and human existence gains its radiance only from inactivities—festivals, play, dance, adornment—that suspend 'care' and the emphasis on the self.**
    - Early Heidegger takes anxiety as the possibility of taking hold of one's proper self and deciding to act, though anxiety strictly means the impossibility of action; Being and Time's world is everywhere a 'work-world' with no festive space.
    - "To the festive there belongs radiance... To the radiance of celebration belong play and dance." —Martin Heidegger
    - After the turn the emphasis on the self gives way to releasement (Gelassenheit) and exuberance (Ausgelassenheit); death could mean a loosening of the self's grip and a friendliness towards the world rather than a consolidation of the I-am.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-024"></span>**Heidegger's ethics of timidity, drawn from his memorial to his Provençal hostess Marcelle Mathieu, models inactivity: awe before the vast, unavailable landscape causes one to take oneself back, disarm, and become an attentive listener to the 'unspoken' language of the earth—so that not-doing (declining to ring the doorbell) proves more powerful than anything done.**
    - "she did not dare to ring the bell—and left again. In this way, what is not done is sometimes more powerful than what is said and done." —Martin Heidegger
    - Timidity (Scheu) and hesitant awe before what cannot be made deliver the self to the other of the self, teaching a friendly receptivity and the ability to listen; the fate of the earth depends on our ability to listen to it.

## The Pathos of Action

Hannah Arendt's philosophy, dominated by the pathos of action, freedom, novelty, and immortal fame, expels the Sabbath, the sacred temenos, the festive, and contemplation from human life; Han argues that this glorification of action is itself the source of hyperactivity and planetary burnout, and that the future depends on resuscitating the vita contemplativa.

* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-025"></span>**In Judaism God is Sabbath, and rest (menucha) is the very essence and completion of creation, suspending history in blissful inactivity; Arendt, though a Jewish thinker, lacks this Sabbath dimension entirely, holding with John Adams that 'it is action, not rest, that constitutes our pleasure' and locating the divine in the freedom to begin something new.**
    - "What was the world lacking? Rest. The Sabbath came, and so came rest." —Rashi
    - Arendt: with the creation of man the principle of beginning—freedom—came into the world; a human is a 'somebody' only insofar as he acts and introduces a new beginning.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-026"></span>**Arendt idealizes the Greek polis as an individualistic stage of freedom and immortal fame while expelling the temenos—the sacred, fenced-in space of contemplative vision from which 'contemplation' (templum) derives—so that festivals, rituals, and games have no place in her thought, and the individuality of a 'someone' is impossible in the collective ritual body.**
    - The polis consists of three spaces—oikos, agora, and temenos; Arendt ignores the temenos, the templum reserved for the gods, the realm of eternal ideas perceived by the theoretical gaze.
    - Arendt equates being alive with acting: only a 'somebody' displaying uniqueness before an audience claims reality, while non-actors have only an animalistic 'feeling of life' lacking the 'feeling of reality.'
    - "This polis did not really know subjectivity as the measure of all objectivity. It fitted the fittingness of the gods, who, in turn, were subjected to destiny, Moira." —Martin Heidegger
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-027"></span>**Arendt's messianism of freedom decouples the political from the social and relegates poverty, hunger, and misery to the realm of mere necessity and technology, holding that the French Revolution failed because the misery of the masses obstructed freedom—a stance Han condemns as blind to the structural violence of global capitalism.**
    - "every attempt to solve the social question with political means leads to terror." —Hannah Arendt
    - Arendt praises the American Revolution for succeeding thanks to the 'invisibility of slaves,' while ignoring the French Revolution's abolition of aristocracy and serfdom as genuine political acts.
    - "Every child who starves to death is murdered." —Jean Ziegler
* <span class="pull-quote">If it does not incorporate the vita contemplativa, the vita activa degenerates into hyperactivity, and culminates in the burnout not only of the psyche but of the whole planet.</span>
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-028"></span>**The modern pathos of the new, which Arendt idealizes, dissolves being into process and makes contemplation impossible; Nietzsche's counter-figures—the 'genius of meditation' and the contemplative 'farmers of the spirit' who make old thoughts bear fruit—reveal that the new becomes destructive unless contained, and Kierkegaard's elevation of repetition over novelty is a revolt against the modern age.**
    - "One becomes weary only of what is new. One never grows weary of the old." —Søren Kierkegaard
    - Nietzsche juxtaposes the preachers of the new—the 'strongest and most evil spirits'—with the 'farmers of the spirit' such as Pascal, Epictetus, Seneca, and Plutarch; the crisis of modernity rests on the departure of the genius of contemplation.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-029"></span>**Arendt's assumption that society is merely the household writ large blinds her social theory to power structures; because she isolates the political from the social she cannot analyze the regimes—disciplinary, neoliberal, informational—that Foucault's biopower reveals, nor the transition from industrial mass society to the neoliberal performance society in which the vita activa becomes a vita performativa.**
    - Every society is a power structure that turns the human into a subjugated subject; the disciplinary regime turns 'formless clay, an inapt body' into a machine, and a new medium (industrialization, digitalization) means a new regime and revolution.
    - The neoliberal regime is not repressive but 'smart': domination becomes a compulsion to increase performance, interpreted as freedom, so that believing we realize ourselves we voluntarily exploit ourselves.
    - Han notes a 'society of singularities': everyone is a performer of the self, and the age of digital media makes everyone a producer and broadcaster—the opposite of Arendt's leveling mass society.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-030"></span>**Against Arendt's claim that the 'who' of a person appears only in action, Han locates identity's ground in the daimon/genius: a genius of inactivity that says 'Stop!' (as Socrates' inner voice prevented him from acting) and shatters the ego's self-sufficiency, revealing a propertyless, nameless countenance that appears only when one leaves the stage in the radiance of inactivity.**
    - "whenever it speaks it turns me away from something I am about to do, but it never encourages me to do anything." —Socrates (in Plato's Apology)
    - The Roman genius is 'the personalization of what, in us, goes beyond and exceeds us'; living with the genius is a day-bright mysticism, a standing-beside-oneself that is a formula for happiness and does not know time.
    - Natality commits us to action and entangles us with time without happiness; only inactivity redeems us from time, which is why Arendt views human happiness with contempt.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-031"></span>**The festive is entirely absent from Arendt's vocabulary, yet the festival—a suspension of time celebrated (begehen) as one walks through a magnificent building—raises us above mere life through exuberance rather than the determination to act; human existence realizes itself only in a vita composita, and it is precisely the loss of contemplation that produced the victory of the animal laborans.**
    - Arendt herself ends The Human Condition citing Cato ('Never is he more active than when he does nothing') and declaring thinking the most active activity—thereby inadvertently praising the vita contemplativa Cicero locates in De Re Publica.
    - "active life must lead us to contemplation, but contemplation... calls us back to activity." —Saint Gregory
    - Arendt misreads Plato's cave as an escape from the world, whereas the parable shows action (the philosopher's risky return and parrhesia) is preceded by the contemplative seeing of truth—the vita activa without the vita contemplativa is blind.

## The Coming Society

The crisis of religion is fundamentally a crisis of attention and contemplation, and Romanticism—Schleiermacher, Hölderlin, Novalis—offers the corrective: a religion of intuition, listening, and self-oblivious union with nature that reconciles humans and nature into a 'coming society' of friendliness where everything is the same yet a little bit different.

* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-032"></span>**The crisis of religion is at root a crisis of attention: as the compulsion to produce and communicate makes contemplative lingering impossible, the soul's hyperactivity ends religious experience, since religion (per Schleiermacher) is not thinking or acting but intuition, feeling, and reverent, child-like passivity toward the universe—and can even exist without God.**
    - "It is neither thinking nor acting, but intuition and feeling... reverent attention and submission, in child-like passivity, to be stirred and filled by the Universe." —Friedrich Schleiermacher
    - Malebranche called attention the natural prayer of the soul; Schleiermacher holds that a religion without God might be better than one with God, since what is essential is the desire for the infinite fulfilled in intuition of the universe.
    - 'To listen' is the verb of religion, whereas 'to act' is the verb of history; self-production and self-exhibition are more damaging to religion than atheism.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-033"></span>**Romanticism gives nature a divine, numinous radiance and locates freedom not in action but in the passivity of intuition and being-with: Hölderlin's 'being' is the reconciled union with nature (Hen kai Pan), and freedom—etymologically rooted in the Indo-Germanic 'fri,' to love—means friendliness, so that the subject, moved to tears before natural beauty, admits it belongs to the earth.**
    - "To be one with everything that lives, to return in blissful self-oblivion into the all of nature, that is the summit of thoughts and joys." —Friedrich Hölderlin
    - Adorno: recollection of nature breaks the subject's self-positing arrogance; tears break the spell the subject casts over nature, and natural beauty can be experienced only through the pain that is a rift through which the other of the subject announces itself.
    - 'Freedom' and 'friend' share the root 'fri' meaning 'to love'; shared willing is a letting-oneself-go into an open belonging to beyng, which is the innermost essence of freedom.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-034"></span>**For Novalis nature is not an inanimate 'it' but a living 'you' that can be reached only by invocation; poetry is a medium of unification, reconciliation, and 'transcendental health' that romanticizes the world by endowing the commonplace with higher meaning, thereby counteracting the profanation intensified by digitalization, in which counting replaces recounting and numbers are the degree zero of meaning.**
    - "Does not the rock become a proper thou the very moment I address it?" —Novalis
    - Romanticizing means giving the world back its magic: endowing the ordinary with a mysterious appearance and the finite with the semblance of the infinite; the poet is a 'transcendental physician' because separation and isolation make people ill.
    - Information is additive, not narrative; the French numérique reveals that counting is the opposite of recounting, and numbers tell nothing.
* <span id="vita-contemplativa-a-praise-of-inactivity-035"></span>**Novalis's radical universalism envisions a 'coming society'—a world family and republic of the living beyond nation and identity—grounded in an ethos of friendliness; drawing on the Hasidic parable (told by Benjamin) that in the world to come everything will be the same 'only a little bit different,' Han interprets this as things becoming friendly toward one another, permeating each other in a 'reasonable chaos' of reconciliation between humans and nature.**
    - "Everything will be the same as here—only a little bit different." —Walter Benjamin (Hasidic saying)
    - The cabalist rabbi's parable: to establish the reign of peace it suffices to displace a cup or bush or stone just a little, but this small displacement is so difficult that the Messiah must come.
    - Novalis's fragment names the future world a 'reasonable chaos' where humans are but fellow citizens in a republic of plants, animals, stones, clouds and stars.
