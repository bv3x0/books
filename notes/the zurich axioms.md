# The Zurich Axioms

## Metadata
- Thesis: Wealth is built not by avoiding risk but by embracing it deliberately and managing it through twelve contrarian principles that reject conventional investment wisdom, emphasizing decisive action, early profit-taking, and mobility over the false security of diversification, long-range planning, and expert forecasts.
- Topics: speculative risk-taking, behavioral finance and investor psychology, contrarian investing, gambling and probability, wealth-building philosophy
- Categories: business & economics, self-help, philosophy
- Author: Max Gunther
- Year: 1985
- Mode: quick

## Introduction

The Zurich Axioms originated from a post-WWII club of Swiss speculators on Wall Street who codified their unwritten rules of risk management into twelve major and sixteen minor axioms; Switzerland's improbable wealth demonstrates that deliberate, skilled risk-taking—not safety-seeking—is the only path out of the 'great unrich.'

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-001"></span>**Switzerland's extraordinary wealth despite lacking natural resources, farmland, or coastline proves that intelligent risk-taking, not resource abundance, generates national and personal prosperity.**
    - Switzerland matches American, West German, and Japanese per-capita income while possessing almost no oil, coal, or fertile land—its edge is that its citizens are the world's cleverest investors, speculators, and gamblers.
    - The Swiss concluded that the sensible way to conduct one's life is not to shun risk but to expose oneself to it deliberately—to bet, but with care and thought.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-002"></span>**The Zurich Axioms were codified by a loose club of Swiss speculators on Wall Street after WWII, including the author's father Frank Henry Gunther, who forced each other to articulate the tacit rules guiding their best speculative moves.**
    - Frank Henry, a Swiss-born banker who headed the New York branch of the Swiss Bank Corporation (Schweizerbankverein), had absorbed speculative rules into his bones but could not initially explain them when asked.
    - The group met irregularly at Oscar's Delmonico and other Wall Street venues through the 1950s, 60s, and 70s, questioning each other: 'Why are you buying gold now? What made you sell this stock when everybody else was buying?'
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-003"></span>**The conventional belief that worry is a sickness to be avoided is the opposite of speculative wisdom: worry is an indicator that one is risking enough to have any hope of meaningful gain.**
    - Modern psychology's central assumption—that mental health means being calm—has produced books like How to Stop Worrying and Start Living and The Relaxation Response, none of which offer trustworthy evidence that worry is harmful.
    - The Axioms are not just a philosophy of speculation but guideposts for successful living; they have made a lot of people rich.

## The First Major Axiom: On Risk

Accepting and even embracing worry as the price of meaningful risk is the foundation of speculative success, because the only realistic chance of escaping financial mediocrity requires putting capital at genuine risk—a truth illustrated by the diverging fates of the cautious 'Sober Sylvia' and the adventurous 'Mad Mary.'

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-004"></span>**The parable of Sober Sylvia and Mad Mary illustrates that safety-seeking produces genteel poverty while deliberate risk-taking produces wealth: Mary, who started identically to Sylvia, became rich through gold speculation while Sylvia subsists in a cold one-bedroom flat.**
    - Gerald Loeb, author of The Battle for Investment Survival, observed both women from the start; Mary bought gold at $40–$50 per ounce before Nixon severed the gold-dollar link in 1971, then sold most holdings near $600 when gold hit $875.
    - Sylvia's bonds lost capital value during the 1970s interest rate surge, and double-digit inflation eroded her savings; her best move was a house whose equity she split in divorce—still not enough to retire comfortably.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-005"></span>**Jesse Livermore's attitude—preferring a life of worry to a life of poverty—captures the First Axiom's core: worry is not a malady but the natural, healthy companion of an adventurous financial life.**
    - "'Every occupation has its aches and pains. If you keep bees, you get stung. Me, I get worried. It's either that or stay poor. If I've got a choice between worried and poor, I'll take worried anytime.'" —Jesse Livermore
    - "Livermore admitted worrying about his speculations even in his sleep, but said, 'I don't think I'd enjoy life half as much if I always knew how rich I was going to be tomorrow.'" —Jesse Livermore
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-006"></span>**The distinction between 'investing' and 'speculating' is entirely cosmetic: all investment is speculation, and pretending otherwise leads to self-deception and worse decisions.**
    - "'All investment is speculation. The only difference is that some people admit it and some don't.'" —Gerald Loeb
    - IBM stock, the bluest of blue chips, fell from its 1973 peak and took nine years to recover; GM, an estate-executor-grade 'investment' bought at its 1971 peak, still had not recovered decades later.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-007"></span>**Minor Axiom I: Always play for meaningful stakes, because gains on trivial amounts leave you where you started, and the potential upside vastly exceeds the downside for the well-equipped speculator.**
    - J. Paul Getty, contrary to popular belief, built his billion-dollar fortune from a middle-class stake; his first decisive bet was $500—nearly his entire savings—on a half-share oil lease near Stone Bluff, Oklahoma, which paid $12,000 within months.
    - Frank Henry estimated that if all his speculations blew up simultaneously, he would lose 50 percent—his chosen degree of worry—meaning he always kept 50 percent intact as a floor.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-008"></span>**Minor Axiom II: Diversification, despite its near-sacred status in conventional advice, actively prevents wealth accumulation by diluting stakes below meaningful levels, creating gain-loss cancellation, and overwhelming the investor with unmanageable complexity.**
    - Diversification has three fatal flaws: it forces bets too small to matter, creates a situation where gains and losses cancel each other out, and turns the speculator into a juggler who cannot take decisive action when trouble hits any single position.
    - The author's personal rule is no more than four simultaneous speculations, most often three or fewer—sometimes just one—because watching a few baskets is far more effective than watching a dozen.

## The Second Major Axiom: On Greed

Greed—acquisitiveness gone out of control—destroys speculative gains by inducing speculators to ride winning streaks past their natural end, and the antidote is to decide on an ending position before entering any venture and exit deliberately when that position is reached, accepting the fear of regret as an unavoidable cost.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-009"></span>**Winning streaks are overwhelmingly more likely to be short than long, so the rational strategy is always to assume brevity and exit early rather than ride for a peak that cannot be seen in advance.**
    - Casino manager Sherlock Feldman observed a roulette player turn $10 into $9,800 through seven consecutive wins, then lose everything—including her original stake—by reaching for a round $10,000 figure rather than cashing out.
    - Long, high winning streaks make news precisely because they are rare; short, modest ones are vastly more common, so betting on the short and modest is mathematically correct.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-010"></span>**The fear of regret—watching a winning set continue after you have exited—is real and painful but must be accepted as the irreducible cost of a disciplined strategy that, across many ventures, produces superior results.**
    - A South American speculator who sold Wometco Enterprises profitably then watched its price quadruple became so paralyzed by fear of repeat regret that he held Warner Communications through an Atari-division collapse that destroyed two-thirds of its value.
    - The author sold Gulf Oil at $31 and watched it reach $60; he sold 1,500 shares of IBM at $70 and watched it reach $130—such experiences are painful but inevitable, and the discipline to accept them is what separates winners from losers.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-011"></span>**Minor Axiom III: Decide your target gain before entering a venture and treat reaching that level as a concrete ending—psychologically reinforced by a pre-promised reward—because each successive position otherwise feels like a new starting point, making exit nearly impossible.**
    - Connecticut couple Alice and Harry held their Fairfield County house past a three-fold gain hoping for a ten-fold Marin County-style result; the 1981–82 real estate collapse left them barely above their original purchase price after a year on the market with one low offer.
    - Frank Henry rewarded himself with oysters and American steaks; Jesse Livermore bought antique shaving mugs; Gerald Loeb's friend Mary bought a new dress—trivial prizes that created a concrete psychological sense of ending and completion.

## The Third Major Axiom: On Hope

The inability to exit losing positions quickly—caused by fear of regret, reluctance to abandon sunk capital, and the ego cost of admitting error—is the single most expensive failure in speculation, and the remedy is to treat early, small losses as a normal operating cost rather than a disaster.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-012"></span>**Cutting losses fast is the defining skill of successful speculators: champion commodity trader Martin Schwartz, who increased his playing capital 175 percent in 1983 to win the U.S. Trading Championship, credited his success entirely to learning how to lose.**
    - "'I'll tell you how I became a winner. I learned how to lose.'" —Martin Schwartz
    - Casino manager Sherlock Feldman said the defining quality of a good poker player is 'knowing when to fold'—the professional studies how to save himself when cards fall against him, while the amateur merely hopes.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-013"></span>**Susan Garner's office-building investment illustrates the compounding cost of hoping: refusing a $1,600 offer when she had paid $2,000, she ultimately sold for $750 after watching the price decline while her capital was frozen and other opportunities were inaccessible.**
    - A highway project near a suburban office building was progressively delayed—first one year, then five, then indefinitely—and each announcement made Garner more stubborn rather than more realistic about her exit.
    - Gerald Loeb's rule of thumb: sell whenever a stock retreats 10–15 percent from its highest held price regardless of whether you have a gain or a loss; Frank Henry gave himself 10–20 percent leeway—both aimed at taking small losses to avoid large ones.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-014"></span>**Three psychological obstacles block timely exit: fear of regret (the loser may recover), reluctance to abandon part of an investment (especially on margin where margin calls force further commitment), and difficulty admitting error—with women and older people generally overcoming the last obstacle more readily than men and younger people.**
    - Margin speculation resembles poker: just as a poker player must keep adding money to stay in a losing hand, a margin investor facing a falling stock faces a margin call—either commit new cash or be sold out, forcing a decision between throwing good money after bad or abandoning the position.
    - The typical loser refuses to admit error, tells himself the drop is temporary, and protects his ego at the expense of his bankbook; years later when the investment recovers he exults 'I was right!'—but during all those years his capital could have doubled elsewhere.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-015"></span>**Minor Axiom IV: Accept small losses as a routine operating expense of speculation—like taxes or utility bills—because the habit of cutting losses protects against catastrophic losses and keeps capital mobile for the next opportunity.**
    - Stop-loss orders offer the advantage of pre-committing to a loss level and removing agonizing real-time decisions, but rob the speculator of flexibility and encourage mental disengagement from the position.
    - The most productive attitude is to expect small losses the way you expect electric bills: not fun, but a known cost of doing business that buys the right to hope for big gains.

## The Fourth Major Axiom: On Forecasts

Financial forecasts are systematically unreliable because all money phenomena are expressions of human behavior—which is inherently unpredictable—and the successful speculator ignores prophetic pronouncements entirely, reacting instead to events as they actually unfold.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-016"></span>**Economic consensus forecasts have a consistent record of spectacular failure: in 1969 economists predicted inflation would taper off in the 1970s (it doubled); in 1979 they predicted double-digit inflation would persist to the mid-1980s (it collapsed by 1982).**
    - In 1983, three-fifths of professional money managers—highly paid specialists with computers and research departments—performed worse than the Standard & Poor's 500 index, meaning a novice throwing dice would have beaten them.
    - One celebrated manager predicted falling interest rates in 1983 and loaded clients into bonds (rates rose), predicted drug stocks would rise (they fell), and concentrated on MCI as a telephone-industry winner (it was a dog).
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-017"></span>**Prophets succeed commercially not by being right but by forecasting often—a few correct predictions get publicized while the majority of wrong ones are ignored, creating a false impression of prophetic ability.**
    - "'You make twenty-five predictions and the ones that come true are the ones you talk about.'" —Theodore Levitt
    - Stock market guru Joseph Granville's 'sell everything' warning in early 1981 caused a 23-point Dow plunge—a self-fulfilling prophecy—but his prediction of another crash on September 28, 1981 was followed by one of the NYSE's biggest single-day gains in history.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-018"></span>**Financial phenomena are entirely unpredictable because they are manifestations of human behavior—millions of people making billions of decisions—not physical events subject to the kind of modeling that allows tide tables or lunar calendars.**
    - Stock prices rise and fall not because accounting ledgers contain certain abstract figures, but because people think the prospects are good or bad—because they are worried, discouraged, or simply because a four-day weekend is coming.
    - Value Line's July 1983 'Selected Stocks for Performance' list included Apple Computer at $55 per share; by a few months later the price had crashed to $17.25—events that no forecast can rule out because every prediction faces unknowable variables.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-019"></span>**The practical consequence of the Fourth Axiom is to base all speculative moves on observable present developments rather than forecast-dependent assumptions, and to never let a forecast become a reason to ignore the Third Axiom when a position turns sour.**
    - Long-term bank CDs sold in the early 1970s at 7–8 percent were sold on economists' predictions that rates would peak and fall; rates instead soared to 10–11 percent by decade's end, trapping depositors in below-market instruments for years.
    - Design your speculative program on the basis of quick reactions to events you can actually see developing; your initial bet is a hope, not a prophecy—never let it harden into a declaration that a venture 'is bound to succeed.'

## The Fifth Major Axiom: On Patterns

The money world is a domain of genuine chaos in which apparent patterns are ephemeral illusions produced by random events, and the most dangerous moment for a speculator is when chaos begins to look orderly—as it did for Yale economist Irving Fisher just before the 1929 crash.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-020"></span>**Irving Fisher's 1929 prediction of a 'permanently high plateau' in stock prices—made just before the worst crash in Wall Street history—demonstrates that even brilliant academics are destroyed when they mistake luck for discerned pattern.**
    - Fisher believed he had beaten the market through intelligence, but he had been lucky; his confidence led him to be unprepared for his streak to end, and like many others, he went down with the crash.
    - The Sovereign American Art Fund, managed by experts who believed they could spot emerging art trends, sold its initial offering at $6 per share; within two years the shares traded at 75 cents after prized acquisitions turned out obscure or fraudulent.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-021"></span>**Mutual fund managers—the world's best-paid, best-equipped investment professionals—consistently fail to beat market averages, proving that no formula can overcome chaos; Forbes found nine out of ten funds fell as fast or faster than stocks overall in bear markets.**
    - Citibank's professional portfolio management service, trusted by Wall Street Journal editor Alfred Malabre, bought Avon Products at $119 (fell to under $20), Sears at $110 (fell to $41.50), and IBM at just under $400 (fell to $151).
    - Two books on the same shelf give exactly opposite advice: Lewis Owen's How Wall Street Doubles My Money Every Three Years says buy stocks near twelve-month highs (momentum); Samuel Greenfield's The Low-High Theory of Investment says buy near twelve-month lows (mean reversion). Both cannot be right; neither is.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-022"></span>**Minor Axiom V (The Historian's Trap): The belief that history repeats itself reliably enough to bet money on it is false and dangerous—Frank Henry's protégée lost heavily on Italian lira speculation after constructing an elaborate historical formula for dollar-lira movements that the market simply ignored.**
    - The young woman told the market it was wrong when her historical pattern failed to materialize; 'Formulas can be wrong, but markets never are. The market does what it does. It makes no predictions and offers no promises.'
    - Wall Street's Republican First-Year Jinx—the stock market's consistent slump in every new Republican president's first year, from Hoover through Reagan—is a random correlation indistinguishable from the Super Bowl Omen, yet investors construct elaborate causal theories around it.
* <span class="pull-quote">Chaos is not dangerous until it begins to look orderly.</span>
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-023"></span>**Minor Axiom VI (The Chartist's Illusion): Graphing price movements makes disorderly data look like meaningful trends; the solid black line on graph paper creates an illusion of solidity and predictability that the underlying numbers do not support.**
    - Stock chartists look for 'jiggles and wiggles' that preceded past price movements and bet on their recurrence; the market has no patterns—making charts of stock prices is like making charts of ocean froth, which you see once and never again in identical form.
    - Mutual fund promoters squeeze years close together on chart pages and bridge over bad years to produce soaring trend lines; the same technique can make a stagnant or inflation-losing fund look like a remarkable performer.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-024"></span>**Minor Axiom VII (Correlation and Causality Delusions): The human mind's compulsive need to construct causal links between correlated events leads speculators to invent mechanisms that don't exist and bet heavily on them—as the author did by losing 25 percent on a Honeywell-IBM 'inverse relationship' that turned out to be pure coincidence.**
    - The author noticed Honeywell and IBM often moved in opposite directions over 18 months and invented a hypothesis that institutional investors periodically shifted mountains of cash between them; in reality the correlation was random, and both stocks plunged together when he finally committed heavily.
    - Unless you can actually observe a cause operating, treat all causal hypotheses with greatest skepticism; when events happen together, assume the proximity results from chance unless you have hard evidence otherwise.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-025"></span>**Minor Axiom VIII (The Gambler's Fallacy): The belief that one is temporarily 'hot' or in a lucky state that will influence random outcomes is a dangerous illusion; Charles Wells broke the bank at Monte Carlo three times in 1891 and Caroline Otero turned two louis into a fortune—both then acted as though permanently invincible, and both died broke.**
    - Wells chose single-number roulette on his third night and hit the number 5 five consecutive times—walking out with over 100,000 francs equivalent to more than a million modern dollars; Otero's color bet came up twenty-eight consecutive times. Both were saved from further losses only by house limits and exhausted bank reserves.
    - Tossing a coin long enough produces memorable runs of heads, but there is nothing orderly about the run—you cannot know when it will start or how long it will last; the Gambler's Fallacy encourages riders to stay on past the peak, which is how fortunes are lost.

## The Sixth Major Axiom: On Mobility

Speculators who allow sentiment, loyalty, nostalgia, or the inertia of waiting for a payoff to root them in positions sacrifice the mobility essential to acting on better opportunities, and only by keeping capital genuinely free and movable can they consistently capture the best available returns.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-026"></span>**Minor Axiom IX: Emotional attachments—to neighborhoods, companies, or familiar investments—are among the most expensive speculative errors, as illustrated by a couple who refused to sell their declining Fairfield County home out of loyalty to neighbors and the family memories it contained, ultimately receiving far less than they could have.**
    - Realtor Janice Shattuck identified a willing buyer—an absentee landlord who would have paid a fair price—but the homeowners were bound by loyalty to remaining neighbors who also eventually sold and left anyway.
    - A chief engineer who held stock in his own troubled company partly because the chairman was ostentatiously buying more stock as a loyalty signal ended holding worthless shares; Frank Henry later wondered whether the chairman had secretly been shorting the same stock in a second account.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-027"></span>**Minor Axiom X: Never hesitate to abandon a venture when something more promising appears; the feeling that a stagnant investment 'owes' you a payoff is irrational, because the same gain is equally valuable regardless of which vehicle produces it.**
    - Getting trapped between a speculation and a hobby—collecting art or coins you have become too attached to sell—freezes capital at precisely the moment when other opportunities require it.
    - The possibility of regret from switching (Hoo Boy stirs while you hold Hey Wow) exactly equals the regret of not switching (Hey Wow soars while you wait for Hoo Boy)—since the regret cancels out, the decision should rest solely on which investment offers the better near-term prospect.

## The Seventh Major Axiom: On Intuition

A hunch is not mystical but is rather a conclusion drawn from a large library of experience stored below conscious access, and hunches deserve to be acted on only when the speculator can plausibly identify a relevant body of accumulated knowledge from which the intuition might have arisen—and especially not when the hunch is merely a disguised hope for an outcome one desires.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-028"></span>**Hunches are ordinary mental events—conclusions assembled from nonconscious data—not supernatural signals; Dr. Eugene Gendlin of the University of Chicago identifies this as the common human experience of knowing something without knowing how one knows it.**
    - You instantly recognize a close friend's face without being able to specify which features identify them, and recognize their voice without being able to describe it—this is the same cognitive mechanism that generates a useful hunch about a market.
    - A New England real estate speculator woke at 3 a.m. with a powerful hunch that the Maine coast house-price market was about to soften; she had spent years subscribing to local papers, attending property owners' association meetings, and reading Business Week—the hunch was likely assembled from dozens of half-remembered data points.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-029"></span>**The test for a trustworthy hunch is whether the speculator possesses a plausible library of relevant knowledge—if no such data base exists, the hunch should be discarded regardless of how insistent it feels.**
    - A hunch about a newly appointed Hoo Boy Computer president—based solely on a news article and a reporter who likely never heard of the man before—fails the test because no relevant library of data exists from which the intuition could have been assembled.
    - Even well-grounded hunches must not produce overconfidence: intuition is a useful speculative tool but not an infallible formula, and the other Axioms—especially on cutting losses—must remain in force no matter how strong the hunch.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-030"></span>**Minor Axiom XI: A hunch that promises an outcome you want is far more suspect than one pointing toward an outcome you don't want, because hope and wishful thinking masquerade as intuition with alarming ease.**
    - Buying paintings by an obscure artist and generating a hunch that they will become valuable inverts the suspicion hierarchy—one should be most skeptical precisely when the intuition aligns with one's financial wishes.
    - By contrast, an intuition warning of danger in a position one wants to hold is more trustworthy because it runs against the pull of desire and self-interest.

## The Eighth Major Axiom: On Religion and the Occult

Neither religious faith nor occult practices—astrology, Tarot, clairvoyance—have ever produced consistently superior financial results for their practitioners, and relying on supernatural assistance is actively dangerous because it induces the unworried state that is fatal to sound speculation.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-031"></span>**Jesse Livermore's lifelong confusion about whether his speculative gift was clairvoyance or skill—amplified by press coverage of events like his short sale of Union Pacific just before the 1906 San Francisco earthquake—contributed to four bankruptcies and ultimately his 1940 suicide.**
    - Livermore shorted Union Pacific in April 1906 based on a hunch the price was too high; the San Francisco earthquake the following day destroyed millions in Union Pacific property, the stock plummeted, and he made $300,000—but this was luck, not prophecy, as other speculators also shorted the stock.
    - His 1940 book How to Trade in Stocks—the Livermore Formula for Combining Time Element and Price was a hymn to patterns and 'Pivotal Points' that contradicted his actual gift; his final bankruptcy and suicide suggest the impossible task of reconciling his two approaches.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-032"></span>**The author's own experience with Tarot cards over Control Data stock—holding past his $120 ending position to $155 based on a 'glorious' card reading, then exiting on an 'appalling' reading before the stock crashed to $28—produced a good financial outcome that was entirely attributable to luck, not the cards.**
    - A shabbily dressed man who put his entire $5,000 life savings into calls on Control Data after a Tarot reading, when the stock was ignored at $30, walked out of Godnick & Son with $60,000 six months later when the stock crossed $100—a spectacular hit that proved only that flukes happen.
    - The Tarot master who invited the author to lunch to discuss the cards' financial powers allowed the author to pay the check, then borrowed $5 for cab fare—demonstrating that the cards had not materially benefited their most dedicated practitioner.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-033"></span>**Minor Axiom XII: If astrology worked, all astrologers would be rich—and they aren't; the test of any touted moneymaking approach is not isolated hits but consistent results, and no occult or religious system has ever passed that test.**
    - A Gallup survey found 32 million American adults believed in astrology, yet astrologers and their followers are no richer as a group than any random selection of people—some rich, most in between, nearly all wanting more.
    - The minister in Frank Henry's acquaintance who interpreted a below-market church land purchase as a 'gift from God' discovered the land was an undevelopable swamp the town had repeatedly refused to permit for construction—prayer had not engaged the building inspector.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-034"></span>**Minor Axiom XIII: A superstition need not be eliminated but merely confined to situations that are genuinely impervious to rational analysis—such as picking a lottery number—where it can be enjoyed humorously without affecting serious financial decisions.**
    - Charles Kellner of Hillsdale, New Jersey, keeps his serious money in real estate and a restaurant where no supernatural belief intrudes, but plays the New Jersey three-digit lottery using numbers from dreams—he has won twice this way and treats it as pure entertainment.
    - The correct use of superstition is humorously, in situations impervious to analysis; the correct time is never when rational figuring-out is possible.

## The Ninth Major Axiom: On Optimism and Pessimism

Optimism feels good and is therefore dangerous: it produces clouded judgment, encourages entering ventures with no exits, and paralyzes exit from failing ones, whereas genuine confidence—knowing in advance how you will handle the worst case—is the productive alternative that the constructive use of pessimism generates.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-035"></span>**Professional gamblers exploit optimism systematically: the amateur hopes for lucky cards when the odds say fold, and this misplaced optimism is exactly what allows professionals to become rich at amateurs' expense.**
    - The pro doesn't have optimism—he has confidence, which springs from the constructive use of pessimism: he goes into the game trained and prepared to act sensibly if his luck is bad, not riding on hope.
    - In money matters, things are nearly always as bad as they seem; a lot of times they're worse. The safest course, almost always, is to assume that if a situation looks bad, it is—and to have planned your response in advance.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-036"></span>**Sam and Judy, San Francisco yuppies who bought deeply discounted lots in a financially troubled southwestern land development, lost their $25,000 nest egg permanently because optimism prevented them from building any exit: land more than a mile from paved roads was unsalable when the development corporation collapsed.**
    - They could have bought higher-priced lots closer to the developed segment where they could have built a short access road if the corporation failed—sacrificing some upside to preserve a meaningful exit; instead they chose maximum upside with no escape route.
    - Many years later the corporation no longer exists, the promised roads and utilities were never built, the state attorney general pursued principals with little success, and the lots remain accessible only on foot or horseback.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-037"></span>**Financial journalism systematically over-represents bullish opinion because optimism is more pleasant to report and more pleasant to hear—a structural bias that misleads speculators about the actual balance of opinion in the market.**
    - By the author's informal count over years, at least three-fourths of market readings quoted in major newspaper columns were optimistic—a lopsided showing, since the market's future is statistically equally likely to be bad as good on any given day.
    - One perennially quoted brokerage officer predicted an imminent bull market throughout 1980 and 1981 while the market went nowhere; after the August 1982 bull market arrived and briefly paused, he declared the 'second great leg' had begun—the Dow then gave back its gains within two days.

## The Tenth Major Axiom: On Consensus

Majority opinion in financial markets is more likely to be wrong than right, partly because it tends to push prices high when sentiment is bullish and low when bearish—meaning the crowd typically buys expensive and sells cheap—and the speculator who thinks independently, as René Descartes urged, gains a structural edge over those who drift with the herd.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-038"></span>**René Descartes—a philosopher who also made a scientific study of gambling and consistently profited from it—built his entire method on refusing to accept majority opinion as proof of truth, a principle directly applicable to financial markets.**
    - "'Scarcely anything has been pronounced by one [learned person] the contrary of which has not been asserted by another. And it would avail nothing to count votes . . . for in the matter of a difficult question, it is more likely that the truth should have been discovered by few than by many.'" —René Descartes
    - Descartes died comfortably well off despite having only a modest inheritance as his visible means of support—gambling in Paris using mathematical analysis and skepticism rather than folk wisdom.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-039"></span>**Minor Axiom XIV: The best time to buy something is often when nobody else wants it—GM common stock was available at a twenty-year low of $34 in 1981–82 when the entire auto industry seemed terminal, and it reached $80 in 1983; Ford, bought at $11 in those dark years, more than quadrupled to $46.**
    - The author's wife had a strong hunch about Ford in early 1982 when the stock was mired at $11—her broker laughed at her and buried her in bearish documentation, and she capitulated to majority pressure despite her independent judgment.
    - Princeton psychology experiments demonstrated that one-third of experimental subjects would state that pencils of obviously different lengths were the same size, rather than contradict the coached majority opinion around the table—financial markets produce identical psychological pressure but with far higher stakes.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-040"></span>**Contrarianism taken to dogma is as dangerous as uncritical majority-following: the crowd is not always wrong, and automatic contrary betting—as with near-terminal Chrysler stock in 1981—would have looked like a lottery ticket purchase even if it ultimately paid off spectacularly.**
    - Chrysler was buyable for $3–4 through most of 1980–82 and hit $35 by end of 1983—a tenfold gain—but from a 1981 viewpoint, the majority shunning it were acting reasonably because the company had one foot in the grave and a debated government loan keeping it alive.
    - The correct approach is to figure everything out independently—sometimes that leads to betting with the majority, sometimes against, but the point is always to arrive at the conclusion through one's own reasoning rather than social drift.

## The Eleventh Major Axiom: On Stubbornness

Perseverance in a specific losing investment—driven by the irrational belief that the investment 'owes' the speculator a gain, or by the desire to have one's judgment vindicated—destroys capital and opportunity, and the Axiom's practical expression is to reject averaging down as a mechanism that compounds losses while providing only psychological comfort.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-041"></span>**A Merrill Lynch client's obsession with Sears Roebuck stock—entered from sentimental attachment to the company's charitable contributions—led to years of 'chasing' through bull and bear markets, missing other opportunities, and ending only slightly better off than when she started.**
    - After selling at a 15–20 percent loss on good advice (Third Axiom), she watched the stock recover and surge past her sale price, becoming determined to 'squeeze some money out of it if she had to wring its neck'—this emotional logic clouded all subsequent decisions.
    - Frank Henry similarly kept buying and selling real estate near Morristown, New Jersey after a loss there, and the author confesses a long-standing emotional fixation on IBM stock—demonstrating that even experienced speculators are vulnerable to this failure.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-042"></span>**Minor Axiom XV: Averaging down—buying more of a falling investment to lower the average cost per share—is a psychologically seductive but financially empty maneuver that may throw good money after bad and prevents the speculator from redeploying capital into genuinely promising alternatives.**
    - Buying 100 additional shares at $50 after an initial 100 shares bought at $100 changes the average cost to $75 but changes nothing about the investment reality: the market has decided the stock is worth $50, and throwing new money in doesn't alter that judgment.
    - Frank Henry knew a man who made himself genuinely happy when his investments fell by averaging down—believing he was being clever—yet got stuck in bad investments for years; the technique is psychologically seductive precisely because it allows inaction to feel like strategy.

## The Twelfth Major Axiom: On Planning

Long-range financial plans create the dangerously false belief that the unknowable future is under control, rooting people in strategies designed for a world that will not exist—as illustrated by a couple whose meticulously constructed 1940s retirement plan left them nearly broke in the 1980s—and the only sound posture is to stay mobile, reactive, and committed only to the general intention of getting rich.

* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-043"></span>**George and Martha's 1940s Long-Range Plan—targeting $700/month retirement income and $20,000 in savings for a house—was destroyed not by poor execution but by the plan's inherent inability to anticipate inflation, interest rate changes, employer bankruptcy, and the compounding of unforeseen expenses over four decades.**
    - The $700/month target that represented top-bracket affluence in the 1940s barely covers 1980s rent without food; the $20,000 house fund wouldn't buy a dog kennel by the time they retired.
    - George had at least one opportunity to leave his CA firm and co-found a now-prosperous independent practice but retreated into the plan's 'cozy comfort'—refusing a risk because the plan assured him a nice outcome if he didn't; the plan's bird in hand flew away anyway.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-044"></span>**The future of financial affairs is not dimly visible but entirely invisible beyond the near term; attempting to plan across twenty or thirty years is categorically different from planning a week ahead, and the money world changes through unknowable events—booms, crashes, wars, technological shifts—that make any specific plan obsolete.**
    - "Jesse Livermore wrote that money lost by short-term speculation is small compared to the gigantic sums lost by investors who let investments ride: 'The [long-term] investors are the big gamblers. They make a bet, stay with it, and if it goes wrong, they can lose it all.'" —Jesse Livermore
    - Planners extend today's observable trends into the future as though the money world is a tree growing predictably; they cannot know which trends will reverse, which new ones will emerge, or whether there will be a dollar or anything to spend it on in twenty years.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-045"></span>**Minor Axiom XVI: Long-term investments must be shunned because they imprison capital in arrangements built for a future that never arrives; Paula W.'s Ford-stock retirement strategy saw her income collapse from $52,000 per year to zero when Ford cut its dividend entirely in 1982, forcing her to sell depreciated shares at exactly the wrong moment.**
    - Paula started with 20,000 shares at $2.60 per share annual dividend ($52,000/year), refused her broker's repeated warnings to switch to utility stocks better suited to income needs, and by 1982 received no dividend at all while being forced to sell shares at depressed prices to cover living expenses.
    - Every investment should be re-evaluated and made to justify itself afresh at least every three months by asking: 'Would I put my money into this if it were presented to me for the first time today?'—if the answer is no, the investment has become a long-term trap.
* <span id="the-zurich-axioms-046"></span>**Life insurance products that combine investment with protection—whole life and its variants—are long-term traps that lock capital into arrangements designed for an unknowable future; the correct alternative is cheap term insurance, which protects dependents without imprisoning capital.**
    - Whole-life policies are front-end-loaded, meaning the salesperson collects a large proportion of multi-decade commissions in the first year or two—his short-term interest and the buyer's long-term commitment are radically misaligned.
    - Term insurance pays off on death and nothing else; when circumstances change—children grow up, debts are paid—premiums simply stop, freeing all capital for speculative deployment rather than locking it in an annuity built for a world that doesn't yet exist.
