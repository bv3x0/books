# Signs and Machines: Capitalism and the Production of Subjectivity

## Metadata
- Thesis: Capitalism operates through two complementary apparatuses—social subjection and machinic enslavement—that together constitute a 'subjective economy' in which the production of subjectivity is inseparable from economic production, and the current crisis is fundamentally a crisis in capitalism's capacity to generate viable modes of subjectivation.
- Topics: production of subjectivity, machinic enslavement vs. social subjection, asignifying semiotics, political subjectivation, neoliberal governmentality
- Categories: philosophy, political science, social science
- Author: Maurizio Lazzarato
- Year: 2014
- Mode: quick

## Introduction

Guattari's 1984 claim that the West faces a crisis of subjectivity—not merely an economic or political crisis—serves as the book's organizing thesis: capitalism's power lies in articulating economic flows with the production of subjectivity, and neoliberalism has failed at this articulation, producing only regressive figures like the indebted man rather than viable new modes of existence.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-001"></span>**Germany and Japan's postwar recoveries demonstrate that economic revival depends on the reconstruction of a 'capital of subjectivity'—collective intelligence, will, and new modes of existence—rather than material resources alone, making subjectivity a primary productive force.**
    - Japan's ongoing stagnation since the 1990s illustrates the converse: injecting capital without producing new modes of subjectivity cognizant of changed conditions cannot restore growth.
    - "Japan 'recovered aspects of their archaic subjectivity, converting them into the most advanced forms of social and material production,' creating an industrial complex for the production of subjectivity." —Felix Guattari
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-002"></span>**Neoliberalism's promotion of the 'entrepreneur of the self' as the universal model of subjectivation has paradoxically produced widespread depression, heteronomy masked as autonomy, and the regressive figure of the indebted man as the defining subject of the current crisis.**
    - The autonomy and initiative demanded of each individual constitute new norms of employability and therefore, strictly speaking, a heteronomy.
    - For the majority of the population, becoming 'human capital' means no more than being compelled to manage declining wages, precarity, unemployment, and poverty as if managing a corporate balance-sheet.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-003"></span>**Capitalism produces subjectivity through two heterogeneous but complementary apparatuses: social subjection, which manufactures individuated subjects with identities and roles, and machinic enslavement, which dismantles the individuated subject and mobilizes pre-individual and supra-individual components of subjectivity.**
    - Social subjection assigns us identity, sex, profession, nationality; machinic enslavement makes us 'dividuals'—component parts of technical and social machines rather than persons.
    - Contemporary critical theories (Badiou, Butler, Zizek, Ranciere, cognitive capitalism) focus on subjection while neglecting machinic enslavement, losing the specificity of capitalist domination.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-004"></span>**The current political impasse—in which strikes, revolts, and riots proliferate but fail to institute durable rupture—results from movements' inability to articulate the 'rupture with causality' characteristic of revolutionary action with organizational forms capable of generalizing new modes of subjectivation.**
    - Revolutionary possibility is characterized by the impossible becoming real, a process erupting at points where 'the world was once closed' that secretes other systems of reference.
    - Although the Leninist organizational forms are neither possible nor desirable today, the methodology—breaking with social-democracy and constructing tools for political innovation at the level of subjectivity—remains urgent.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-005"></span>**Political subjectivation must traverse a non-discursive, asignifying 'unnamable point' before it can generate new languages, knowledge, and politics, which is why theories that privilege language, cognition, or culture as the basis of politics systematically fail to account for how rupture actually occurs.**
    - Subjective mutation is not primarily discursive; it affects the nucleus of non-discursivity at the heart of subjectivity from which new discourses and politics can proliferate.
    - Foucault's 'care of the self' and Guattari's 'aesthetic paradigm' converge on the same insight: political action must begin from existential self-affirmation rather than from existing discursive or economic frameworks.

## Production and the Production of Subjectivity: Between Social Subjection and Machinic Enslavement

Deleuze and Guattari's distinction between social subjection (which produces individuated subjects through representational, signifying semiotics) and machinic enslavement (which mobilizes pre-individual and supra-individual subjectivity through asignifying, diagrammatic operations) reveals the dual and complementary mechanisms through which capitalism extracts value from and produces subjectivity across the whole of social life.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-006"></span>**Social subjection assigns individuals identities, roles, and places within the division of labor through signifying semiotics, producing individuated 'persons'—worker, consumer, debtor—who are alienated within these functions yet held responsible for them.**
    - Foucault's governmentality describes subjects who produce themselves as actors in their own assignations, such that domination issues from the subjects themselves through self-exploitation.
    - Property rights constitute the most successful individualizing apparatus by emptying nature, animals, machines, and signs of all creativity and assigning production exclusively to individual human owners.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-007"></span>**Machinic enslavement, borrowed from cybernetics, operates through desubjectivation: the individual becomes a 'dividual,' a component part of social and technical assemblages alongside non-human elements, with subjectivity's components (memory, affect, cognition) no longer unified in a person but distributed across the assemblage.**
    - Unlike subjection's subject/object logic, enslavement involves no such dualism: the dividual is contiguous with machines, constituting inputs and outputs of production and communication processes that exceed them.
    - "The panopticon exemplifies diagrammatic power: 'it automatizes and disindividualizes power' such that 'any individual, taken almost at random, can operate the machine.'" —Michel Foucault
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-008"></span>**The Egyptian megamachine described by Lewis Mumford provides the historical archetype of enslavement: human beings composed into a social machine through centralized coordination, specialized roles, and a supporting 'production of subjectivity'—the myths, priesthood, and bureaucracy required to make people function as machine components.**
    - The megamachine was not primarily technological but social, composed of 'uniform, specialized, interchangeable' parts marshaled together and directed by sign flows (written orders) that fixed accountability.
    - Beginning in the sixteenth century, capitalism revived the megamachine by progressively replacing 'recalcitrant and unreliable' human operators with mechanical, electronic, and chemical ones, culminating today in cybernetic 'generalized enslavement.'
* <span class="pull-quote">Individuals becomes 'dividuals,' and masses become samples, data, markets, or 'banks.'</span>
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-009"></span>**Capital operates with two semiotic registers: signifying semiotics (language, narrative, representation) mobilize consciousness and produce individuated subjects, while asignifying semiotics (stock indices, mathematical equations, computer languages, monetary flows) act directly on real processes bypassing consciousness and representation.**
    - Asignifying semiotics are capitalism's most essential tools because they 'act on things,' connecting organs, intellectual activities, and machines directly without passing through the representations of a subject.
    - The European Central Bank raising the discount rate by one percent and thousands of plans collapsing illustrates how asignifying financial signs act directly on material flows regardless of whether they 'signify something for someone.'
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-010"></span>**Machinic surplus value—produced by the unassignable and unmeasurable productivity of the humans-machines assemblage—exceeds both human labor-time surplus value and what can be captured by cognitive capitalist theories, because production is never the act of an individual or even a cooperative group but always of a collective assemblage of human and non-human elements.**
    - Capital pays for subjection (wages, availability of labor) but exploits the difference between subjection and enslavement: the machinic part of production is neither quantifiable nor assignable, constituting uncompensated surplus.
    - Marx's 'anthropomorphic' view in Capital assigns all productivity to human labor, but the Grundrisse anticipates that production is above all a matter of the machinic assemblage—the mobilization of science, communications, and the social.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-011"></span>**The univocity of 'production'—economic and subjective—means that the field of desire and the field of labor cannot be separated; capitalism captures and exploits not primarily knowledge or culture but the production of new modes of subjectivation whose basis is machinic desire, understood as the creation of possibles from within capitalist assemblages.**
    - Deterritorialized desire is not Freudian drive or Weberian deferred gratification; it is 'an economy of possibilities' and autopoietic subjectivity, which explains both capitalism's dynamism and its current crisis.
    - The failure of 'human capital' as a subjectivation model—transformed by crisis into the mere entrepreneurial management of unemployment and debt—exposes neoliberalism's inability to articulate production and the production of subjectivity.

## Signifying Semiologies and Asignifying Semiotics in Production and in the Production of Subjectivity

Guattari's semiotic theory—distinguishing natural encodings, symbolic/pre-signifying semiologies, semiologies of signification, and asignifying semiotics—reveals that capitalism is organized around asignifying 'languages of infrastructure' that act diagrammatically on real processes, while signifying semiologies serve primarily to reterritorialize the decoded flows of subjectivity onto individuated subjects, a distinction that contemporary critical theories uniformly fail to grasp because of their residual logocentrism.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-012"></span>**Structuralism is dead but language survives in contemporary critical theory as a privileged political medium: Virno, Ranciere, Butler, Agamben, and Zizek all treat language as uniquely human, intrinsically political, or constitutive of the subject, thereby reproducing a logocentrism at odds with capitalism's machine-centric organization of expression.**
    - For Ranciere, the logos constitutes the measure of politics' sole principle—equality; for Virno and Butler the starting point is Hannah Arendt's claim that 'men become political beings as beings of language.'
    - Pasolini observed in the 1960s that neo-capitalism brought about 'the substitution of languages of infrastructures for the languages of superstructures,' marking the first time in history that 'the languages of production are guiding society linguistically.'
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-013"></span>**Guattari's extension of proto-subjectivity and proto-enunciation to non-human assemblages—technical machines, biological systems, aesthetic processes—displaces the subject as the exclusive source of enunciation and requires a general semiotics capable of accounting for human and non-human, signifying and asignifying, modes of expression.**
    - "All machinic systems, whatever domain they belong to—technical, biological, semiotic, logical, abstract—are by themselves the support for proto-subjective processes characterized as 'modular subjectivity' or 'partial subjectivity.'" —Felix Guattari
    - Duchamp's ready-mades and cinema both mark the shift: objects and images 'speak' and express themselves not as capitalist fetishism or alienation but as a new regime of expression requiring a new semiotics.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-014"></span>**Signifying semiologies function politically by establishing national languages, invariant significations, and the 'semiotic triangle' of reference/signification/representation, which together reduce polyvocal symbolic semiotics to individuated subjects and stabilize the social field disrupted by capitalist deterritorialization.**
    - In capitalism, grammar and syntax function as the police of language: 'A rule of grammar is a power marker before it is a syntactical marker,' imposing exclusive disjunctions (you are a man, you are a woman) that prevent heterogeneous becomings.
    - Pasolini's analysis of European linguists as 'colonialists with peoples of color,' treating 'pure speakers' as sources of raw linguistic material while using immigrants to keep wages low, illustrates the political economy of linguistic standardization.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-015"></span>**Asignifying semiotics operate diagrammatically—they connect organs, systems, and machines directly without passing through consciousness or representation, producing 'power signs' or 'sign-points' that anticipate and create reality rather than representing it, and they constitute the specific semiotic engine of capitalism.**
    - Money functions as a power sign when it operates as capital and credit: 'They represent nothing, they have no equivalents, except in the future exploitation of the labor force, nature, and society. They are power signs because instead of representing something they anticipate it, create it, and mold it.'
    - Mathematical equations, computer programs, and diagrams 'directly participate in the process of generating their object,' whereas advertising images only provide an extrinsic representation—this difference marks the fundamental divide between asignifying and signifying semiotics.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-016"></span>**The daily encounter with machines—ATMs, GPS, call centers, online purchasing, television, supermarket self-checkouts—constitutes the practical reality of machinic enslavement in which humans function as component parts and perform unpaid labor for corporate assemblages, demonstrating that critical theory's neglect of machines is a catastrophic blind spot.**
    - At the supermarket the consumer does the work of a clerk for free; buying a plane ticket online means performing unpaid work that increases the company's productivity—these are not metaphors but literal instances of machinic surplus value extraction.
    - In Marx's time there was only the factory's interior and a handful of apparatuses like railroads; today machines are everywhere, especially in daily life, but have disappeared from critical theory.

## Mixed Semiotics

Through four case studies—the financial trader's machinic subjectivity, infant preverbal development, cinema's semiotic mechanisms, and service-sector labor organization—this chapter demonstrates that all actual sites of subjectivation and production involve mixed semiotics (signifying, symbolic, and asignifying simultaneously) in which linguistic and cognitive elements rarely play the principal role, and that machinic enslavement is present in every domain from trading floors to geriatrics clinics.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-017"></span>**The financial trader exemplifies machinic subjectivity: homo economicus is not a rational sovereign subject but a terminal of asignifying, symbolic, and signifying semiotics whose decision-making depends on diagrams, curves, computer networks, mimetic affects, and laws, with signifying discourse serving only to reconstruct the fiction of individual agency after the fact.**
    - Diagrams and curves 'speak' and 'express themselves,' providing thresholds of proto-subjectivity from which human subjectivity determines choices—enunciation would be impossible without these non-human sites of partial subjectivation.
    - Mimetic communication on trading floors occurs through contagion rather than cognition: affects 'stick to subjectivity' pre-personally, making Spinoza's account of transitivist affect more relevant than any model of cognitive rationality.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-018"></span>**Daniel Stern's infant research demonstrates that the three preverbal senses of self (emergent, core, subjective) precede and remain independent of the verbal self, operating through amodal perception, vitality affects, and symbolic semiotics that constitute the 'existential core' and 'ultimate reservoir' for all subsequent learning and creativity.**
    - Vitality affects—'surging,' 'fading,' 'bursting,' 'crescendo'—are captured not by language but by dance, music, and film; they constitute the preverbal 'matrix' from which 'thoughts and perceived forms and identifiable acts and verbalized feelings will later arise.'
    - The emergence of language introduces a cleavage between 'lived' and 'represented' experience: the adult's words 'Oh, look at the yellow sunlight' fracture the child's amodal experience rather than enriching it, potentially forcing pre-verbal experience 'underground.'
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-019"></span>**Cinema's semiotic assemblage—combining asignifying intensities, symbolic semiotics of image and gesture, and signifying chains of language—creates a textbook case of how the film industry neutralizes the desubjectivating potential of non-human semiotics by hierarchizing them through personological and familialist signifiers, functioning as 'group psychoanalysis' that normalizes subjectivity.**
    - Film images, unlike linguistic signs, cannot be fixed by syntagmatic and paradigmatic axes; they reintroduce polyvocality and multireferentiality, making possible—for a brief moment—the production of 'orphans: single, amnesiac, unconscious, and eternal.'
    - Commercial cinema's effect on the unconscious is more powerful than psychoanalysis because its 'irrational, elementary, oneiric elements were forced below the level of consciousness' and exploited as 'subconscious instruments of shock and persuasion' by the culture industry.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-020"></span>**Marie-Anne Dujarier's analysis of mass service-sector labor reveals that the organization of work proceeds primarily through asignifying semiotics—organizational charts, protocols, indicators, management software, budgets—while signifying semiotics only accompany or legitimate commands already issued through diagrammatic apparatuses, a distinction her otherwise excellent sociology fails to conceptualize.**
    - As one moves up the corporate hierarchy, the language of 'work' gives way to the language of 'process'—an acknowledgment that upper management governs machinic enslavement (the humans-machines systems) rather than individual labor.
    - Call center scripts 'taylorize conversation' by reducing signifying semiotics to triggers for prefabricated procedures; the corporation, not school immigrants or youth, is responsible for the 'degradation' of language, as Pasolini already argued in the 1960s.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-021"></span>**Pasolini's 'neo-capitalism' analysis anticipates Guattari's: the languages of production and consumption operate through nonverbal, asignifying, and symbolic semiotics that mold subjectivity at the existential level—body, flesh, behavior—bypassing representation and ideology to produce the 'existential flexibility' capital requires while destroying popular cultures through a 'cultural genocide' more effective than fascist repression.**
    - Unlike fascism's theatrical regimentation, neo-capitalism's 'consumption touched [people's] innermost selves, it gave them other feelings, other ways of thinking, living, other cultural models'—a real rather than scenographic transformation of subjectivity.
    - Pasolini's 'machinic animism'—cinema's capacity to re-enchant reality by making subject and object indistinguishable—offers the possibility of reclaiming what capitalist desacralization destroyed: the animated, expressive nature of the world that peasant and animist cultures preserved.

## Conflict and Sign Systems

The 2003 French intermittent workers' dispute with the unemployment insurance system reveals how signifying semiotic assemblages—composed of scholars, experts, and journalists producing catchphrases, narratives, and subjection machines—function to neutralize singular speech, enforce consensus, and reproduce capitalist subjectivation, while also demonstrating that political rupture requires constructing counter-assemblages of enunciation capable of re-singularizing shared semiotic space.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-022"></span>**The intermittent workers' movement's motto 'We are the experts!' challenged the three transversal practices of capitalist knowledge/power assemblages—division of population into experts and laymen, delegation of speech and decision to specialists, and monopoly over problematization—simultaneously contesting who counts as knowing and who counts as deciding.**
    - The 'co-determination' of French unemployment insurance, based on the Fordist model, has become an 'oligarchical' power excluding new subjects—the unemployed, precarious workers, women, students—produced by neoliberal differentiation since the late 1970s.
    - The power to formulate questions is a power of politicization—introducing new objects and subjects into political space—which is precisely what the Coordination des Intermittents achieved by insisting on 'new social rights' rather than 'the right to work.'
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-023"></span>**Sociologist Pierre Michel Menger's work exemplifies the scholar function in security societies: by framing intermittent employment as a disciplinary 'exception' requiring normalization back to standard employment, his discourse provided journalists with ready-made watchwords that synchronized perfectly with the state's reform agenda while disqualifying intermittents' demands as naive and irrational.**
    - Menger's claim that the system of intermittent employment itself creates insecurity ignores that over thirty years precarity has spread to all economic sectors; his 'exceptional' framing serves to confine political demands within the reassuring categories of the abnormal.
    - The CERC report on job security directly contradicts Menger by treating the phenomena among intermittent workers—fluid status combining unemployment and employment, multiplied contract types—as the emerging rule rather than the exception.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-024"></span>**Media semiotics do not merely communicate catchphrases but constitute a 'subjection machine' that, like psychoanalysis, discredits singular speech through interpretation, reconstructs subjects to fit dominant utterances, and produces the 'freedom of expression' as a correlate of its own apparatus of power—ensuring that more speech produces less singular enunciation.**
    - Television functions based on a small number of ready-made utterances about dominant reality, fitting subjects to prefabricated audiovisual semiotics so that at the interview's end 'you are a subject of utterance,' an effect of the interpretation machine, with the 'impression' of having spoken freely.
    - "De Certeau: 'The media transform the great silence of things into its opposite... Narrated reality constantly tells us what must be believed and what must be done'—statistics and surveys issue the command 'Be quiet!' in the form of facts." —Michel de Certeau
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-025"></span>**Public space in security societies is characterized not by a shortage of speech but by its overabundance: the inclusion of governed populations in a common semiotic space through surveys, elections, marketing, and media produces consensus and conformism, making singular expression impossible without first interrupting and re-singularizing shared communication.**
    - The intermittents' demand for 'the right to blunder' during media appearances—refusing the media's codes, temporalities, and syntactical constraints—revealed the conditions of singular speech by refusing to be severed from their collective assemblage.
    - Elections, marketing, and advertising mutually reinforce each other as apparatuses that presuppose prior consensus on problems, limiting 'free expression' to choosing between alternatives already codified by others.

## "Scum" and the Critique of Performatives

Through a critique of post-operaist and feminist appropriations of Austin's performative theory—and through analysis of Sarkozy's 2005 'scum' utterance and suburban riots—this chapter argues that enunciation is fundamentally dialogic, strategic, and event-generating rather than performative, and that the non-discursive existential self-affirmation that produces political rupture (parrhesia) is radically incommensurable with the institutionalized distribution of roles that performatives presuppose.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-026"></span>**Post-operaist theory's 'absolute performative' (Virno) and Butler's appropriation of performatives both retain only half of Austin's theory—the enunciation accomplishes an action—while abandoning the essential second condition: that performatives instantiate social obligations through conventional distribution of speaker/listener roles, making 'I speak' a non-performative that institutes no obligation.**
    - Austin himself abandoned the performative/constative distinction, arguing that all utterances accomplish social acts through illocutionary force—a radicalization that linguistics largely ignored because it put in question the autonomy of language.
    - Butler seeks the 'political promise of emancipation' in the performative's repetition and citation, but Foucault showed that the performative represents the 'counterexample' to political rupture: its conditions and effects are 'codified in advance,' producing no risk or indetermination.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-027"></span>**Bakhtin's theory of enunciation establishes a difference in kind between linguistic/grammatical elements (reproducible) and the existential/dialogic elements (non-reproducible) that actually complete an utterance: every speech act requires an 'ethical-political element' inaccessible to linguistic categorization, making the enunciation irreducible to any structural or performative formalization.**
    - The dialogic relation presupposes language but does not exist within the system of language: it belongs neither to a logical nor linguistic nor psychological system but constitutes a 'sphere of being' defined by relations of sense between utterances.
    - Bakhtin identifies five constituents of enunciation, of which only the first three (phonics, referential meaning, verbal connections) are reproducible; the fourth (intonation/axiological directedness) and fifth (feeling of creative speech activity, including gesture and facial expression) are absolutely singular and non-reproducible.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-028"></span>**Sarkozy's utterance 'You are scum' was not a performative constituting suburban youth as dominated but a strategic, dialogic speech act that opened an indeterminate field of response-reactions—and the suburban riots of 2005 demonstrated that existential affirmation (the refusal of the assignation) preceded and exceeded any linguistic or performative logic.**
    - Far from neutralizing agency, the enunciation activated it on a scale unimaginable prior to its utterance; Rosa Parks's refusal to give up her seat is similarly not a performative but 'an act of resistance, of self-positioning, of affirmation, showing itself in a gesture of refusal without speech.'
    - Sarkozy's utterance belongs to a long chain of utterances (from the Communist Party's 'Let's make French!' to the National Front's 'The French first!' to the Socialist Party's 'We cannot be a home for the world's poor') that it responds to, relies on, and anticipates responses from.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-029"></span>**Speech genres—relatively stable types of utterances learned through concrete social interaction rather than grammar—are the actual medium through which subjectivation and language acquisition occur, and their degree of standardization or creative freedom depends on micro- and macro-political relations (hierarchy, trust, sympathy) rather than on linguistic structure or performative conventions.**
    - Standard speech genres (commands, orders, factual questions) represent 'maximally standard' forms in which 'the creative aspect is almost completely lacking'; creativity requires space-times of trust and 'unofficial' equality that bypass the framework of social hierarchy.
    - What 'precedes and exceeds' the subject in language is not a transcendent structure (Butler) or signifier chain (Lacan) but chains of concrete utterances 'transfigured in what is created' by each new singular speech act.

## The Discursive and the Existential in the Production of Subjectivity

Guattari's concept of 'existential pragmatics'—a self-referential, self-productive logic of existence that is radically dissymmetrical with discursive logic—establishes that subjective mutation necessarily traverses a non-discursive, asignifying focal point before it can proliferate through language and narrative, and that the aesthetic paradigm rather than cognitive or linguistic models is the appropriate framework for political experimentation with subjectivation.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-030"></span>**The existential dimension of enunciation—the self-affectation, self-positioning, and apprehension of the world that precede and exceed discursive competence—constitutes the 'active core' of all linguistic and semiotic creativity and the condition of possibility for any genuine political rupture with dominant significations.**
    - "'When a love machine or fear machine is activated, it is not due to the effect of discursive, cognitive, or deductive statements. It happens immediately. And this machine gradually develops different means of expression.'" —Felix Guattari
    - Speech has a dual function: to signify and communicate, but also to 'produce assemblages of enunciation able to capture, territorialize, and deploy the singularities of a focal point of existential subjectivation.'
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-031"></span>**Discursive logic and existential logic are dissymmetrical in four key respects: their referents (extrinsic vs. self-referential), their temporality (linear vs. circular/intensive), their mode of repetition (producing discourse vs. producing changes in subjective state), and their agents (subjects and objects vs. threshold-crossing intensities and mutant subjectivities).**
    - In existential logic, 'the singular element is itself its own reference and generates its own reference, it secretes its world of reference'—existence is processual, irreversible, and event-generating rather than reversible, structural, and ahistorical.
    - The 'refrain'—empty speech whose significance lies in its repetition rather than semantic content—gives consistency to the relation to the self: Leninist or Christie refrains 'initiate a kind of universe, a framework, a stage corresponding to a production of subjectivity at the level of the collective.'
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-032"></span>**The 'aesthetic paradigm' names the only appropriate methodology for articulating the discursive and existential dimensions of subjectivation: neither scientific-cognitive mapping nor bi-univocal translation but 'topical art'—cartographies that localize and give access to existential territories without claiming to translate existence into discourse.**
    - Theological debates at the dawn of Christianity and Bolshevik debates around 1905 functioned not as searches for true statements but as 'mythico-conceptual' cartographies capable of 'seizing subjectivity, of making it pass thresholds, of initiating a process.'
    - The act 'secretes its own reference' and constitutes 'a kind of cogito'—a non-representational, machinic cogito that opens optional matter and possible futures rather than reproducing already coded possibilities.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-033"></span>**Cognitive capitalism's reduction of production to knowledge-by-knowledge is tautological and fails to explain innovation or creativity because 'out of the mere processing of linguistic, cognitive, economic flows new subjectivity cannot emerge'; moreover, its practical implementation has constituted a program of 'anti-production'—standardizing and homogenizing knowledge, art, and culture through privatization, competition, and profitability.**
    - The cognitive divestments enacted across education, research, culture, and art represent operations of power that produce both economic impoverishment (precarity, miserable wages for the 'cognitive proletariat') and conformism: 'Not only lack amid overabundance, but stupidity in the midst of knowledge and science.'
    - Only a rupture with the dominant mode of subjectivation can secrete existential crystallizations productive of new references, which in politics manifests as the strike, revolt, or riot that 'suspend time for a brief moment and create other possibilities.'

## Enunciation and Politics: A Parallel Reading of Democracy: Foucault and Ranciere

Comparing Ranciere's and Foucault's readings of Greek democracy reveals two incompatible models of political subjectivation: where Ranciere grounds politics in the egalitarian logic of language and sees political action as demonstrating equality through argumentation and interlocution, Foucault's analysis of parrhesia and the Cynics shows that political enunciation requires existential self-affirmation and ethical differentiation that exceed any egalitarian framework, pointing toward a politics of 'an other life and an other world' irreducible to dialectical recognition.

* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-034"></span>**Foucault's analysis of parrhesia demonstrates that effective political enunciation requires not only politeia (constitutional equality) and isegoria (the statutory right to speak) but dunasteia—the actual force and power of self-affirmation that introduces ethical differentiation into equality and whose effects cannot be codified in advance.**
    - Unlike performatives whose 'conditions and effects are codified,' parrhesia 'opens up an unspecified risk,' a field of dangers and indetermination, and has a retroactive effect on the enunciating subject's mode of being—it binds the speaker to his truth.
    - Democratic consensus in Western democracies has effectively neutralized parrhesia by canceling the risk of truth-telling; the enemies of democracy identified a genuine problem—competition and conflict among equals threatening to degenerate into demagogy—that equality as sole political principle cannot resolve.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-035"></span>**Ranciere's model of politics as demonstration of equality through the logos—showing that those with no part speak a common language and are therefore equal beings of reason—produces a dialectical politics of recognition whose practical correlate is the reformist social democracy of the welfare state, and which is constitutionally incapable of including new political subjects beyond the State, unions, and business associations.**
    - For Ranciere, politics creates a 'theatrical stage' on which the gap between the rule of equality and the deed of inequality is dramatized through argumentation and interlocution—an agonism that 'resembles dialectical recognition' and aims at inclusion.
    - The 2005 suburban riots were declared non-political by Ranciere because they 'did not constitute a stage of interlocution recognizing the enemy as belonging to the same community as oneself'—a judgment that reveals the normative limits of his model.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-036"></span>**The Cynics provide Foucault's alternative model: rather than seeking inclusion and recognition, they practice 'an other life for an other world' through public self-exposure, bodily enunciation, and shameless dramatization of animal existence, which constitutes not a refusal of politics but its radicalization by binding ethics (bios) inseparably to truth and politics.**
    - The Cynics' motto 'deface the coinage' refers simultaneously to debasing money (nomisma) and debasing the law (nomos)—they do not ask for recognition but scrutinize institutions and ways of life through self-experimentation whose 'short path to virtue' is silent practice rather than logos.
    - By attacking animality as the point of differentiation between human and non-human, the Cynics attack the foundations of Western philosophy—including its descendant in Ranciere's claim that political equality is grounded in the universal possession of language.
* <span id="signs-and-machines-capitalism-and-the-production-o-037"></span>**Guattari's analytical militantism, exemplified in the GIP model Foucault co-founded, represents the organizational form adequate to a politics articulating equality and ethical differentiation: continuous work on both the utterances produced and the modes of production of the subject of enunciation, since the political act of intervening is inseparable from constituting the subject who intervenes.**
    - The three questions that cannot be separated in political subjectivation are aletheia (truth), politeia (political structures), and ethos (the formation of the ethical subject)—posing any one without the others produces either a 'pure politics' that never attains consistency or an ethics without transformation.
    - Under contemporary conditions of capitalist subjection and enslavement, the political question is 'How do we invent and practice both equality and ethical differentiation (singularization) while breaking with machinic enslavements and social subjections?'
