# Market Wizards: The Next Generation

## Metadata
- Thesis: Exceptional trading success in the next generation of Market Wizards comes from no single strategy, but from matching a real edge to personality, enforcing risk control, and adapting as markets change. The book’s interviews argue that survival, disciplined sizing, obsessive study, and patience for the right environment matter more than prediction or complexity.
- Topics: trading psychology, risk management, market inefficiency, position sizing, strategy adaptation
- Categories: business & economics, biography & autobiography, psychology
- Author: Jack D. Schwager and George F. Coyle
- Year: 2025
- Mode: quick

## Kristjan Kullamägi

Kullamägi’s rise from repeated small-account blowups to a nine-figure equity peak shows how simple, well-researched setups can produce extraordinary results when paired with strict risk limits and the right market environment. His worst period came not from lacking an edge, but from violating his own regime filter after a euphoric winning streak.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-001"></span>**Kullamägi’s trading obsession began when a security-guard job at the Nasdaq Stockholm building made markets feel like a path out of ordinary work, leading him to quit a biomedical degree only months before completion.**
    - He worked night and weekend security shifts, using the quiet hours to read trading books and study charts after each blown-up account.
    - His first live account was about $5,000, saved from security-guard wages, and he lost 90% within two months.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-002"></span>**His early failures became useful only because he diagnosed them mechanically: all-in position sizing, no risk control, high commissions, and undisciplined stops were progressively removed from the process.**
    - He blew up three times before becoming profitable; each account lasted longer than the prior one, which he treated as evidence of improvement.
    - Switching to a US broker reduced commissions by roughly 90%, making high-frequency intraday trading more viable from Sweden.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-003"></span>**Kullamägi eventually narrowed his trading to three recurring setups: episodic pivots, momentum breakouts, and parabolic shorts, each with defined triggers, stops, and favorable market conditions.**
    - He credits Pradeep Bonde of Stockbee for the episodic pivot and momentum burst concepts rather than claiming originality.
    - He also recommends William O’Neill’s How to Make Money in Stocks for historical examples of winning stock patterns.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-004"></span>**The episodic pivot setup seeks a neglected stock that gaps sharply higher on major news and massive volume because the market is being forced to reassess the company.**
    - The required ingredients are a significant news catalyst, a large gap up, and an explosive volume increase, often 10 times normal volume.
    - Tempus AI in January 2025 and Peloton in August 2024 are presented as examples of stocks that re-rated after major catalysts.
    - LAES in December 2024 showed how a sector theme, quantum computing, could produce both an episodic pivot and later a parabolic short.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-005"></span>**His breakout strategy buys leading stocks emerging from short consolidations because explosive movers often advance in stair-step bursts rather than smooth trends.**
    - He scans for the top 1% or 2% of stocks over one-, three-, and six-month periods, then looks for a one- to three-week consolidation on reduced volume.
    - The stop is normally the day’s low, but he avoids the trade if that stop is more than one average daily true range away because the reward-risk becomes skewed.
    - PTON is used as both an episodic pivot and later breakout example after it formed a consolidation and broke higher.
* <span class="pull-quote">I used to live to trade, and now I trade to live.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-006"></span>**Two of Kullamägi’s three main setups depend heavily on the broad market trend, so ignoring his 10-day and 20-day moving-average filter in 2022 turned a strong methodology into a large drawdown.**
    - He treats both moving averages rising as a favorable uptrend and a 10-day cross below the 20-day as a caution signal.
    - From a November 2021 equity peak of about $105 million to a mid-2022 low, he lost roughly $60 million.
    - He estimates that following the 10/20-day rule would have avoided about 90% of the 2022 losses.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-007"></span>**Extending holding periods from day trades to swing trades and then to moving-average trailing exits increased his profit potential, but it also made portfolio-level exposure and market tops more dangerous.**
    - Day trading kept him close to stops but was stressful and required constant new ideas; swing trading let winners work with less screen time.
    - He takes partial profits after the first three to five days, then trails the remainder with a 10-day or 20-day moving average.
    - At the 2021 peak he was leveraged long, so positions stopping out together caused a large equity retracement even without one catastrophic trade.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-008"></span>**Kullamägi’s later underperformance after 2021 came from losing focus rather than losing edge; travel, boating, and reduced chart review made him miss obvious opportunities such as SMCI in January 2024.**
    - He saw SMCI gap more than 10% on massive volume after a bullish preliminary earnings report, but passed because his attention was elsewhere.
    - He had previously reviewed saved screenshots of top episodic pivots, breakouts, and parabolic shorts every month to refresh conviction, but stopped doing so.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-009"></span>**His parabolic short method is powerful but psychologically demanding because the trader must wait for day three or four of an extreme move and accept multiple small losses before the collapse.**
    - He avoids shorting day one regardless of the percentage move because stocks can rise 1,000% in a single day.
    - In LAES, he was early on day three, stopped out, then re-shorted day five near $10 and caught a move toward $5.
    - A fat-finger short in Kodak during the 2020 White House pharmaceutical announcement cost him about $1.5 million after repeated trading halts.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-010"></span>**The chapter’s practical lesson is that a trader can borrow setups from others, but conviction must come from personally verifying hundreds or thousands of examples and matching the method to temperament.**
    - Kullamägi says he usually risks 0.5% or less of account equity per trade and at most about 1%.
    - His win rate is only about 25% to 30%, but the average win is much larger than the average loss.
    - Belief in oneself is useful only when grounded in research, probability, position sizing, and stop discipline.

## Lance Breitstein

Breitstein’s career shows that extreme short-term trading can have real edge when a trader combines speed, nuanced context, pattern recognition, and relentless process improvement. His later shift to larger, longer-held trades shows that the highest-probability game is not always the highest-upside game once capital and life priorities change.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-011"></span>**Breitstein chose a $26,000 Trillium prop-trading job over a Bank of Montreal offer above $100,000 because he optimized for long-term upside and a meritocratic trading arena rather than immediate salary.**
    - He used Wall Street Oasis contacts to verify that Trillium had real traders making money before rejecting the safer bank path.
    - He believed sacrificing roughly $75,000 per year for a couple of years was rational if it opened a path to seven-figure annual earnings.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-012"></span>**Family financial stress gave Breitstein a powerful reason to outwork others, while his near-perfect academic record at Indiana University proved he could convert urgency into disciplined effort.**
    - He graduated third in a class of a couple thousand with only one A minus in four years of the honors program.
    - He viewed poverty avoidance and financial security as a personal why that made late nights, low pay, and social sacrifice tolerable.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-013"></span>**His crucial early advantage was engineering proximity to Trillium’s top trader in Princeton, where mentorship, low living costs, and social isolation reinforced an obsessive learning routine.**
    - He moved to the Princeton satellite office because no other new hire wanted to leave New York City, but it placed him beside the firm’s best trader.
    - The mentor taught him nuanced chart pattern analysis rather than simple textbook technical signals.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-014"></span>**Breitstein turned early underperformance into skill by recording trading sessions, reviewing decisive clips on Sundays, and practicing fast decisions at altered playback speeds.**
    - He lost money for 11 consecutive months and was still only slightly above breakeven after about 18 months.
    - He slowed clips to analyze missed cues and replayed them at 1.5x or 2x speed so real-time trading felt slower.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-015"></span>**His argument for day-trading edge is that the shortest time frames contain micro-inefficiencies, but only traders with speed, hotkey execution, and contextual judgment can exploit them.**
    - He configured hotkeys to buy specific share sizes at controlled limits above the offer, avoiding uncontrolled market orders.
    - Algorithms beat humans on simple headlines, but humans can still outperform when the interpretation depends on context, prior expectations, sentiment, or whether news is actually new.
    - He optimized screen layout and even ticker-entry keystrokes to shave fractions of a second from reaction time.
* <span class="pull-quote">Bad trades don’t kill people; bad sizing kills people.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-016"></span>**Breitstein’s technical patterns are not generic chart formations; they require multiple confirming nuances such as news, volume, volatility, trend context, and alignment across intraday and daily time frames.**
    - His Tesla 2013 exhaustion-gap short was a breakthrough trade because the same setup made far more money for his mentor, proving that size was the main gap.
    - He describes bouncy ball shorts, inverse bouncy ball breakouts, capitulation buys, and breaking-news trades as recurring pattern families.
    - Bitcoin’s post-2024 election breakout combined an all-time-high breakout, pro-crypto political catalyst, and reflexive buying interest.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-017"></span>**Risk control in Breitstein’s trading is mostly achieved through tight predetermined stops, with position size reduced when the setup is so extreme that no precise stop is used.**
    - For intraday trades he often trails below or above the prior two-minute bar; for multi-day trades he may use the prior day’s low or high.
    - In Allergan in 2014, after-hours news about Bill Ackman caused a fat-tail loss around $100,000 against a $4,000 loss limit.
    - He allows small no-stop positions only in rare capitulation situations and sizes them far smaller.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-018"></span>**His worst Trillium loss in Avis came from goal-driven oversizing, demonstrating that monetary targets can distort objective expected-value sizing even when the setup itself is valid.**
    - He lost more than $2 million in less than an hour on Avis in November 2021 while trying to reach a $20 million annual profit goal.
    - The exit was correct because Avis later ran to $550 that day, but the initial position was too large for the objective setup quality.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-019"></span>**Leaving Trillium forced Breitstein to give up ultra-fast edge, but applying the same pattern logic to larger multi-day trades produced more money with far less trading activity.**
    - At Trillium he generated $46 million over 10 years; in his personal account he earned $71 million over five years on $5 million of net initial deposits.
    - His August 5, 2024 Nikkei futures capitulation buy became the biggest trade of his career, making about $10 million while risking about $2 million pre-tax.
    - He says he now trades about 1% as often as before while making more because the longer game scales better.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-020"></span>**Breitstein sells option premium only after volatility has already exploded, often at strike prices where he would want to own or short the underlying stock anyway.**
    - Selling puts after a panic can replace a lower limit buy order: if exercised, the premium improves the effective entry; if not, he keeps the premium.
    - He is much more cautious selling calls because upside risk is unbounded and position sizing becomes even more critical.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-021"></span>**His post-Trillium conflict is that trading remains financially optimal but physically and psychologically costly, so he seeks a life centered more on health, teaching, and philanthropy.**
    - A sleep study told him his sleep results were among the worst the specialists had seen outside active military cases.
    - He spent about 15 months and $700,000 building a trading course whose net profits he says will go to charity.
    - Atomic Habits shaped his language of process, identity, and incremental improvement.

## Simon Russo

Russo’s story is both a spectacular proof of trading edge and a warning that even a deeply skilled trader can approach ruin when selection rules and risk controls are overridden. His career’s causal pattern is stark: obsessive research, calm focus, and adaptive methods created enormous gains, while every catastrophic loss began as a small deviation from process.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-022"></span>**Russo’s anonymous profile begins with a music prodigy’s temperament: total devotion to craft, disdain for good-enough work, and a willingness to abandon Thornton for trading after repeated failures.**
    - He studied music intensely from age 12, later attending the USC Thornton School of Music for composition and arranging before dropping out.
    - Music and trading appealed to him for the same reason: each offered a never-ending path of self-discovery, mastery, and personal responsibility.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-023"></span>**His early trading repeatedly turned profitable ideas into blowups because he combined negative-asymmetric shorts with oversized positions, no stops, and hope-based exits.**
    - He lost his first $3,000 account, then repeatedly blew up accounts funded by his father, including one that grew from $50,000 to $550,000 before vanishing.
    - A late-day biotech scalp intended to make $2,000 became an account-ending short when he added to the loser and held overnight.
    - AQXP sent his account negative by about $10,000 before a lucky long trade brought him back near zero.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-024"></span>**Russo’s eventual edge in small-cap shorts came from selecting only stocks whose rallies were driven by meaningless news and whose filings revealed likely dilution or identifiable selling pressure.**
    - He analyzed cash burn, debt, warrants, convertible securities, and SEC filings to infer whether the company needed capital on unfavorable terms.
    - A stock gapping from $2 to $5 while holders can convert debt at $3 creates an obvious incentive for those holders to sell into the rally.
    - He avoided shorts when news was genuinely material, such as a company moving from losses to profits or a biotech reporting credible clinical success.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-025"></span>**The 2016 MGT short marked Russo’s transition from talented but self-destructive trader to process-driven professional because he took a small loss first, reentered, pyramided only after confirmation, and controlled risk.**
    - He first shorted too early and accepted the stop, then re-shorted near the top when momentum failed and added as the stock broke down.
    - The MGT trade made about $30,000, roughly matching his entire year-to-date profit at that point.
    - He moved the stop down as the trade worked, allowing additional size without increasing initial risk.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-026"></span>**Russo’s pyramiding method improved his average win/loss ratio because each add was funded by a lower stop, keeping dollar risk roughly constant while increasing exposure to a confirmed move.**
    - After a short works, he waits for price action that justifies lowering the stop, then uses the freed risk budget to add to the position.
    - The technique works for his selected stocks because once they break, they often continue; it is not a universal endorsement of pyramiding.
* <span class="pull-quote">The smallest mistakes can have large consequences.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-027"></span>**His move from pure short seller to trading broken shorts on the long side required abandoning identity and treating market behavior as more important than ego.**
    - A broken short is a stock that should fail based on news and fundamentals but instead trends upward, traps shorts, consolidates, and breaks to new highs.
    - VRPX in August 2021 became a long after bullish COVID-related news, a small float, clean capital structure, and a failed breakdown that reversed to new highs.
    - The same bearish stock may later become a short again once the long squeeze turns parabolic and exhausts.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-028"></span>**Scaling required moving from small caps into mid- and large-cap momentum names, where the research edge is thinner but liquidity allows trades large enough to matter.**
    - Small caps offered higher-probability shorts through SEC filings and dilution analysis, but capacity was limited; large caps relied more on news and price action.
    - GameStop, AMC, DWAC, Tesla, and later broad-market capitulation trades became meaningful because they could absorb much larger size.
    - He cites GameStop trades of roughly $10 million and $7 million, plus an AMC trade around $10 million.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-029"></span>**The SPY and Carvana losses show that Russo’s worst drawdowns came when he traded outside his selection criteria and let stubbornness replace risk budgeting.**
    - The short SPY trade cost about $50 million after he entered a non-strategy mean-reversion short while already up about $50 million for the year.
    - The Carvana short also cost about $50 million; it began as attachment to a ticker rather than a valid setup and was exited only after a bullish earnings gap.
    - Carvana later rose far enough that holding the short could have wiped out his account despite the realized loss already being enormous.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-030"></span>**Russo’s postscript drawdown made explicit that willpower was an inadequate risk-control system, so he replaced mental stops with automated liquidation tiers and panic overrides.**
    - A sub-A+ trade escalated from intended 0.5% risk to a peak unrealized drawdown near $120 million after he added repeatedly to a loser.
    - His new system liquidates half a position if unrealized loss exceeds 1.2 times the trade’s risk tier and the rest at 1.5 times risk.
    - A panic override liquidates the full position and blocks new trades in the symbol if a gap blows past 3 times tier risk.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-031"></span>**After making enough money to question trading’s purpose, Russo reduced market involvement, built an analyst-driven alert system, and redirected much of his energy toward anonymous philanthropy.**
    - The account that began with $40,000 in late 2015 generated over $500 million in cumulative profits over roughly a decade.
    - He says that over one recent 14-month period he made well over $200 million while spending far less time on markets.
    - The Billionaire Who Wasn’t influenced his thinking about anonymous giving and using wealth beyond self-indulgence.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-032"></span>**Russo rejects the idea that hard work alone can make anyone a Market Wizard because his own success depended on rare traits: calm under stress, extreme focus, love of research, and a compatible environment.**
    - He frames trading as probabilities under randomness and warns that tail risk is always near, even for skilled traders.
    - He recommends Nassim Taleb’s The Black Swan and Fooled by Randomness as antidotes to overconfidence in trading outcomes.
    - His advice is to borrow elements from other traders, not copy their entire methodology, because edge must fit the trader’s own traits.

## Lukas Fröhlich

Fröhlich’s chapter traces a rare metamorphosis from hyper-leveraged teenage speculator to small-cap short seller, euphoric mid-cap long trader, and then contrarian long-term investor. The unifying logic is adaptation: he repeatedly changes strategy as capital size, market regime, and opportunity set change, while increasingly grounding risk in research, stops, and environment selection.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-033"></span>**Fröhlich’s verified 2020 return was so extreme that the authors obtained independent accounting review, which found his public claim understated the result rather than exaggerated it.**
    - The independent agreed-upon procedure calculated his 2020 return at 1,132,045% using Modified Dietz methodology, versus his public 892,000% claim.
    - Because profits were withdrawn during the year, the account balance itself grew by 37,127% rather than by the full return percentage.
    - From January 2020 through December 2025, his account compounded at an average annual rate of 309.3% with a 35.5% maximum drawdown.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-034"></span>**His original motivation was not market fascination but freedom from school and authority after a difficult childhood transition from France to Germany.**
    - He moved from a French-speaking school to a German-speaking school without knowing German, leaving him socially isolated and academically strained.
    - Searching online for the fastest way to make money led him to trading, which he imagined could help him leave school early and avoid having a boss.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-035"></span>**His teenage trading failures were driven by the classic beginner combination of volatility, leverage, no method, and reluctance to cut losses.**
    - He started trading at 13 with about $500 saved from gifts and later sold possessions, including his bicycle, to fund new attempts.
    - A 100-to-1 leveraged short DAX CFD that rose 2% left his account negative by about 200% of equity.
    - He stopped repeated blowups only after imposing maximum potential loss per stock and maximum daily portfolio loss rules.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-036"></span>**The small-cap short strategy worked because Fröhlich combined SEC-filing research on distressed issuers with technical patterns in gap-up stocks, rather than blindly shorting every big mover.**
    - He learned from Tim Grittani the value of quantifying patterns and doing deep research on small-cap shorts.
    - He looked for companies with working-capital problems that were likely to raise capital through discounted offerings, creating incentives for promotional price spikes and later selling pressure.
    - He collaborated with Miah Lee on research and backtesting before finding a broker that could source borrowable shares after high school.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-037"></span>**The SONN trade illustrates his textbook small-cap short: a cash-poor company with warrants and convertibles gaps on volume, then collapses as potential supply hits the market.**
    - On April 14, 2020, SONN appeared on his scanner after a huge premarket gap and massive volume.
    - He inferred that holders of warrants and convertibles would convert and sell into the elevated price, adding supply and pressuring the stock lower.
    - He says the full fundamental and technical review took about 20 minutes once the process was established.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-038"></span>**Fröhlich controlled small-cap short risk through mental stops, small initial entries, and pyramiding only after weakness because halted stocks can reopen hundreds of percent higher.**
    - Stops could be new daily highs, percentage thresholds, standard-deviation moves above the open, or time stops tied to expected intraday pattern.
    - He typically entered with about half intended size and added only if the stock began behaving as expected.
    - He had been trapped short in halted stocks multiple times and exited as soon as trading reopened.
* <span class="pull-quote">As soon as something becomes too easy or a consensus trade, the game changes.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-039"></span>**His 2020 shift to mid-cap breakouts exploited a specific euphoric bull-market regime created by quantitative easing, rate cuts, stimulus checks, retail participation, and social-media themes.**
    - He targeted $1 billion to $5 billion market-cap stocks with smaller floats, a catalyst, a 40% to 100% price validation move, and heavy volume.
    - Electric vehicle stocks in 2020 are cited as a hyped theme where fundamentals mattered less than story and social-media attention.
    - He also needed a more scalable strategy because his account had outgrown small-cap short capacity.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-040"></span>**The mid-cap breakout process depended on catalyst, consolidation, and controlled aggression: he wanted institutional accumulation before breakout, then used independent add-on setups to pyramid.**
    - He preferred stocks consolidating in the upper half of the post-catalyst range, often near the 20-day exponential moving average.
    - Additions had to qualify as attractive standalone setups; if a pyramid addition failed quickly, he exited the whole trade.
    - GRWG in late 2020 and early 2021 combined cannabis-sector attention, strong earnings catalysts, EMA pullbacks, pyramiding, and a hysteria-based exit.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-041"></span>**Fröhlich stopped using mid-cap breakouts after mid-2021 because the strategy’s edge depended on a strong bull market and became dangerous when that regime ended.**
    - The same strategy that outperformed in the seventh to ninth inning of a bull market became disastrous in the subsequent bear-market environment.
    - He argues most traders fail with such methods because they cannot sit out for years when the market is unsuitable.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-042"></span>**His current strategy blends contrarian value and trend-following by seeking hated stocks with strong businesses, low valuations, and catalysts the market is not pricing.**
    - Alibaba in 2024 combined record-low Chinese valuations, a P/E near 10, earnings growth, cloud potential, buybacks, and an anticipated Chinese stimulus response.
    - Centene in summer 2025 was bought after healthcare funding fears because he expected politicians to protect ACA subsidies and reverse Medicare cuts.
    - He uses reclaiming or holding the 200-day moving average as a key signal before building a substantial position.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-043"></span>**Fröhlich’s sizing philosophy is aggressive but bounded: he believes experienced traders often undersize, yet he uses 5% average risk for peace of mind and learns from drawdowns.**
    - He says single-theme trade risk around 5% to 10% of account equity sounds right, though he usually sticks near 5%.
    - The chapter explains the Kelly criterion and notes Ed Thorp’s preference for half Kelly under most circumstances.
    - In 2022 he suffered about a 40% drawdown after buying too early, sizing too large, and adding to losing positions.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-044"></span>**The chapter frames Fröhlich as an unusual trader because he has repeatedly changed time frames and market direction, but the common thread is searching for inefficient, volatile opportunity sets that match account size and regime.**
    - If starting from zero in 2025, he says he would probably focus on crypto because it resembles small caps 15 years earlier in inefficiency and volatility.
    - He still occasionally trades mean-reversion opportunities to satisfy his urge for activity without disrupting core long-term positions.
    - Coyle warns most traders should not attempt such radical strategy switching because most successful traders hone one or two methods that fit temperament.

## Phil Goedeker

Goedeker built wealth through two supposedly dangerous strategies, short selling and option premium selling, because his instinctive rule was to exit immediately when trades did not work. The chapter’s logic is that negative-asymmetric strategies can be survivable only when a trader is unusually skilled at taking small losses, sizing A+ trades differently, and hedging before disaster compounds.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-045"></span>**Goedeker’s market obsession began as a teenager at Lake of the Ozarks, where wealth looked like freedom from his father’s one-week-vacation work life.**
    - After seeing mansions and large boats at about age 15, he began reading the Sunday business section of the St. Louis Post-Dispatch and library stock-market books.
    - Money was the initial motive, but the market soon became a fascinating game he loved studying.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-046"></span>**His first two trading attempts failed because he bought short-term momentum without edge and then abandoned positions almost immediately when they failed to rise.**
    - He lost two separate $3,000 summer savings accounts during college.
    - He would buy stocks after several up days, sell quickly on a down day, and bleed through commissions and random entries.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-047"></span>**Discovering short selling transformed his impatience into an advantage because overextended small caps could collapse quickly if he waited for the rally to stall.**
    - His first major short, Host America, was halted after misleading Walmart-related publicity and reopened near $5 after trading around $15-$16.
    - He grew $5,000 to over $1 million in about nine months during his senior year of college.
    - He was often 200% short on margin but exited immediately when a stock made a new high against him.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-048"></span>**A 50% drawdown after first reaching $1 million taught Goedeker to bank profits, reset capital, and stop experimenting with strategies that did not fit him.**
    - After reaching about $1,007,000, his account fell to $500,000; he wired $400,000 to the bank and restarted with $100,000.
    - His only losing year was 2009, down about $30,000, after experimenting with areas such as triple-leveraged ETFs.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-049"></span>**Analyzing his 2010 trades led him to the crucial sizing insight: only a small minority of A+ parabolic short setups made the money, while oversized mediocre trades caused avoidable losses.**
    - Out of about 500 tickers, only around 15 produced big gains of roughly $75,000 to $100,000, while many other trades were noise.
    - He increased size moderately on A+ trades and cut size on other trades by roughly 90%, leading to his first $1 million-plus profit year.
* <span class="pull-quote">You can only make what the market provides.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-050"></span>**Goedeker’s A+ short setup is not selling into strength, but shorting a trashy small-cap only after the parabolic rally breaks and then adding only when the trade is already working.**
    - If he shorted at $10 and the stock rose to $10.50 or $12, he covered rather than averaged up against the loss.
    - Other traders in chat rooms added to losing shorts at higher prices; Goedeker treated that as evidence their entries were wrong.
    - He was willing to take multiple paper-cut losses on the same idea and reenter only after another breakdown.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-051"></span>**His obsessive chart study was self-taught and archival, giving him pattern memory before trading education and online mentorship became widely available.**
    - He printed charts of stocks with 600%, 800%, or 1,000% moves and kept binders showing the entire rise and later decline.
    - Current patterns could be matched against past chart examples to judge how similar moves had evolved.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-052"></span>**Goedeker shifted toward selling option premium because short-selling small caps demanded constant screen time, while options offered a lower-stress way to monetize risk control and account size.**
    - In 2016, another trader agreed to teach him options in exchange for lessons in Goedeker’s short-selling style.
    - He began with $200,000, quickly increased the option account to $1 million and then $2 million, and made about $1 million in his first option year.
    - He sells very deep out-of-the-money options, mostly puts, on stable mega-cap stocks such as Apple, Amazon, and Microsoft.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-053"></span>**His option-selling survival depends on assuming any impossible event can happen and reacting instantly with hedges or exits before losses accelerate.**
    - A $1 million loss selling GameStop calls taught him not to sell options on stocks capable of violent squeeze moves.
    - After the April 3, 2025 post-Liberation Day selloff open, he bought roughly 800 to 900 June SPY puts to hedge the entire option portfolio while down only about $100,000.
    - He keeps only about 10% of his total assets in the trading account because a 1987-style crash could still threaten the option account.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-054"></span>**Goedeker deliberately sets low annual performance goals because ambitious targets invite overtrading when the market is not supplying his kind of opportunity.**
    - He made over $10 million in 2020, his best year, when small caps were often gapping up 1,000% and failing late in the day.
    - A bull market creates the euphoric small-cap rallies he needs as a short seller; bear markets often lack those extreme overbought setups.
    - He says making $10 million, $5 million, or $2 million may not affect his happiness, but losing $5 million would bother him greatly.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-055"></span>**The chapter’s broader lesson is that consistency and adaptability are compatible when the trader preserves the core niche but adjusts to borrow costs, crowding, and changing intraday behavior.**
    - Rising borrow costs forced him toward shorter holding periods because holding small-cap shorts for days or weeks became uneconomical.
    - When small-cap gap-ups stopped rolling over after the first 45 minutes and began holding gains into the afternoon, he changed timing rather than abandoning the principle.
    - Schwager cautions that option selling deserves a strong warning because few traders have Goedeker’s reflexive risk control.

## Kelvin Chiu

Chiu’s chapter explains how a commodities trader evolved from fragile academic perfectionist to independent risk-taker by learning to structure asymmetric trades, process drawdowns, and size conviction. His arc turns on recognizing that maximizing return/risk can become too conservative, while maximizing P&L safely requires timing, conviction stops, emotional work, and controlled exposure to both convex and concave trades.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-056"></span>**Chiu’s perfect academic record in Hong Kong and Cambridge initially hurt his trading because it left him unprepared for a field where being wrong is routine.**
    - He was one of Hong Kong’s top 50 students by citywide exam results and later graduated from Cambridge with perfect grades.
    - Straight-A environments reward being right, while trading often rewards losing small repeatedly and letting a few large wins dominate.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-057"></span>**His Goldman Sachs years began with responsibility before readiness: he ran an agricultural options book at 23 but had no real trading method.**
    - He entered Goldman through a 2007 London internship and joined the agricultural commodities desk after the firm started building it out.
    - In 2010 and 2011 he lost just under $1 million each year while trying fundamentals, technicals, headlines, broker chatter, and other traders’ ideas.
    - Negative self-talk made losses feel like personal failure and impaired learning.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-058"></span>**Having his book taken away felt humiliating but became the apprenticeship that let Chiu learn fundamental commodity analysis without daily P&L fear.**
    - With reduced pressure, he could observe senior agricultural traders, study supply-demand balances, and compare current fundamentals with historical analogs.
    - Trading in the Zone helped him shift attention from outcomes to process.
    - He began tracking trades closely to diagnose what worked, what failed, and what needed improvement.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-059"></span>**Chiu found his first durable methodology in agricultural calendar spreads and relative-value trades because annual crops force supply-demand imbalances to resolve within a crop year.**
    - Calendar spreads buy one futures month and sell another in the same commodity; relative-value spreads buy one related commodity and sell another.
    - He preferred grains and oilseeds over multi-year-cycle commodities such as coffee or cocoa because the timing of balance-sheet adjustment was clearer.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-060"></span>**Vitol expanded Chiu’s sense of possible risk and reward, but the 2016 soybean meal spread loss taught him that concave trades can be psychologically and financially dangerous.**
    - At Vitol his best year made about $35 million, while some other traders were reportedly up 10 times that amount.
    - A short old-crop July meal versus long December meal spread moved from 14 to 45 against him before he exited around 38, losing about $10 million.
    - He calls trades with limited gain and potentially much larger loss concave trades.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-061"></span>**When Chiu went independent in 2018, he initially copied independent traders’ preference for convex trades with limited downside and large upside, prioritizing a smooth equity curve over maximum profits.**
    - He planned to save $10 million before going solo but started a few million short of that target, with about $1 million from immediate family.
    - In storable grain spreads, long nearby and short forward contracts near full carry can have limited downside and large upside if tightness develops.
    - He stopped adding to winning convex trades after going solo because he wanted lower stress and steadier returns than at Vitol.
* <span class="pull-quote">It is my process that defines me as a trader, not any single outcome.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-062"></span>**Missing the 2019 corn spread trade forced Chiu to reconsider his blanket ban on concave trades because some negative-asymmetric structures can still offer excellent expected value with timing and margin of safety.**
    - After heavy Midwest rains, USDA reports surprised the market by showing better acreage and yield than expected, making the elevated July/December 2020 corn spread attractive to short.
    - He did not take the trade because it was concave, but the missed profit revealed that he was maximizing Sortino ratio rather than total P&L.
    - He decided to reintroduce concave trades only when timing, end-game fair value, and position size made blowup probability effectively zero.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-063"></span>**His 2022 wheat spread trade after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine shows the mature concave approach: wait for forced buying to exhaust, sell weakness, add near expiry, and use conviction rather than a fixed price stop.**
    - After US wheat futures surged more than 50% on multiple limit-up days, he concluded global trade flows were still functioning and US wheat had priced itself out of export demand.
    - He sold May Chicago wheat versus July only after the spread weakened and expiration approached, because spreads converge toward fundamentally determined fair value near delivery.
    - He ultimately built a short position of about 11,000 spreads; a return to the high could have cost around 25%, though he now tries to cap high-conviction loss near 15%.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-064"></span>**CBT helped Chiu separate self-worth from P&L, making larger risk-taking emotionally possible without turning every drawdown into a crisis of identity.**
    - A 2021 drawdown above 25% exposed that he still reacted poorly to losses despite strong long-term results.
    - He reframed identity around growth, resilience, and courage rather than cumulative achievements.
    - The result was a belief that every trade either makes money or teaches, allowing process to dominate individual outcomes.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-065"></span>**Expanding beyond grains required Chiu to treat technical analysis as the risk-management layer for outright positions, while fundamentals supplied conviction and sizing.**
    - Fundamentals alone can justify adding to falling outright positions even when analysis is wrong or an exogenous shock has changed the game.
    - He cites COVID-19 and President Trump’s tariff announcement as unpredictable events that can overwhelm prior fundamental analysis.
    - He learned technical analysis from books, trend-following CTAs, and Peter Brandt’s research.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-066"></span>**Chiu views sizing as his main edge because hit rate and payoff ratio matter, but outsized profits come from aggressively sizing rare high-conviction situations.**
    - He describes good trading as long periods of inactivity punctuated by short, intense periods of heavy risk-taking.
    - A 2023 long July corn versus short September corn spread looked perfect fundamentally, but a weather-driven shift triggered signal fear that he ignored and lost about 13% of his account.
    - He treats irritation and bodily discomfort as signal fear, an intuitive warning that something in the trade has changed before conscious analysis fully explains it.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-067"></span>**As his account grew, Chiu began building a multi-strategy operation and looking for scalable opportunities such as Japanese small caps and uranium rather than relying only on non-scalable agricultural spreads.**
    - He hired five traders, preferring former independent traders, generalists, and people who use technical analysis for risk management.
    - He identifies Japanese small- and mid-cap equities as a generational opportunity because reforms, activism, and balance-sheet assets can unlock value.
    - He likes uranium because AI power demand, China and India demand, production issues, and Russian supply-chain control create supply-demand pressure.

## Jason Berry

Berry’s near-absurd consistency comes from combining reflexive loss-cutting with many small, researched, short-term edges that are constantly replaced as they decay. The chapter argues that career-length short-term trading is less about one enduring system than about continual innovation, daily review, scenario preparation, and a training culture that keeps traders in a beginner’s mindset.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-068"></span>**Berry’s track record is extraordinary because his trading account stayed small while monthly profits were steady, making percentage returns misleading but consistency unmistakable.**
    - During the past 14 years he had only five losing months, with the worst monthly loss, €28,073, below his average monthly return of €30,554.
    - His account allocation was only €100,000 through 2023 and €200,000 thereafter, so profits grew arithmetically rather than through compounding.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-069"></span>**Berry’s earliest trading success came from instinctively cutting losers while following a simple breakout system, even before he had trading theory or market knowledge.**
    - As a LIFFE floor clerk, he disliked the floor culture and moved into electronic trading after meeting prop traders who were setting up in Dublin.
    - When trading a price-band breakout system for European markets, he made money because he managed risk and did not become attached to losing positions.
    - He links his comfort with screens, fast response, and losing to video game experience.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-070"></span>**Strict risk limits at the Dublin office created the foundation for Berry’s control-first approach, which later became central to his trader-training philosophy.**
    - Early rules included not losing more than 10 ticks on any trade and not losing more than $300 on any day.
    - He traded mostly by gut feel at first, but the constraint of small losses let experience accumulate without ruin.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-071"></span>**Positive Equity’s mission reflects Berry’s belief that training should give traders enough runway and structure to build careers, not just churn through trainees.**
    - Berry formed Positive Equity with two partners after buying out the Dublin operation from Saxon.
    - He estimates he has trained well over 200 traders across Dublin and Croatia.
    - He wants traders to keep a trainee mindset even after success, because new edge must replace lost edge.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-072"></span>**Berry’s core trading style is very short-term momentum and spread trading during market-specific windows when he knows trend behavior is more reliable.**
    - He monitors many markets but focuses on specific products at specific times of day when those markets tend to trend better.
    - He teaches trainees to seek triple-F trades, meaning fantastic fill feeling: trades that move immediately in the intended direction.
    - He withholds current specific setups because colleagues are concerned about protecting the firm’s edge.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-073"></span>**Berry’s old edges show how short-term strategies decay as technology, competition, and market structure change, forcing constant replacement of alpha sources.**
    - Economic-news intermarket lag trades once let humans sell NASDAQ and buy S&P before one caught up, but speed-focused firms arbitraged much of it away.
    - Nokia once led the Eurostoxx 50, enabling Nokia/FESX relative trades until Nokia’s market importance collapsed after Apple changed mobile phones.
    - A VIX time-of-day order-flow trade produced about half his monthly P&L for four years before disappearing when the Ukraine war began in 2022.
* <span class="pull-quote">No strategy lasts forever.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-074"></span>**Scenario planning lets Berry react to news immediately because he has already mapped the relative impact on markets before the event occurs.**
    - He considers how 50%, 20%, or 10% tariffs on the EU would affect US bonds, US equities, and European equities before any announcement hits.
    - He similarly plans responses to potential China-Taiwan conflict or an end to the Russia-Ukraine war.
    - Because many of his trades are spreads, he evaluates relative impact across markets rather than only directional impact.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-075"></span>**Daily and monthly review are central to Berry’s consistency because he records not only P&L but decisions, size, psychology, missed trades, and inefficient behavior.**
    - Each day’s summary covers trades seen, trades taken, results, size, holding time, exits, and emotional state.
    - He rereads the report the next morning and reviews month-end patterns to decide whether to cut losses faster, change size, or stop forcing trades.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-076"></span>**Berry’s smooth equity curve comes from numerous small trades with tightly controlled downside rather than reliance on occasional large wins.**
    - He cites a rare 2008 day when four central banks cut rates and he made 160 grand, but says the normal engine is grinding many low-risk moves.
    - He has very low tolerance for offside trades, so his best months are many times larger than his worst months.
    - Some strategies have limited capacity, and sharing them with other Positive Equity traders also constrains his own size.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-077"></span>**His three Cs framework teaches traders to regain control before consistency and only then confidence, making size increases conditional on P&L stability rather than ambition.**
    - Trainees first aim to stop the equity curve from falling, then produce consistent profits, then earn the confidence to size up, hold longer, and add to winners.
    - The simulation phase usually lasts nine to 18 months, and the full training program typically takes two to three years.
    - Live trading is treated as a privilege that can be lost if simulated edge does not carry over into real execution.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-078"></span>**Berry’s hiring philosophy favors people with competitive achievement, grit, and personality fit because trading skills can be taught but loss behavior and work ethic are harder to install.**
    - He says success rates improved from low levels when the firm stopped accepting too many maybe candidates and focused on clear A-players.
    - A trader Berry cut for failing to control losses later had a €245,000 losing day at a competitor, validating the risk assessment.
    - He argues traders do not age out; they effort out, because staying in tune requires continuing screen time and adaptation.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-079"></span>**The chapter’s final caution is that even a highly consistent trader pays for success with stress, odd hours, family disruption, and constant subordination to the market’s schedule.**
    - Berry notes that Putin attacked Ukraine in the middle of the night and Trump often posts market-moving messages near bedtime or waking hours.
    - He wonders whether trading success will help him live longer or whether years of stress will shorten his life.
    - Coyle connects Berry’s life to the Reminiscences of a Stock Operator idea that a speculator must stay on the job.

## Kenny Sharkness

Sharkness succeeds through a constantly changing portfolio of strategies because his personality fits multitasking, rapid adaptation, collaboration, and rigorous self-review. The chapter argues that frequent change is not necessarily chaos when each strategy is sized by expected value, filtered by market environment, and integrated into a disciplined process of journaling and learning from others.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-080"></span>**Sharkness entered trading through SMB’s paid training program because finance jobs and sales roles did not fit his personality, while trading resembled the competitive games he already loved.**
    - He paid about $6,000 for SMB’s training program and relied on parental support while living cheaply near New York City.
    - His background included poker, video games, chess, Scrabble, and board games, all of which made trading feel like another game.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-081"></span>**Mentorship from Gilberto Mendez taught Sharkness to blend multiple time frames, chart patterns, and the idea that stocks expend and regain energy.**
    - Mendez was SMB’s top trader at the time and taught breakouts, fades, risk management, and multi-timeframe context.
    - Even when trading tick charts, Sharkness checked 5-minute, hourly, and daily charts to avoid taking signals invalidated by broader context.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-082"></span>**His natural instinct was to exit trades that did not work immediately, producing many small losses but protecting him while he developed edge.**
    - He became profitable after about 10 months and made roughly $40,000 in his first profitable year.
    - Early breakout trades had very tight stops; he wanted to be in the money right away or exit quickly.
    - He won less than 50% of trades, but larger winners offset frequent small losses.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-083"></span>**The parabolic short strategy gave Sharkness an early high-opportunity niche, but borrow-cost changes and account growth later reduced its usefulness.**
    - He learned the strategy from Peter To and reinforced it by reviewing about 1,000 daily charts per day.
    - He watched stocks more than one average daily true range above VWAP and often shorted after a break below VWAP or another intraday reversal signal.
    - Around 2017, locates became harder and more expensive, creating squeezes and making overnight small-cap shorts less safe.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-084"></span>**The SMB-Kershner merger changed Sharkness’s sense of what was possible because he could observe traders making $25,000 to $50,000 days and learn more scalable strategies from them.**
    - Before the merger, making $2,000 per day like Mendez seemed extraordinary; after the merger, Kershner traders showed a higher ceiling.
    - Strategies included index rebalances, end-of-day imbalances, IPO opens, fat-finger anomalies, and quick fades of unexplained opening spikes.
    - An internal system let him request access to other traders’ executions and study how they traded in real time.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-085"></span>**Journaling is Sharkness’s main improvement engine because his playbook and anti-playbook convert daily wins, mistakes, and moods into process goals.**
    - The playbook concept came from SMB cofounder Mike Bellafiore, while Dr. Brett Steenbarger helped him work on impatience and sizing.
    - He writes up the best and worst trade each day, reviews weekly and monthly, and sets process goals rather than P&L targets.
    - A typical goal might be pausing before exiting winners too quickly or checking whether a day truly offers high opportunity before trading aggressively.
* <span class="pull-quote">Everything works sometimes, and nothing works all the time.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-086"></span>**His worst losses taught different lessons: AMC showed the importance of timeframe-aware risk budgets, while KBIO showed the danger of adding to a losing short in a crowded small-cap squeeze.**
    - He was down about $3 million on an AMC short in 2020 before the stock collapsed and he made the money back the next day.
    - KBIO cost about $150,000 after he added to a losing short as Martin Shkreli’s involvement triggered a squeeze from $2 toward $40.
    - Making back the KBIO loss over the following months became a confidence-building turning point.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-087"></span>**Sharkness’s unusual method is a portfolio of constantly changing strategies across time frames, but most profit still comes from a handful of large, high-conviction days.**
    - He estimates that 80% or more of profits come from trades where he decides to size up.
    - Small trades keep him active and prepared, while one strategy at a time usually carries meaningful size.
    - He wants to reduce activity because many trades are unproductive, but activity helps him stay ready for rare major opportunities.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-088"></span>**His modern parabolic-trade toolkit uses options dynamically to express both a likely final short and the possibility of one more upside squeeze before collapse.**
    - In CRWV, he bought out-of-the-money calls before implied volatility spiked, rolled them higher as the stock rose, and considered shorting stock if it reached the $200 area.
    - Selling puts after implied volatility expands can reduce net long-volatility exposure created by long calls.
    - SMCI in 2024 was his best trade: initial long calls helped during the rally, while the eventual short generated most of the profit.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-089"></span>**His 2022 drawdown was a market-environment failure: after huge 2020-2021 gains, he kept taking trades in a low-follow-through bear market instead of reducing activity quickly.**
    - Stocks would look ready to run for a day and then fail, repeatedly dinging him in small amounts that accumulated.
    - He attributes the drawdown partly to ego after strong years and the desire to keep winning at the same level when the market no longer offered the same setups.
    - He cut size sharply until consistency returned, then rebuilt after a strong trade restored confidence.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-090"></span>**As his account grew, Sharkness added fundamentals, catalysts, and longer holding periods because micro scalping no longer moved the needle.**
    - Liquidia became a longer-term position based on biotech litigation and drug-market fundamentals, timed with technical analysis.
    - Trump’s 2025 tariff announcement created panic selling in many stocks not directly affected by tariffs, producing long opportunities on the rebound.
    - He now blends fundamentals, price action, sentiment, and catalysts, while using the 1-minute chart for execution around higher-timeframe ideas.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-091"></span>**Collaboration remains central to Sharkness because other traders expand idea generation, challenge his decisions, and reveal what is working in markets he cannot watch alone.**
    - He trains a team of five or six traders and gives them access to his reasoning while encouraging independent thought.
    - A macro specialist, gene-therapy team, or junior trader can surface catalysts that become useful to his own process.
    - His advice to new traders is to take it slowly, prove edge at small size, find a mentor whose style fits, and learn from statistics.

## Rick Bandazian Jr.

Bandazian’s chapter shows that an individual trader can still beat algorithms in tiny niches where speed must be paired with legal, deal-specific, and contextual judgment. His consistency comes from radical risk aversion, preparation before news releases, and time stops that recognize his edge decays almost immediately after other traders notice the same event.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-092"></span>**Bandazian’s path to trading began in conventional finance, where investment banking, capital markets, event-driven analysis, and merger arbitrage taught him how corporate events move stocks.**
    - He cold-called and mailed resumes to major firms from Fordham, then worked at J.P. Morgan in syndicated finance and later equity capital markets.
    - At First New York Securities he became an event-driven analyst and later helped run research for a hedge fund spinout.
    - Merger arbitrage work taught him to value catalysts, deal terms, regulatory risk, and timing rather than static cheapness.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-093"></span>**He discovered his niche only after two fast headline trades produced large immediate profits, revealing that his edge was reacting to corporate-event news faster and more accurately than others.**
    - At First New York, he bought a company on a takeover-talk headline and made the equivalent of months of former salary in seconds or minutes, then repeated the experience on another headline.
    - The results convinced him to stop middling event-driven and momentum experiments and focus on headline-driven trades.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-094"></span>**Bandazian’s risk aversion is not a weakness but the core design principle of his trading: he pecks away, exits quickly, and avoids overnight binary risk.**
    - He funded his original independent account with $25,000 and has withdrawn millions while never having a down month over 11 years.
    - He defines one unit as $2,500 and usually exits when a loss approaches that level.
    - His average winning hold time is about five minutes, losses are usually shorter, and he rarely holds overnight positions.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-095"></span>**His original simple-headline edge disappeared between 2016 and 2018 as bots became too fast, so he adapted toward nuanced merger and SEC-filing interpretation that machines still mishandle.**
    - He studies time and sales after missed trades to see whether news moved before Bloomberg headlines and whether bots or faster traders changed the game.
    - A bot can trade a clean $50 bid for a $40 stock, but struggles with multi-contingent filings, regulatory details, old news, or buried clauses.
    - His edge shifted from raw speed to preparation plus speed when new information requires judgment.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-096"></span>**Bandazian builds watch lists and fair-value expectations for announced deals so that he can trade immediately when the market misprices a new filing, approval, denial, or deal term.**
    - He categorizes bids by strategic versus activist buyer, merger versus tender, regulatory approvals, domicile, and other deal features to estimate fair value.
    - He shorted Whitestone REIT after a $14 bid because Maryland incorporation and company history made management likely to reject activist pressure.
    - He bought Foot Locker after the Dick’s Sporting Goods acquisition release because a buried election clause allowed FL to trade above the generic model price.
* <span class="pull-quote">Every second that passes while I’m in a trade, I’m aware that my odds of making a profit decline.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-097"></span>**The Twitter-Musk trade shows how SEC filings can reward a trader who has already done scenario analysis before the decisive document appears.**
    - After Elon Musk took a stake in Twitter, Bandazian modeled whether Musk intended to buy the company and where the stock should trade if he bid.
    - When a 13D filing appeared around 6 a.m., he bought before the price fully adjusted and immediately offered stock near a 10% discount to the bid level he considered fair.
    - He even has 13D as the license plate on his truck, underscoring how central SEC filings are to his identity as a trader.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-098"></span>**Time stops are as important as price stops because his informational advantage decays rapidly once other merger arbs and event traders digest the same news.**
    - About 85% of his trades are winners or breakeven.
    - He normally gives a trade three or four minutes; if the expected move does not happen quickly, the edge has likely fallen toward 50%.
    - His worst loss, about $12,000 in Allergan, came from buying on old Icahn-related news in thin premarket conditions where bids were not available for a clean exit.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-099"></span>**Bandazian’s workday is the cost of his consistency: he monitors news from pre-dawn through evening because many opportunities occur outside regular market hours when bots are less dominant.**
    - He typically checks news at 3:45 a.m., returns to the desk by 5:45 a.m., monitors through the day, and often reviews again from 7 p.m. to 8 p.m.
    - In recent years about two-thirds of his trading has occurred outside regular stock-market hours.
    - He acknowledges that this schedule is increasingly difficult with a wife, children, and age.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-100"></span>**His volunteer firefighting reveals the same willingness to act under pressure, but he manages trading and emergency calls by exiting positions quickly when a serious call requires him.**
    - He has been a volunteer firefighter for about 10 years.
    - Because his trades are short-lived, he can usually get flat before answering a life-or-death call.
    - He may ignore minor calls when waiting for a release, but says he will always answer life-or-death situations.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-101"></span>**Bandazian’s record challenges efficient-market assumptions, but its lack of scale explains why the niche can exist: it is too small and idiosyncratic for many large firms to pursue aggressively.**
    - Assuming a constant $100,000 account, the authors estimate his average annual return at about 316%.
    - He believes a less risk-averse version of himself might make three or four times as much, but his own comfort with size is the limiting factor.
    - The opportunity sits between retail simplicity and institutional scalability: too nuanced for most retail traders and too capacity-limited for large quant shops.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-102"></span>**His advice is to find a differentiated edge that fits personality, not to become the millionth trader guessing the Federal Reserve or copying a method built for someone else.**
    - He says his own method fits extreme risk aversion, speed, and willingness to monitor a narrow information niche for long hours.
    - He worries algorithms may eventually capture even his nuanced deal niche, so he is developing a semi-systematic macro strategy around offsides positioning.
    - Jason Shapiro’s interview in Unknown Market Wizards catalyzed his exploration of the offsides concept.

## Epilogue

The epilogue distills the interviews into a cautionary philosophy: markets punish emotional reflexes, and extraordinary success requires both unusual talent and unusual commitment. It asks readers to examine what they actually want from markets, because the same lessons can guide a future Market Wizard, a part-time profitable hobbyist, or someone who should not trade at all.

* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-103"></span>**The Saint Peter speculator parable makes FOMO the book’s closing image: even someone who invents a rumor can be emotionally pulled into chasing it.**
    - The speculator clears heaven’s crowded trading section by shouting that tomorrow’s closing prices are available in hell, then decides to follow the crowd because the rumor might be true.
    - The joke illustrates how greed, fear, and social proof can overpower knowledge and discipline unless a trader has rules that interrupt reflexive behavior.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-104"></span>**Russo’s Carvana loss is used as the epilogue’s concrete warning that one emotion-driven trade can become ruinous unless the trader eventually returns to rules.**
    - Russo locked in about a $50 million Carvana loss, but exiting prevented a loss that could have increased ninefold or wiped out the account.
    - The trade violated his normal conditions and risk rules, but discipline reentered when he liquidated rather than continued hoping.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-105"></span>**The book argues that not everyone can become a Market Wizard because exceptional trading requires both rare emotional composition and extraordinary dedication.**
    - The authors compare market wizardry to elite athletics: innate traits and learned skill both matter, and desire alone does not create world-class ability.
    - Even passion is only necessary, not sufficient; talent, emotional makeup, and compatible method still determine whether the effort can compound into mastery.
* <span class="pull-quote">Win or lose, everyone gets what they want out of the markets.</span>
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-106"></span>**Schwager’s self-reflection distinguishes wanting trading profits from truly wanting the life and sacrifice of a trader.**
    - He notes that he never devoted even one week to full-time trading, but did take a year’s sabbatical to write A Complete Guide to the Futures Markets.
    - His actions revealed that he wanted to be a successful writer more than he wanted to be a trader.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-107"></span>**The traders in the book are portrayed as people who love the craft itself, not merely the money, because their routines require screen time, research, review, and sacrifices most people would find intolerable.**
    - Many are glued to screens during market hours and spend non-market hours on research, journaling, chart study, or scenario planning.
    - Readers who mainly want a quick, easy fortune are warned that this is the opposite of the life the interviewed traders actually lived.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-108"></span>**Ed Seykota’s line about everyone getting what they want reframes trading as a mirror of desire, discipline, and self-knowledge rather than a purely financial contest.**
    - The line comes from Schwager’s interview with Ed Seykota in the first Market Wizards book.
    - If a person’s actions do not match the claimed goal of becoming a trader, the market will reveal the deeper preference.
* <span id="market-wizards-the-next-generation-109"></span>**The epilogue closes by making the book’s lessons plural rather than prescriptive: readers may use them to pursue elite trading, profitable hobby trading, or a decision not to trade.**
    - The same interviews supply methods for risk control and introspection, but the correct application depends on what each reader genuinely wants from markets.
    - Some readers may be destined for market wizardry, while others may only want a pleasurable, modestly profitable market hobby.
