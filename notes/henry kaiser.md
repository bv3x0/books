# Henry J. Kaiser: Builder in the Modern American West

## Metadata
- Thesis: Henry J. Kaiser's career demonstrates how a self-made entrepreneur leveraged government partnerships, relentless energy, and visionary foresight to industrialize the American West and reshape national life across shipbuilding, steel, aluminum, health care, and beyond. His success was inseparable from federal support, yet his genuine innovations in production, labor relations, and prepaid medicine left lasting monuments that outlived his empire.
- Topics: western industrialization, government-business relations, prepaid health care, World War II production, entrepreneurial leadership

## Roots

Kaiser's character and ambition were forged by a modest upbringing in upstate New York—shaped by a driven mother, a failing father, and early entrepreneurial lessons in photography and salesmanship—creating a restless young man determined to escape modest origins by going west.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-001"></span>**Kaiser's German immigrant parents embodied backward-looking peasant values: his father Frank made boots by hand and hiked deliveries to customers, a business model so primitive that Kaiser later cited it as an unconscious spur toward his own emphasis on speed, volume, and modern production methods.**
    - Frank Kaiser's shoe repair business in Sprout Brook used pre-industrial methods while nearby factories in Utica mass-produced shoes—a vivid contrast young Henry absorbed.
    - "Boyhood chum Harry McFee later speculated that Frank Kaiser's laborious craftsmanship 'may have unconsciously so strongly impressed young Kaiser that it made him naturally turn to the easier, quicker, greater measures which have made him America's No. 1 production miracle man.'" —Harry McFee
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-002"></span>**Kaiser's mother Mary was the central emotional and intellectual force in his early life; her death in 1899 when he was seventeen severed his strongest tie to home and, by his repeated account, fueled his lifelong mission to provide affordable health care for working Americans.**
    - Mary waited up for Henry no matter how late he returned, insisted he continue learning informally, and read to him from books including an early biography of Theodore Roosevelt that Kaiser credited with inspiring his own adventurous spirit.
    - Kaiser claimed on hundreds of occasions that his mother died in his arms and that the family's inability to afford medical care drove him to build what became the Kaiser Permanente Medical Care Program—though this is almost certainly a fabrication, as her death was sudden on a Friday morning when he was on the road.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-003"></span>**Kaiser's early career in photography—building a small chain of shops in Lake Placid, Daytona Beach, Jacksonville, and Palm Beach—taught him the mechanics of entrepreneurship: raising credit, hiring employees, managing seasonal markets, and dealing with suppliers like Eastman-Kodak who refused to grant him an exclusive distributorship.**
    - After buying out partner W. W. Brownell in Lake Placid, Kaiser hung a shingle reading 'Henry J. Kaiser: the Man With the Smile' and followed tourists south to Florida for winter business.
    - When Eastman-Kodak responded to his demand for an exclusive Daytona Beach distributorship by licensing thirteen other dealers, Kaiser learned firsthand how established suppliers could neutralize an upstart retailer.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-004"></span>**Edgar Fosburgh's demand that Kaiser abandon photography, go west, build a house, save $1,000, and earn $125 a month before marrying his daughter Bess catalyzed Kaiser's move to Spokane in 1906 and his pivot into construction—accidentally matching him to an industry on the cusp of a massive government-funded boom.**
    - Fosburgh, a lumberman, considered photography inconsequential and wanted Kaiser to establish himself in a 'substantial' business before granting his blessing.
    - Kaiser fulfilled all terms within ten months of leaving the East, purchased lots in the prestigious southeast section of Spokane, and hired a noted architect—spending far beyond the means of a typical hardware salesman but confident in his earning trajectory.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-005"></span>**Kaiser's years as a hardware salesman and then contractor for J. F. Hill Company in Spokane gave him his foundational business education: competitive bidding, local political corruption in contract awards, loyalty to subordinates, and the danger of compromising personal integrity under managerial pressure—which he demonstrated by finishing Hill's contracts unpaid after being threatened with firing for refusing to falsify reports.**
    - Kaiser memorized every item's price at McGowan Brothers Hardware, aggressively pursued contracts other salesmen declined, and rose from $7/week clerk to city sales manager earning $250/month.
    - When new management at the Hill Company demanded he doctor his reports to disadvantage rivals, Kaiser refused and worked four months without pay to complete existing contracts—earning warm admirers and future customers.

## Launching a Career

Kaiser's nomadic years from 1901 to 1921—running photography studios, marrying Bess Fosburgh, working through the hardware and construction trades in Spokane—established the core habits, partnerships, and corporate seed that would grow into his industrial empire, with his marriage to Bess providing both the emotional anchor and the practical counsel that shaped his early business decisions.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-006"></span>**Bess Fosburgh's profoundly insecure childhood—shuttled between relatives after her mother died two weeks after her birth, dismissed by her own father as an 'ugly duckling'—made Kaiser the first important man who did not abandon her, creating a marriage of unusual depth in which Bess's instincts about people became a genuine input into Kaiser's corporate personnel decisions.**
    - Bess spent thirteen years in temporary living arrangements before boarding school, and her first serious romance was with the ardent young photographer who waited on her in Lake Placid.
    - According to one of Bess's most intimate friends, Henry particularly valued her advice on personnel moves because 'she had acute instincts for sensing character and integrity in Kaiser's associates.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-007"></span>**Kaiser's pivot from photography to hardware sales and then to construction contracting in Spokane was driven by Edgar Fosburgh's demand that he enter a 'substantial' business, but it was also consistent with Kaiser's own growing restlessness with a trade that required him to dishonestly alter photographs to satisfy paying clients.**
    - Kaiser told associates he disliked promotional photography for hotels and travel agencies in Florida because he was required to alter photographs of natural scenery to satisfy clients—'commercialization of his work drained much of the joy he had originally derived from his creative endeavors.'
    - At McGowan Brothers Hardware in Spokane, Kaiser aggressively pursued large construction hardware contracts—including a $10,000 contract for reinforcing steel for a bridge and a $12,000 order for the National Bank Building—demonstrating the go-after-business instinct he would apply throughout his career.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-008"></span>**Kaiser's move from hardware to the Hawkeye Fuel Company and then to J. F. Hill Company gave him his crucial introduction to general contracting—including competitive bidding, managing remote workforces, and the hazards of corporate political infighting—at the precise moment the 1916 Federal Highway Act was about to create an enormous national market for road builders.**
    - Kaiser joined Hill by 'loaning' himself to Hawkeye to fix shaker screens, then never returned to McGowan—a pattern of seizing opportunities that characterized his entire career.
    - In British Columbia working for Hill's subsidiary, Kaiser earned $8,000 a year and had authority to submit bids for the company before being fired for refusing to falsify reports during a management power struggle.

## Taming the Wilderness—Roads

Between 1914 and 1930, Kaiser built hundreds of miles of roads across Canada, the Pacific Northwest, California, and Cuba, developing in the process his core corporate organization, his mastery of new construction technology, his political skills in lobbying for government highway funding, and his reputation for completing contracts at unprecedented speed.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-009"></span>**Kaiser's first independent contracting venture in Vancouver in 1914 succeeded only because he secured a $25,000 loan from the Canadian Bank of Commerce on the strength of a single contract and an idea—a moment he later called the most important loan of his life, because it demonstrated that audacity backed by a credible plan could unlock capital even without collateral.**
    - "The bank president asked incredulously: 'You mean to sit there and inform me, young man, you want me to loan you $25,000 and you don't even have a company, you don't even have any equipment, you don't have any men?'" —Canadian Bank of Commerce branch president
    - Kaiser had leveraged a personal connection to Sir William McKenzie of Toronto, who had developed respect for him during the Hill Company management struggle, to gain the initial interview.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-010"></span>**Kaiser's partnership with Warren Brothers—a Massachusetts paving company whose bitulithic asphalt technology he adopted and who repeatedly underwrote his early contracts—was as important to his rise as any government contract, establishing a template of mutual benefit with established firms that he would replicate throughout his career.**
    - Warren Brothers held majority stock in Kaiser's second company, the Nanaimo Paving Company, providing capital in exchange for Kaiser using and promoting their bitulithic asphalt across western markets.
    - The relationship endured for decades: Warren Brothers offered Kaiser the 200-mile Cuba subcontract in 1927, and Kaiser later invited Warren to share in some of his most profitable work.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-011"></span>**Kaiser's acquisition of R. G. Le Tourneau's earth-moving patents for $50,000—after Le Tourneau observed that Kaiser was the first contractor who 'saw them as instruments to make big jobs small'—gave Kaiser decisive technological superiority that enabled him to pursue dams, large highways, and other megaprojects his competitors could not efficiently attempt.**
    - Le Tourneau's 'earth mover' was a large tractor on caterpillar tracks with five telescoping buckets that could scrape about ten cubic yards of earth per minute—transforming what previously required armies of workers into a mechanized operation.
    - "At Philbrook Dam on the Feather River, the first earthen dam Kaiser built, Le Tourneau recalled that 'Kaiser had that big job timed to perfection. More, he knew how to get along with men when men didn't know how to get along with each other.'" —R. G. Le Tourneau
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-012"></span>**The 200-mile Cuba highway subcontract (1927–1930) was the pivotal step from regional contractor to nationally significant builder: it required managing thousands of foreign workers, overcoming supply corruption, finishing ahead of schedule to ensure payment from an unstable government, and yielded a net profit of $2.1 million that funded entry into major dam building.**
    - Kaiser's men discovered that Cuban workers could not be publicly corrected without creating sullen workers—'foremen eventually learned that Cubans delivered and received criticism indirectly, through a third party and in private'—a lesson in cross-cultural management.
    - "Ordway summarized the Cuba job's significance: 'our determination to go into Cuba gave us that one large job we had never had and always wanted. We finished the job a year ahead of schedule to enhance our reputation, and it provided the money that allowed us to go into big dam building and beyond.'" —Alonzo B. Ordway
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-013"></span>**Kaiser's entry into California road building via the Redding–Red Bluff contract in 1921 and his subsequent friendship with Warren A. 'Dad' Bechtel—who recruited him into the Associated General Contractors and with whom he would share dam building, pipeline, and eventually global projects—established the West Coast network that put him in position to bid on Hoover Dam.**
    - Kaiser Paving set records on U.S. Highway 99 by progressing at a mile per week when state engineers assumed two miles per month was the maximum, attracting state 'spies' who expected to find sloppy work but found none.
    - Kaiser's political work promoting the first Gas Tax Act in California and his later presidency of the national Associated General Contractors (1932) gave him lobbying experience he would apply at the federal level during the Hoover Dam project.

## Taming the Wilderness—Dams

Kaiser's decade of dam building (1931–1942)—anchored by the Hoover, Bonneville, and Grand Coulee contracts won through the Six Companies consortium—transformed him from regional contractor to national industrialist, gave him mastery of federal politics and bureaucratic relations, and provided the organizational template and capital he used to enter manufacturing.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-014"></span>**The Six Companies consortium for Hoover Dam—formed in February 1931 and comprising Kaiser-Bechtel, Utah Construction, Morrison-Knudsen, MacDonald and Kahn, J. F. Shea, and Pacific Bridge—won the contract with a bid of $48.9 million, five million below the nearest competitor, by pooling expertise, spreading risk across firms, and submitting an averaged estimate from each partner.**
    - The partners had to raise $5 million for bonding, with unequal initial shares; Kaiser and Bechtel together held 30 percent, the largest single allocation.
    - Kaiser spent weeks driving all night in his yellow Marmon automobile to study canyon walls, so that 'by the spring of 1931 Kaiser probably knew the canyon better than anyone but job superintendent Frank T. Crowe.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-015"></span>**Working conditions at Hoover Dam were brutal and Six Companies' treatment of labor was exploitative: the company worked three shifts in 128-degree heat, housed men in sweltering barracks without showers or treated water, resisted union organizing with hired security, and knowingly violated Nevada safety laws by continuing gasoline-powered trucks in tunnels that were poisoning workers with carbon monoxide—eventually settling forty-eight gas suits out of court.**
    - The state mine inspector ordered Six Companies to stop using gasoline-powered trucks in diversionary tunnels in November 1931, but company lawyers successfully obstructed Nevada laws for the duration of the project.
    - A scholar examining the gas-suit strategy concluded it 'appeared that Six Companies investigators had engaged in pimping, criminal conspiracy, intimidation of witnesses, and jury tampering in their effort to dispose of the gas cases.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-016"></span>**Kaiser's designation as Six Companies' Washington liaison transformed him from a field contractor into a sophisticated political operator: he shepherded congressional appropriations, built close relationships with New Deal officials including Harold Ickes, managed damaging public investigations, and pioneered aggressive use of public relations—including a $25,000 guest house for visiting dignitaries—to maintain the project's image and funding.**
    - "Interior Secretary Ickes, irritated by Kaiser's 'telegraphic bombardment' during a controversy, wrote to Senator Pittman that Kaiser 'is doing himself and his company no good by his lack of composure'—and Kaiser learned from the reprimand." —Harold L. Ickes
    - When Ickes threatened a $350,000 fine for contract violations as work wound down, Kaiser arranged publication of a handsome illustrated booklet, So Hoover Was Built, mailing copies to Ickes before general release; the secretary backed down to a $100,000 fine.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-017"></span>**Kaiser's entry into cement production at Permanente in 1939—bidding $1.19 per barrel against the West Coast combine's $1.41 to win the Shasta Dam contract, then building the world's first ten-mile conveyor belt rather than pay prohibitive railroad rates for aggregates—marked his transition from contractor to industrialist and proved that challenging established monopolies with government backing was viable.**
    - A California railroad man observed after the cement victory: 'If Kaiser's life can be said to have a turning point, it was then. He licked a tough bunch. From then on, he wasn't afraid to tackle anything.'
    - When local railroads demanded $.27 per ton for a short haul of sand and gravel, Kaiser's engineers built a ten-mile conveyor belt moving a thousand tons per hour at $.18 per ton—'billed as the world's longest continuous conveyor belt.'

## Patriot in Pinstripes—Shipbuilding

Between 1940 and 1945 Kaiser's yards at Richmond and Portland/Vancouver produced 1,490 vessels—including 821 Liberty Ships—at unprecedented speeds by applying construction-industry innovations like prefabrication and competitive yard rivalry, transforming an unknown West Coast contractor into a national war hero while also generating massive social disruption in the communities surrounding the shipyards.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-018"></span>**Kaiser entered shipbuilding not as his own initiative but through Six Companies associates Stephen Bechtel and John McCone, who saw it as 'about ripe to become big volume business' in 1937; Kaiser's contribution was accelerating their cautious timeline by immediately delegating shipyard construction to Clay Bedford in Richmond and Edgar Kaiser in Portland, moving with characteristic speed once committed.**
    - When the British commission awarded a contract for sixty cargo ships in December 1940, Kaiser and Todd formed two companies—Todd-Bath for the East Coast, Todd-California for the West—and Kaiser sent Bedford to Richmond with the instruction: 'Clay, you're going to build a shipyard.'
    - Bedford's crew at Richmond built shipways before blueprints were drawn, dumping 300,000 cubic yards of rock on an eighty-acre marshy plot in three weeks, and laid the keel for the first vessel on April 14, 1941.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-019"></span>**The shipyards triggered demographic explosions in Richmond and Portland/Vancouver that overwhelmed local infrastructure: Richmond's population grew 400 percent between 1940 and 1944 as nearly 100,000 workers flooded in, creating housing chaos, crime surges, school overcrowding, and public health crises that Kaiser management and government agencies only partially addressed.**
    - "A machinist's wife living in her car described her situation: 'I just wish we were home. War or no war, if I'd known there'd be no place in Richmond we could live, I just wouldn't have come. You can live like this just so long, then you can't stand it any more.'" —unnamed shipyard worker's wife
    - A 1944 survey showed the average Richmond worker grossed $61/week; after taxes, food, rent, and essentials, he had six dollars left—and many spent that on San Francisco's Barbary Coast, leaving the yards after four years without a dime in savings.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-020"></span>**Kaiser's yards achieved their production records through the perfection of prefabricated subassembly construction and the strategic use of inter-yard competition: by positioning all welding 'downhand' to exploit gravity, simplifying tasks so workers could be trained in hours rather than weeks, and having each yard's records serve as a target for the other, they cut average ship production time to 30–35 days when competitors averaged two months.**
    - Bedford's crew laid a keel at midnight on November 7, 1942 and produced a ship in four days, fifteen hours, and twenty-six minutes—a feat criticized as a 'publicity stunt' but which Kaiser defended as an 'incentive stunt' that generated 250 worker suggestions for further improvements.
    - "Kaiser stressed that he shared innovations with other builders: 'When Bedford surpassed Edgar's record, there were observers from all the other yards to see what we have discovered.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-021"></span>**Kaiser's labor relations at the shipyards were complicated by the AFL-CIO rivalry: he initially signed contracts with AFL Boilermakers locals covering only 66 workers, and when thousands of CIO-affiliated workers arrived, the conflict over representation consumed months, was ultimately settled by federal intervention keeping the AFL contracts in place, and exposed racial and gender discrimination by union leader Tom Ray, who spent $250,000 on a private social club.**
    - The NLRB discovered that Ray had denied blacks and women full voting membership in the union while collecting full dues from them, and national AFL officials dumped him when he became a liability.
    - An FEPC study of the three Portland/Vancouver yards in September 1944 found that 7,892 blacks comprised 8.8 percent of the workforce, but only one earned more than $1.80 per hour.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-022"></span>**The postwar congressional investigation of Kaiser's shipbuilding profits—where the GAO charged he invested only $2.5 million of his own capital and reaped $129 million—and Kaiser's counter-claim that the yards actually lost $18.6 million (by deducting RFC interest on steel loans) illustrated how the complexity of his interlocked corporate finances made definitive accounting impossible, and established the 'government parasite' narrative that would haunt him for years.**
    - "GAO chief counsel Ralph E. Casey stated: 'I dare say that at no time in the history of American business, whether in wartime or in peacetime, have so many men made so much money with so little risk—and all at the expense of the taxpayer.'" —Ralph E. Casey
    - "Historian Frederick C. Lane's study of wartime shipbuilding found that 'the corporate connections between Kaiser's shipbuilding and his production of steel and magnesium made it difficult to determine his final profits.'" —Frederick C. Lane

## Man of Steel

Kaiser's battle to build and sustain a western steel mill at Fontana, California—against Big Steel's resistance, bureaucratic opposition, and a decade-long fight over RFC loan terms—demonstrated both his prescient grasp of postwar western growth and the high political cost of his dependence on government financing, ultimately vindicated when he paid off the RFC in full in 1950 and Fontana eclipsed the government-owned Geneva plant.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-023"></span>**Kaiser's campaign for a western steel mill exposed Big Steel's deliberate suppression of expansion: the 'Gano Dunn Report' of February 1941—authored by a U.S. Steel loan executive—falsely assured the government that existing capacity could meet any emergency, while Kaiser had warned of impending shortages a full year before federal officials acknowledged them.**
    - "Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau told Roosevelt in mid-December 1940: 'No expert knowledge is necessary to see that the steel industry will be unable to handle the volume of orders that lies ahead… an immediate major expansion program for the steel industry is clearly called for.'" —Henry Morgenthau Jr.
    - "OPACS economist Richard V. Gilbert called the Dunn Report 'nothing short of irresponsible… Playing it safe from the viewpoint of industry has always meant restricting capacity… It is high time our requirements and capabilities were estimated by people who play it safe for the country.'" —Richard V. Gilbert
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-024"></span>**Kaiser secured RFC authorization for the Fontana mill through a combination of persistence, personal lobbying with the President, and comic improvisation: when WPB officials demanded a 'turbo-blower' for the blast furnace, Kaiser didn't know what one was—but nonchalantly promised to provide it, then had his engineers confirm they could build one before WPB noticed the gap.**
    - One witness recalled that Kaiser personally followed final authorization forms from room to room while they collected bureaucratic signatures, then 'dashed over to Director Jesse Jones' offices at the RFC.'
    - Jones demanded assurances from established producers before releasing loans; Kaiser secured commitments from Republic Steel's Tom Girdler and Charles White within twenty-four hours—men who would later become fierce antagonists.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-025"></span>**The decade-long fight over the RFC loan for Fontana (1942–1950) illustrates how Kaiser's political vulnerability grew as the New Deal receded: RFC officials initially hinted at debt reduction but became increasingly hostile as the 79th Congress turned conservative and Kaiser's enemies circulated 'New Deal's Darling' attacks, forcing him to pay the loan in full plus interest in November 1950.**
    - The RFC's concession of selling the Geneva plant to U.S. Steel for $47.5 million—one-fourth of its $200 million construction cost—established a precedent Kaiser expected to receive at Fontana, but conservative Republicans blocked parallel treatment.
    - After years of frustration, Kaiser published Outrage in Steel (1948), accusing the RFC of 'crushing Fontana by favoritism to Geneva' and calling its policies 'an outrage against the people.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-026"></span>**Kaiser's labor relations with Big Steel's United Steel Workers of America followed a consistent pattern of breaking ranks to settle early: his January 1946 acceptance of the $.185/hour increase—while Fairless of U.S. Steel refused the same amount—positioned him publicly as a statesman, infuriated Big Steel, and set a precedent he and Edgar repeated in 1952 and 1959, culminating in a landmark long-term labor-management committee with USWA head David MacDonald that pioneered worker-sharing of productivity gains.**
    - "Kaiser told the nation: '…three-and-a-half cents is two percent of steel wages. Who can estimate costs down to two percent? Can anyone hesitate to save his country for three-and-a-half cents?'" —Henry J. Kaiser
    - The Kaiser-MacDonald committee, chaired by University of Pennsylvania labor expert Dr. George W. Taylor, agreed that workers would receive one-third of cost savings from eliminated wasteful practices, plus guaranteed jobs for those displaced by technology—earning Kaiser the AFL-CIO's Murray-Green Award in 1964.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-027"></span>**Fontana's eventual dominance over Geneva reversed conventional industrial wisdom that mills should be located near raw materials: Kaiser correctly intuited that postwar western growth would make proximity to markets more valuable than proximity to iron ore, and by 1960 Fontana outproduced Geneva in ingot capacity and blooming and sheet rolling output, cementing Kaiser as the pacesetter in western steel.**
    - By the mid-1950s Kaiser Steel sold 65 percent of its output within sixty miles of Fontana, while Geneva shipped more than three-fourths of its steel to the West Coast at freight rates of $15/ton versus Kaiser's $1.82 to Los Angeles.
    - When Kaiser died in 1967, his company ranked as the dominant integrated steel facility in the West—vindicating his 1942 prediction of 'an industrial base [that] might break the region's generations-old dependence upon eastern manufacturing.'

## Creating an Image

Kaiser's transformation from publicity-shy contractor to masterful media celebrity between 1941 and 1945—driven by the cargo plane proposal, serious consideration as FDR's 1944 running mate, and a growing professional public relations operation—demonstrated both the power of wartime heroism to reshape a businessman's image and the permanent vulnerability that celebrity created when political winds shifted.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-028"></span>**Kaiser's 1942 cargo plane proposal—unveiled on a Sunday morning so reporters could file for Monday eastern papers—marked his deliberate transition from accidental celebrity to calculated publicist: he recognized that staging access to information was as important as the idea itself, and that national columnists could pressure the government more effectively than direct lobbying.**
    - Within ten days of the Portland speech, Kaiser presented the cargo plane plan in the Oval Office; Senator Abe Murdock informed Kaiser that his name was 'becoming a household word throughout the country.'
    - Kaiser had already moved in this direction: as early as 1931, he had generated a 'steady flow of news stories, information kits, progress reports and special briefings which built a national and international reputation for the Six Companies.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-029"></span>**Kaiser came seriously close to the 1944 Democratic vice presidential nomination—FBI background check ordered, discussed at senior White House level, supported by Samuel Rosenman—but was ultimately dropped, possibly because Tommy Corcoran spread a rumor (denied) that he was Jewish and because he had endorsed a large wartime sales tax in 1942.**
    - "Harold Ickes recalled that Corcoran claimed columnist Eliot Janeway 'had circulated the rumor that Kaiser was a Jew,' and when Ickes was asked if he wanted to 'make antisemitism an issue,' the discussion turned to Truman." —Harold L. Ickes
    - "Columnist Jack Smith later claimed 'FDR decided then and there that he wanted Henry J. Kaiser for Vice President,' but old-line party bosses talked him out of choosing a man who had never held public office." —Jack Smith
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-030"></span>**Kaiser's public relations operation—assembled by late 1942 with Cadman, Calhoun, and outside consultants—evolved from crisis management and myth amplification toward more strategic image control, but the organization repeatedly overreacted to criticism, producing embarrassing overreach such as demanding congressional-record retractions, threatening lawsuits, and releasing statements in a congressman's name without authorization.**
    - When C. C. Crow, editor of a Pacific Coast lumber digest, ran vitriolic attacks, Kaiser's publicists hired detectives who reported U.S. Steel was behind the operation—but wisely advised against a lawsuit that 'would merely give him the notoriety he seeks.'
    - "Experienced banker George D. Woods advised Kaiser that the emphasis on his personal role had made sense when he first emerged publicly, 'but that time has long since passed… at this stage it is better to talk about Companies.'" —George D. Woods

## Planning for a Postwar World

Kaiser used his wartime celebrity platform to become the most prominent private advocate for postwar economic reconversion, correctly predicting pent-up consumer demand, proposing housing programs and health care systems that anticipated the Great Society, and personally entering housing, home appliances, fiberglass, and other consumer industries—though his ambitions consistently outran execution in these non-production-oriented fields.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-031"></span>**Kaiser's December 1942 address to the National Association of Manufacturers—urging industrialists to start planning for peace while most were still gearing up for war—reflected a genuine and prescient conviction that postwar consumer demand would be explosive, a view he shared months before mainstream business organizations acknowledged it.**
    - "In September 1942 Kaiser told an Oakland banquet audience: 'At home our markets will be drained dry, and a pent-up consumer demand will be released, seeking satisfaction in every artifice and device we know how to make.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
    - "Financier Bernard Baruch wrote to Roosevelt in June 1943 that Kaiser 'has some wonderful practical ideas about postwar conditions'—validating Kaiser's outsider status in mainstream business planning circles." —Bernard M. Baruch
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-032"></span>**Kaiser and Fritz Burns entered the postwar housing market as mass producers rather than tract builders, applying shipyard prefabrication principles to factory-built kitchen and bathroom units—their Kaiser Community Homes built 5,000 units in 1947 and introduced curved street layouts and multiple exterior color options—but total output never exceeded 10,000 units and Kaiser's interest faded as the housing shortage abated by 1950.**
    - The partners offered four basic models—Cape Cod, Colonial, California, and Contemporary—with theoretically 750 variations and actually 28, priced at $7,000–$9,000 in 1946, serving middle- and lower-middle-class buyers shut out of the prewar market by the Depression and the war.
    - Industry analysts noted that certain methods from the shipyards were applied: 'a factory turned out panel assemblies, partly finished porches and roofs, and other sections requiring large, stationary machinery.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-033"></span>**Kaiser's research laboratory 'hobby-lobby' in Emeryville tested dozens of postwar consumer products—dishwashers, air conditioners, lawnmowers—and Kaiser seriously pursued entry into fiberglass, paper, railroads, and airports, but most initiatives failed to move beyond preliminary negotiations because he was simultaneously committed to automobiles, steel, aluminum, housing, and health care, spreading organizational capacity past its limits.**
    - Kaiser drew Harold Boeschenstein of Owens-Corning Fiberglass into a vision of the material's 'potential to create hundreds of new cottage industries generating more uses for the material'—and within a few years major suppliers set up several West Coast plants, spawning hundreds of ancillary businesses.
    - Negotiations for the Southland Paper Mills in Texas, with assets of $130 million, reached the point of agreed percentages in May 1945 before falling through—had circumstances been slightly different, Kaiser might have entered paper rather than aluminum.

## Debacle in Detroit

Kaiser-Frazer's automobile venture (1945–1955) was Kaiser's single spectacular failure: profitable in only 1947 and 1948, losing $123 million over its decade, it was undermined by undercapitalization, Big Steel's punitive supply restrictions, inability to develop new models fast enough, and a catastrophic stock-offering collapse engineered by financier Cyrus Eaton—yet its hard lessons in consumer competition shaped the organization's more disciplined global expansion afterward.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-034"></span>**Kaiser and Joe Frazer were an unlikely partnership of opposites—Kaiser the high-speed government-contract builder, Frazer the Yale-educated thirty-year auto industry veteran—who came together because each had what the other lacked: Kaiser had capital and construction capacity, Frazer had automobile knowledge and a dealer network, and both needed each other to seize the unprecedented opening left by sixteen years of depression and war.**
    - Banker Amadeo Giannini of Bank of America brokered the partnership, telling Frazer (who couldn't raise eastern capital) to 'look up Kaiser, since he knew that Kaiser wanted to enter the field and was seriously considering setting up production facilities in southern California.'
    - "Frazer had mocked Kaiser's December 1942 NAM speech as 'half-baked… stupid bushwah,' making their eventual partnership all the more ironic." —Joe Frazer
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-035"></span>**Big Steel's deliberate restriction of steel supplies to Kaiser-Frazer—exemplified by Republic Steel's Tom Girdler telling Edgar Kaiser and Frazer they were 'not even on the list' while accusing Kaiser of having 'a stranglehold' on Truman—forced the auto company into an expensive scramble for steel from small independents and black-market sources that added millions in production costs.**
    - "Girdler 'flew into a rage' and accused Kaiser of having embarrassed Big Steel in the same way Kaiser Steel had embarrassed them in the January 1946 wage settlement: 'Throughout the entire conversation, Mr. Girdler was most profane and really abusive.'" —Joe Frazer
    - Supply crises were constant: when body presses nearly exhausted steel inventory one afternoon, 'road expediters' located 47,000 pounds at Bethlehem's Buffalo works, rented six DC-3s in Philadelphia, and unloaded the steel at Willow Run at 4 A.M. the next morning.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-036"></span>**The February 1948 stock-offering collapse—in which Cyrus Eaton's Otis and Company orchestrated a manufactured lawsuit on the day of the offering to escape their underwriting commitment—damaged Kaiser-Frazer fatally: the company failed to raise badly needed capital, its stock plunged, and six years of litigation consuming thousands of work-hours produced no winners except lawyers.**
    - Court testimony revealed that David Martin, who filed the disabling suit on behalf of plaintiff Masterson on February 9, had previously worked for Otis and Company, and that Eaton's office had exchanged suspicious phone calls with the senator and plaintiff in the days prior.
    - "Kaiser demanded an honest accounting: 'Never in my history have I seen a reputable magazine publish baseless rumors that are so destructive and damaging to an industry or industries.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-037"></span>**The Henry J compact car (1950)—introduced at $1,299 with the aim of undercutting Big Three entry models by $250—generated initial enthusiasm but failed because American consumers of the 1950s wanted 'pizzazz' and the company lacked the capital and design resources to update the product rapidly enough, while the parallel Sears 'Allstate' marketing deal further demoralized loyal Kaiser dealers.**
    - Consumer Reports praised the 1951 models as 'a very satisfactory car to drive and ride in, well-shorn of weight, and rating generally high as efficient transportation'—but positive reviews could not overcome the Big Three's superior marketing and dealer organization.
    - "Sales manager W. A. MacDonald muttered privately after the Henry J's public unveiling: 'Jesus Christ! How am I going to sell this monstrosity? I just wonder if my salary is worth it.'" —W. A. MacDonald

## Venturing Abroad

Kaiser's postwar international expansion—anchored by the Argentine Industrias Kaiser Argentina and Brazilian Willys-Overland do Brazil ventures of the mid-1950s—pioneered a model of minority-ownership, local-investor-controlled partnerships that contrasted with traditional colonial capitalism and succeeded precisely because Argentine and Brazilian consumers retained ownership and pride in their national industries.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-038"></span>**The myth that Kaiser avoided foreign business for twenty-five years after Cuba was false: throughout the 1940s he shipped cement to Hawaii and the Panama Canal Zone, negotiated with Soviet, Chinese, and Venezuelan officials, and opened small automobile assembly plants in the Netherlands, Israel, Mexico, and Japan—establishing that foreign auto markets interested him well before the Argentina deal.**
    - In February 1952, American Automobile reported that 'in ratio of exports of passenger cars to total production, Kaiser-Frazer was the leader among American producers' in 1951—a hollow achievement in domestic terms but revealing of Kaiser's consistent foreign market interest.
    - Kaiser's managers signed a memorandum of understanding with T. V. Soong to build a small shipyard in Shanghai in April 1945, but the collapse of negotiations with both the Nationalists and the Communists by early 1946 ended China as a serious prospect.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-039"></span>**Kaiser's deal with Juan Perón for Industrias Kaiser Argentina (IKA) succeeded because Kaiser insisted on Argentine majority ownership—a critical decision that allowed local investors to claim the plant as their own, protected it when Perón was overthrown in 1955, and demonstrated an alternative to colonial-capitalist exploitation that made IKA a model praised by Business Week, Harper's, and the New York Herald-Tribune.**
    - When Argentine underwriters offered IKA stock to local investors, 'the issue was snapped up in three hours': the government took 19 percent, private investors the majority, and Americans received only token percentages.
    - By 1964 IKA was Argentina's largest manufacturing plant with 9,000 Argentine employees and only 35 North Americans on the payroll, earning profits of $6 million in 1960 and $9 million in 1961.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-040"></span>**The Brazilian Willys-Overland do Brazil (WOB) venture mirrored IKA's success: by 1963 it had 8,500 employees, 48,000 Brazilian shareholders owning 52 percent, total sales of $182 million, and had produced over 250,000 jeeps—proof that Kaiser's model of democratic ownership and rapid technology transfer could be replicated across different Latin American political environments.**
    - Kaiser briefly considered using Brazil as an export base, dazzling the Brazilian ambassador with a vision of producing 50,000 vehicles annually for sale in the United States—but sober second thoughts produced a more limited jeep-focused agenda.
    - In 1967 Kaiser sold his holdings in both WOB and IKA for a total of $40 million to Ford and Renault respectively, concluding that the organization's comparative advantage lay elsewhere.

## Cargo Planes and Government Investigations

Kaiser's decade-long involvement in aircraft—from the 1942 cargo plane proposal and Spruce Goose partnership with Howard Hughes, through the Korean War C-119 contracts at Willow Run, to their congressional cancellation in 1953—illustrated both his habitual overconfidence when entering unfamiliar industries and the political vulnerability of his government-dependent business model once the New Deal coalition collapsed.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-041"></span>**Kaiser's July 1942 proposal to build 5,000 cargo planes—framed as a way to fly supplies over German submarines—attracted enormous media enthusiasm but was effectively sabotaged by established aircraft manufacturers who dominated the WPB review committee, forcing Kaiser to accept a consolation prize of three prototype giant cargo planes that could not use strategic materials or raid competitor personnel.**
    - WPB chief Donald Nelson appointed three established producers to a four-man committee assessing Kaiser's qualifications; 'Not surprisingly, the committee concluded that permitting Kaiser to engage in experimentation was a luxury the nation could not afford.'
    - "Columnist Raymond Clapper charged: 'All he has to show is two mealy letters from Donald Nelson…. Don't waste the time of such a valuable man. Why kid a man who has his production record?'" —Raymond Clapper
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-042"></span>**The Kaiser-Hughes partnership collapsed because their working styles were fundamentally incompatible: Kaiser was a deadline-obsessed builder who needed information to coordinate production, while Hughes was a compulsive perfectionist who missed appointments, ignored urgent telegrams, and fired a succession of project managers—leading the WPB to cancel the contract in February 1944 after $10 million was spent on a single overweight prototype.**
    - "Ed Bern, Hughes' general manager who resigned in August 1943, warned WPB's Nelson by phone: 'We have a terribly chaotic situation out here. It is going to blow right up in your face.'" —Ed Bern
    - Despite cancelation, Hughes personally funded completion of the 'Spruce Goose' at a cost of $7–17 million of his own capital, achieving a single brief test flight on November 2, 1947.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-043"></span>**The 1953 Senate investigation of Kaiser-Frazer's C-119 production—led by Senator Styles Bridges, supplied by Cyrus Eaton's operatives, and using cost-per-plane comparisons that deliberately ignored Kaiser's higher retooling charges and the military's demand for more modifications than Fairchild planes received—ended with Air Force Secretary Talbott canceling the contract the moment Kaiser was mid-testimony, a politically convenient escape from an embarrassing program.**
    - Bridges 'discovered' that the RFC loan and Kaiser's meeting with Undersecretary McCone occurred on the same day in December 1950—ignoring that the C-119 contract was awarded ten days later amid genuine fears of expanded conflict with China.
    - "Kaiser graciously accepted cancellation: 'Throughout my career, whenever I have done government work, I have had a firm principle… That is that I am proud of my government; I want to do the work and service that it wants me to do, and I want to relinquish anything which it does not want me to do.'" —Henry J. Kaiser

## Light Metals—Heavy Profits

Kaiser Aluminum became the organization's greatest long-term financial success: acquired on April Fool's Day 1946 when virtually no one wanted the government-built surplus plants, the company grew by correctly anticipating explosive postwar consumer and industrial demand for aluminum, challenging Alcoa's dominance through aggressive pricing and the 1955 Ravenswood plant, and ultimately earning $58.9 million in net profits in 1966—dwarfing all other Kaiser enterprises.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-044"></span>**Kaiser's April 1946 lease of the government-built Mead reduction plant and Trentwood rolling mill in Washington state for $9.87 million—when virtually no other company would bid—was the founding act of an aluminum empire that most experts considered foolhardy, but Kaiser had quietly analyzed postwar consumer applications for years and recognized that Alcoa's near-monopoly would be broken by the Justice Department.**
    - Most of Kaiser's advisors and three Six Companies partners—MacDonald and Kahn, Morrison-Knudsen, and Utah Construction—refused to participate; the doubters believed wartime aluminum capacity so exceeded postwar demand that the plants were white elephants.
    - A Fortune article in 1956 called Kaiser's manager Dusty Rhoades 'a fierce and shrewd competitor' who 'wrung a much higher rate of production out of his plants than experienced wartime operators ever had.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-045"></span>**The Korean War gave Kaiser the authorization and political cover to triple production capacity—including building the huge Chalmette plant in Louisiana, sited through the lobbying of Mayor De Lesseps Morrison and Congressman Hale Boggs—and to borrow $115 million from eighteen insurance companies and eight banks, permanently breaking free from Alcoa's dominance as a raw material supplier.**
    - "Interior Secretary Chapman told Kaiser he had to give smaller independent companies a chance to expand; Kaiser replied: 'I might as well tell the world that you don't want us to expand aluminum. You know you have given us the run around for damn near a year.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
    - "At the Chalmette dedication, Mobilization Director Charles Wilson recounted Kaiser's challenge to double Wilson's own production target, to which an aide had said 'Take it easy, H. J. Remember, Rome wasn't built in a day.' Kaiser allegedly replied: 'I don't know about that; I wasn't working on that job.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-046"></span>**The 1955 Ravenswood, West Virginia, rolling mill—built on the Ohio River for proximity to eastern markets rather than near raw materials—was the strategic masterstroke that made KACC fully competitive nationally: it cut transportation costs to eastern customers by 75 percent, allowed KACC to capture 20–25 percent of the eastern foil market, and revived the depressed Ohio River Valley while tripling Ravenswood's population.**
    - Over nine thousand applicants visited the KACC personnel office within two weeks of Kaiser's visit to Ravenswood; locals feared population might multiply tenfold, and KACC built a twenty-room elementary school and helped fund a new hospital in nearby Ripley.
    - Time reported in 1956 that the Ravenswood plant was 'reviving the long-depressed Ohio River Valley'—and by the mid-1960s Ravenswood's population had tripled from 1,100 to roughly 4,500.

## Kaiser and the Doctors

The Kaiser Permanente Medical Care Program—founded on Dr. Sidney Garfield's prepaid group practice model at Grand Coulee Dam in 1938 and expanded through the wartime shipyards—survived a decade of fierce AMA opposition, internal power struggles between Kaiser's corporate managers and the doctors, and persistent labor draft threats to become by Kaiser's death the nation's largest HMO, which Kaiser himself considered his most significant achievement.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-047"></span>**Dr. Sidney Garfield's prepaid health plan in California's Mojave Desert (1933) succeeded by aligning financial incentives toward prevention: by receiving a fixed fee per worker whether or not they were ill, Garfield profited from keeping workers healthy rather than treating sickness—the foundational insight that distinguished Kaiser Permanente from fee-for-service medicine and that Garfield articulated as: 'To the private physician, a sick person is an asset. To Permanente, a sick person is a liability.'**
    - Insurance broker Alonzo Ordway helped develop the fixed-fee arrangement with Industrial Indemnity that transformed Garfield's failing desert operation—which had 12-bed capacity and couldn't afford to pay staff for 7 months—into a financially viable enterprise.
    - At Grand Coulee, union leaders were so impressed with Garfield's expanded plan that they negotiated coverage for workers' dependents—charged at $.50/week per spouse and $.25/week per child—and workers' health improved dramatically as they learned to seek treatment before becoming seriously ill.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-048"></span>**The AMA's opposition to Kaiser Permanente was not primarily about medical quality but about economic self-interest and market control: local chapters threatened to expel doctors who joined Garfield, used medical licensing boards to place him on probation for technical violations, pressured draft boards to call up key KPMCP physicians during the Korean War, and attacked Garfield in Senate testimony—while their chief spokesman, Morris Fishbein, had not practiced medicine in thirty years.**
    - Army officers joked to Garfield: 'Wouldn't the AMA love to get Sid in the Army?'—acknowledging that draft pressure on KPMCP doctors was an AMA tactic rather than a military necessity.
    - A captain in the Army Medical Services Corps wrote Kaiser that Fishbein's influence amounted to 'a ruthless dictatorship in which he puts to shame the very dictatorships we are so actively fighting against… he practices medical autocracy.'
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-049"></span>**The 1955 Lake Tahoe conference resolved the bitter internal struggle between Kaiser's corporate managers and the KPMCP doctors: Kaiser had imposed Clifford Keene on Garfield as a management watchdog and effectively sidelined Garfield from day-to-day administration, while doctors accused him of 'lay domination'; the truce created a joint governance structure of equal doctor and corporate representation that became KPMCP's enduring organizational principle.**
    - The doctors drafted a statement to Edgar Kaiser in May 1955 charging that Henry Kaiser and his managers had engaged in 'a series of unilateral actions effecting lay domination' of the plan—the most direct internal challenge Kaiser faced in any of his enterprises.
    - "Kaiser's reply to the doctors was plaintive and acerbic: 'it is unfortunate that some of you appear to have failed to realize the full measure that others have given to make your present success possible.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-050"></span>**Kaiser consistently opposed federal health insurance while supporting government loans for private medical facilities—a position that alienated liberal Democratic allies but reflected his genuine belief that competitive private programs like KPMCP could reform national health care more effectively than government mandates, a view Edgar Kaiser later expressed to President Johnson when predicting that Medicare's fee-for-service payments would produce runaway costs.**
    - "Kaiser told Senator Wagner in 1944: 'I believe that if there could be a few more such examples [as KPMCP], there would be a rapid development of this new type of medical service which would surpass anything thus far developed in the United States or any other country.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
    - When Eisenhower's HEW Secretary Oveta Culp Hobby lectured Kaiser to pursue 'less strident tactics,' she was reflecting the AMA's successful preemption of her views—suggesting how effectively the organization had neutralized Kaiser's political allies.

## Boss

Kaiser's managerial style—intensely personal, contemptuous of hierarchy, relentless in pressure, gifted at identifying and developing talent, and dependent on direct communication rather than formal structure—produced spectacular results in production-oriented industries but struggled in consumer marketing, and ultimately exhausted a generation of managers who could not replicate his energy after his death.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-051"></span>**Kaiser's core management team—assembled largely before Six Companies and including Ordway, Bedford, Price, Havas, Rhoades, Trefethen, and Sherwood—owed their loyalty to his habit of retaining key men during slack periods, paying for their development, and giving them autonomy that other contractors denied; in return they gave decades of service and accepted physical demands that bordered on abuse.**
    - "Ordway recalled their first meeting: Kaiser 'applied his vacuum cleaner and learned more about me in the next hour than I knew about myself.' Ordway worked for Kaiser for more than half a century." —Alonzo B. Ordway
    - "Edgar Kaiser observed: 'He doesn't have just two sons, he has a thousand sons'—capturing the quasi-familial intensity Kaiser created among his inner circle of managers." —Edgar Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-052"></span>**Kaiser's aversion to organizational hierarchy was so strong that even after his empire expanded to dozens of companies, he resisted creating formal management structures—delegating authority through personal contact, telephone, and rapid-fire telegram rather than through flow charts, and resolving important decisions through informal ad-hoc conferences of his top seven or eight men.**
    - "When a Six Companies partner requested an organization chart, treasurer Sherwood replied: 'We have not attempted to keep [flow charts] up-to-date as carrying as many operations as we do it is difficult to keep personnel always concentrated for any length of time.'" —George G. Sherwood
    - His annual telephone bill reached $250,000 as early as 1942; Kaiser considered verbal communication a strategic investment and paper work an inefficient substitute.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-053"></span>**Kaiser demanded absolute personal integrity from himself and his employees—refusing to pay bribes even at competitive cost, personally outwitting a building inspector rather than pay a bribe, and famously teaching a salesman's supervisor (not the salesman) that responsibility for an under-the-table deal rested with the manager who had failed to instill proper values.**
    - When a Honolulu building inspector hinted at payment to overlook a six-inch zoning violation, Kaiser had workers cut off six inches and replaster the wall over the weekend; when the inspector returned Monday and found his measurements were off, Kaiser later told him he'd do the same 'a thousand times' before paying anyone at City Hall a nickel.
    - When a salesman's superior had not taught him the ethics of clean dealing, Kaiser 'went around the table again' and voted to keep the salesman—firing nobody, but ensuring everyone learned the lesson.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-054"></span>**The 1956 reorganization creating Kaiser Industries Corporation—driven by the need to provide Kaiser-Frazer stockholders partial recovery, use $76 million in tax-loss carryforwards, improve SEC access for future stock issues, and shake off the 'government parasite' image—marked the belated modernization of Kaiser's corporate structure, transforming a family-controlled conglomerate into a publicly traded holding company capable of accessing Wall Street capital.**
    - Financial advisor George Woods argued that creating a modern, publicly owned holding company would allow Kaiser to 'be taken seriously by other business leaders, to be accepted as members of the club'—an intangible goal that had motivated many of Kaiser's decisions since the 1940s.
    - "Edgar Kaiser later called the automobile venture's hard lessons 'the best thing that ever happened to us'—the failure had forced the reorganization that ultimately gave the organization financial credibility." —Edgar Kaiser

## Global Development and a Pacific Paradise

In his final two decades Kaiser Engineering tackled dams, steel plants, and mines on six continents while Kaiser himself built hotels, a new city, and media operations in Hawaii—demonstrating that his creativity accelerated rather than diminished with age, and leaving behind in Hawaii Kai, the Hawaiian Village, and the KHVH television station durable monuments to his restless, optimistic vision.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-055"></span>**Kaiser Engineers' $130 million contract to expand the Tata Iron Works in Jamshedpur, India (1955)—executed while Soviet engineers simultaneously built a government-owned plant nearby—inserted Kaiser directly into Cold War competition for Third World development, with his team training thousands of Indian craftsmen and engineers while reportedly enjoying freer social relations with local workers than the Soviet engineers maintained.**
    - Newsweek claimed that 'communists' tried to foment unrest but that the Americans impressed local observers by working beside native laborers in 125-degree heat: 'If an operator had trouble with a big shovel, a Kaiser engineer would leap aboard and give an impromptu lesson.'
    - As in Argentina, the Americans were primarily teachers: they hired experts to provide extensive orientation in Indian customs and traditions for Americans sent there, learning from their hosts even as they taught.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-056"></span>**The Akosombo Dam and aluminum plant in Ghana—Kaiser Engineers' largest African project—required Edgar Kaiser to navigate Kwame Nkrumah's Cold War manipulation and gradual authoritarianism through years of fraught negotiations (1959–1966), ultimately succeeding because Nkrumah, despite imprisoning domestic opponents, regarded Henry Kaiser with 'reverence' and deferred to the senior Kaiser for final decisions.**
    - Edgar Kaiser's experience with Nkrumah and other Third World leaders became an apprenticeship in realpolitik that led to his becoming a foreign affairs advisor to Presidents Kennedy and Johnson.
    - The Volta River project eventually cost over $200 million, underwritten chiefly by World Bank loans; Kaiser Engineers provided design and supervision for the dam and its power transmission network.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-057"></span>**Kaiser transformed Waikiki Beach by purchasing the ramshackle Niumalu Hotel in 1955, dredging a salt-water lagoon, trucking in 3,000 loads of sand, and building the Hawaiian Village in 89 days—pioneering aggressive tourism promotion including personal mainland sales calls with a slide projector, and ultimately selling the complex to Conrad Hilton for $21.5 million in 1961 when he was ready for the larger challenge of Hawaii Kai.**
    - Kaiser's construction violated building codes repeatedly and woke guests at 7 A.M. with pile drivers, but he defended each day cut from the schedule as more valuable than the complaints it generated.
    - By 1960 the hotel's twin seventeen-story Diamond Head Towers had brought capacity to over 1,000 rooms, and the hotel yielded a return of about 1–2 percent annually—modest financially but spectacular as a catalyst for Hawaiian tourism.
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-058"></span>**Kaiser's sponsorship of the television western Maverick (1957)—a $7 million bet opposed by 31 of his managers—made it the number-one rated show in America, demonstrating his instinct for spotting James Garner's star quality and correctly timing entry into Sunday night competition against Ed Sullivan; his subsequent failures with Hong Kong and Follow the Sun showed that one spectacular television success rarely translated into a formula.**
    - Robert Elliott claimed 'there were thirty-one votes against and one in favor: Kaiser himself'—one of many examples where Kaiser's intuition overrode consensus and proved right.
    - "When Maverick's quality deteriorated, Kaiser blasted Jack Warner and Bill Orr: 'You cannot sell us a Cadillac and deliver us a flivver.'" —Henry J. Kaiser

## The Sunset Years

Kaiser's final decade in Hawaii—marked by boat racing championships, the Hawaii Kai city project, ongoing corporate oversight, a happy marriage to Ale, and gradual physical decline—revealed a man who never truly retired, who worked twelve-hour days into his mid-eighties, and whose last conversations with his grandson still focused on the future rather than the past.

* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-059"></span>**Ale Kaiser—the nurse who had cared for Bess during her final illness and whom Henry married less than a month after Bess died in March 1951—was well matched to him: she actively supported the health care mission, helped domesticate his habits, and maintained a household full of young people and celebrities that gave his final years warmth and social richness, while their union, initially scandalous, proved deeply satisfying to both.**
    - "Kaiser explained his hasty remarriage: 'I haven't time to do what the average person does, to slide around corners…. This is a constructive move, and in sixty days I will be a sweeter, simpler, more useful person because of it.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
    - "Ale told Hancock: 'I've got to stay one step ahead of Mr. Kaiser and keep him guessing'—characterizing the dynamic of a marriage where both partners were strong-willed and mutually stimulating." —Ale Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-060"></span>**Hawaii Kai—Kaiser's planned city of 75,000 east of Honolulu on Bishop Estate property, begun in 1959—was his last great building project: chronically undersold in its early years, transformed by the inclusion of 28 outside developers after 1963, and reaching 1,000 units sold by December 1965, it proved Kaiser's instinct that infrastructure investment would precede and enable residential growth even when associates saw only mud and mosquitoes.**
    - Hancock asked Kaiser why he was beginning new sections when construction was far ahead of demand; Kaiser replied that he had only a few more years to live and feared 'that his successors might reject phases of the master plan which were but ideas and drawings.'
    - "The Bishop Estate trustees sent a letter stating that any further action could be taken only 'at your own personal risk'; Kaiser grinned and told Hancock: 'They've given me permission to do anything I want out here without their approval—so long as I do it at my own risk!'" —Henry J. Kaiser
* <span id="henry-j-kaiser-builder-in-the-modern-american-west-061"></span>**Kaiser's health declined gradually from a 1956 heart attack forward—he regularly defied doctors, left hospitals against advice, and flew back to Hawaii in an oxygen tent still conducting personnel discussions—until he slipped into a coma in late August 1967 and died on August 24 at his Portlock estate, with his last recorded conversation still focused on business problems and future opportunities.**
    - "James McCloud saw Kaiser for the last time in Oakland about two months before he died, confined to a wheelchair but 'bright as a new dollar'; Kaiser looked at McCloud and said: 'My, aren't you lucky—you've got so many problems.'" —Henry J. Kaiser
    - "President Johnson's condolence telegram stated: 'Henry J. Kaiser embodied in his own career all that has been best in our country's tradition. His own energy, imagination and determination gave him greatness—and he used that greatness to give unflaggingly for the betterment of his country and his fellow man.'" —Lyndon B. Johnson
